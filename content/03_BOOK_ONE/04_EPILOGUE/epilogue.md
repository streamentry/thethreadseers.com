## THE ELEVEN MINUTES

She had got very good at the eleven minutes and she had never once been able to explain it to anybody, and the two people she tried hardest to explain it to — Eli Park, in a letter from a car park in Lyon, and Zara Washington, on a telephone from Alexandria — had both written back the same thing, in different words, and she had framed both letters and hung them in a room four thousand miles away.

Eli Park's said: *I think it is because you are the only person in the building who is not looking at a number. I have watched you do it four times now and every time somebody reads a figure you wait until they have finished and then you say one sentence. I think you learned it from your grandmother counting your breaths and I think you learned the *reason* from a woman in a corridor in eleven minutes, in October, who spent all eleven of them apologising for things that were not her fault. You are extremely rude to figures. It is your best quality.*

Zara Washington's said: *You are not calm. I want to be extremely clear that I can feel what you actually are from eleven hundred miles away through a bad telephone line, and what you actually are is a person having an enormous opinion about a room, continuously, at a volume you have trained yourself to keep under your breath. It is not calm. It is containment. Those are different and you have been pretending they are the same for two years and the pretending is what the eleven minutes is.*

Lyra Chen wrote back and said that both of them were wrong, and that Eli was right about the numbers, and that Zara was right about everything else, and that she had known Zara was right since about December and had not said so because she was eighteen and it was convenient.

---

The thing that is not finished came to her in a corridor, in October 2015, at about eleven o'clock in the morning, and it came to her in the form of a *scheduled* item, which is the only form in which anything in that building has ever arrived that she did not already know about.

There was a girl on the second floor doing a practical, and the girl was fifteen, and she was doing the eleven minutes.

That was the whole of it. That was the entire discovery, and it took Lyra Chen four and a half seconds, standing on a stair landing, and what she felt was not recognition, because the girl was not like her, and the girl did not resemble anybody she had known. What she felt was *vertigo*, of a kind she had only felt twice before, both times in that building, both times in a corridor, and both times immediately before a great deal of trouble.

The girl was fifteen and she was doing the eleven minutes, and Lyra Chen stood on a stair landing and understood with total clarity, in about four seconds, that **it is learnable**.

That is the horror. That is the entire horror of the second half of her life, and she has said so, in print, more than once, and it is the sentence she is most quoted for and the one she most wishes she had not said:

> *It is not a gift and it is not a gift, and I have said that in eleven countries and I am going to say it again. It is not rare. It is not special. It is a* skill, *and it is taught, and it is taught badly, and it is taught to children of fifteen by teachers who have been told by a doctor that it is not a gift because a doctor in a corridor in September needed a number, and the number was wrong, and the number went into a system, and the system has been running for two years, and it has produced eleven children in two countries who can sit in a room and hold their nerve and say one thing at the end of it, and every single one of those eleven children is going to be offered something.*

She went down the stairs and said it out loud to a person of twenty-six in a corridor, and it took four minutes, and the person of twenty-six was a former student of hers who had a schedule and a supervisor and a small amount of money and a great deal of charm, and who was, at that moment, eleven days into a nine-year plan to go and sit in a room in a foreign city with a folder and a napkin.

"I am not asking you not to do it," she said. "I did it. I have the board. I want you to understand exactly what you are being told and exactly how little I am asking."

"Then what are you asking?"

"I am asking you to look at the item number on the sheet you are going to hand in, and to notice whether anybody has asked the girl what she would like to be called on a form."

## THE LETTER

It came in the last week of November 2015, to a house on Hậu Giang Street, and it was forwarded by a solicitor in Lyon. The girl who sent it was fifteen, and she had found the address on a form in a building in Westbrook and had had the extraordinary luck, or the extraordinary *sense*, to ask somebody in that building whether the address was right, and had been given the address by a woman of eighteen who worked at a desk and who had thought about it for four days before she did it.

It was nine lines, and not one of the nine was hers.

The words were a man's. He had had twenty-four days in a good room and a bad back, and he had been working through them one at a time, and he had written nine lines, and he could not send them, because every route to that address ran through an institution that had a file on him and would have opened it. So he had asked a fifteen-year-old to write them out for him, and he had taught her the hand first, which took four days, and the hand was the only part of the letter that was hers. It was in a hand that had been taught to write by a man with silver scars up both forearms and a limp he does not entirely have and did, in 2013, have entirely.

> *I did not read your book. I am not going to. I have four days and I am going to spend them on the fourth line, so I will write the other three first so that they are out of the way.*
>
> *1. I was wrong about a great many things and I have had twenty-four days in a good room and a bad back to work through them and I have got to three of them and I am not going to write down the other forty-one because the minutes exist and Ms. Rowntree keeps them and I have asked her for a copy of the whole file and she has said yes, which I did not expect, and I am going to read it in January when she sends it and it is eleven hundred pages.*
>
> *2. You were right, in August, in a demonstration hall, about the four tenths of a second, and nobody in this building has ever said so out loud except a sixteen-year-old with a napkin, and I would like somebody to have said it to me, so: you were right, and it was the only true thing anybody said to me that day, and I was in the middle of doing the other thing at the time.*
>
> *3. I have read the register of case forty-one. All of it. It took two evenings and I had to stop on the first one and take something for it, which I want recorded because I have spent twenty-four days finding out that the reason I could not sleep was not grief.*
>
> *4. I want to write to you about the eleventh of April 2002. There is a map in the third volume and it is not what I thought it was for eleven years and I have had it in a drawer in a good room and I have taken it out, and I would like to tell you what I have found, and I would like to know whether you would want to know, and I am asking rather than assuming, which is a thing I have been told I have never once done in my life and which I am going to start on a Tuesday in a house in another country with eleven lines and no heading.*
>
> *Do not reply to this address.*

---

She replied to the solicitor in Lyon.

Four lines, and the fourth one was not an answer to the question, and she has never explained that to anybody, and the letter is in a tin in a kitchen on Ellery Street with three pieces of red silk in it.

> *Item 4: yes. I would want to know. I am eighteen and I have no gift and I have spent a month standing on a stair landing understanding that a thing I learned at fifteen is being taught, and I would like to know who taught it, and I would like to know whether they were taught, or whether they were found, and I think those are different, and I think it is the whole of the difference and I think it is worth an island nobody has looked at.*

She did not, in the end, go to the island.

She went, in the end, to a house on a street in a city of eleven million people, to a room nine feet by eleven, to nine years, and she was there in March 2016 and she is there still, and there are eleven students in the room and a waiting list of forty and three old women and a well, and the well is not a *nexus* and the wall is in the wrong place and the smell of turpentine has got into the mortar and will, according to a conservator from Lyon, outlast everything else in the building.

*2017.*

And there is a map in a tin, and there are nine lines in a letter, and there is an island that does not appear on most charts, and the charts that have it disagree with each other about its position, and nobody has been there since a woman of thirty-two stood under a stone archway on the eleventh of April 2002 and tried to work out where a thread dimension *converges* rather than where anybody had decided to count it.

And in a room in a valley, there is a board, and on the board there is a number, and the number is not a severity, and the number has been eleven since 2013, and nobody has worked out yet whether eleven is a good number or a bad one, and a man of twenty has written on the margin of the published figure, in pencil, and left it in:

> *Ask somebody. I asked. It took four years. It is not the same as the number going down and I would like somebody in this building to stop suggesting that it is. I said the same thing at sixteen and nobody heard me then either. — E.P.*
