## SIXTY-SEVEN

The thread-burn had been at sixty-one on Thursday and at sixty-seven at three o'clock this morning and it was at sixty-nine when he put his sleeve back down over it.

The rule was simple and he had written it down in nineteen ninety-four and had never broken it: eighty is cardiac involvement. He had said that number out loud, in a corridor, at three in the morning, to a woman who was taking notes, and he had said it because he wanted it written down, and he had been annoyed for eleven years about wanting it written down.

He had nineteen days, at that rate, and he was aware that the rate was not a constant and had not been a constant since roughly Tuesday.

He got up to the Observatory because the Observatory was the only room in the building with a telescope in it, and because a man at seventy per cent does not sleep, and because there is a particular kind of tiredness that comes from lying still.

The morning was coming up grey off the roof of the Hall of Records and there was still a haze over the courtyard where the archway had been, the better part of two days on, and if he looked at the wrong part of it he could see the shape of what had fallen.

He had looked at it every morning since the eleventh and he had got very good at it.

## WHAT HE SAW

Lyra Chen's window was on the fourth floor of the east dormitory and at ten past six she was not in it.

He had learned, over four days, a great deal about her, and he was aware that a large part of what he had learned had come from the fact that she had no idea she was being watched, which is the most useful condition a person can be in.

She was not sleeping. She had not slept more than fifty minutes at a time since Wednesday and he had the numbers, because the numbers were on the same feed as the numbers, and because he had built the feed.

That was a real thing and he would not pretend otherwise later, in the room where he said the true things. He had built a system that told him where a sixteen-year-old was and how long she had been asleep and how fast her heart was going, and he had built it in order to protect a girl, and the protection had never once required the numbers, and he had looked at them anyway, every day, and had felt the ordinary specific shame of a man reading something he had no right to read.

She was the only person in this building doing what she ought to be doing.

That was the finding he had not been ready for. He had come into the eleventh of September with a *candidate* — a tone, an index entry, a reference frequency in a box — and he had been in the building four days and had watched Zara stand up in a hall of two hundred and eleven people and refuse to accept a number without a unit, and had not at the time known which of the two of them he was watching, and had watched a professor of mathematics stand up in a corridor afterwards and say so in public, and had watched a first-year boy with a bad shoulder go and ask a registrar about a Council resolution because he had decided that it was her job and not his.

He had told himself on the sixteenth of November, two thousand and twelve, in a document he has a copy of, that the arithmetic was trivial. It had never once occurred to him to check what she would *do*.

## THE FILE

The Syndicate's offer had come up the stairs at nine o'clock the previous evening and it had been this, in writing, in a hand he had never been able to trace:

*Site report 14/09 corrects on receipt of instruction. No survivor. No splice. No medical protocol. Standard.*

Four lines. It would have taken the site team four hours.

He had sat with it from nine until midnight and at midnight he had written back three words, which were *not required thank you*, and he had understood while writing them that he was not refusing it for Kenji Nakamura.

He was refusing it because of the eleven seconds in the corridor in nineteen ninety-eight.

*I'm sorry. Do I know you?*

If he took the four lines, then on the twenty-seventh of November, two thousand and twenty-six, there would be a boy of twenty-one in Portland who was not in a record, whose sister was not in a record, and who would spend the rest of his life with a permanent bridge in his chest that nobody had authorised and no doctor in the world could remove, and who would not be able to find the paper that said *he did this to me*.

Harlow had had his own taken. He had spent the last eleven days deciding that he would not take another, and he had decided it at midnight, alone, having calculated that the boy in the crater was worth less to him than the girl in the dormitory by an enormous margin.

He wrote that down too. It is page four of the file.

## SEVEN IN THE MORNING

The tab on his bench at ten past seven said *draw schedule — live, 15/09 14:00 to 18/09 14:00* and *subject: see 43/41* and he looked at it for a while, and then he opened the drawer and took out the paper crane and put it in his breast pocket, and then he did the thing he had done on every working day for sixteen years and which had a ritual to it, which was to put the photograph face down and not open the box.

Then he stopped with his hand on the lid, and he opened it again, and he took out the photograph, and he looked at the girl in it, who was six, and whose jaw was his own.

She was twenty-three. She had her mother's hands. She was on a bus. She was showing thread sensitivity, unconfirmed, untrained, and entirely alone with it, and a great many kind people had been telling her for four years that she was unwell, and the only thing Marcus Harlow had to offer her in exchange for what he was about to do with a girl in the east dormitory was the truth.

He put the photograph back, face down.

In the anteroom, outside the door he had not opened, somebody had left a tray with four mugs on it, and the four mugs were the kind the Academy gave you at a coffee morning, and there was a piece of paper under it in a fist of capitals: *WE HEARD THE WHOLE BUILDING. WE ARE ALL RIGHT. WE ARE GOING TO THE REGISTRAR AT EIGHT. DO NOT COME DOWN.*

It was in Harlow's handwriting. It was a note he had written at eight o'clock in the morning on the fourteenth, yesterday, and left on his own bench, and someone had picked it up and put it on this tray and left it where he would find it.

He read it twice.

Then he took the crane back out of his pocket, and looked at the grey creases, and said, out loud, in an empty room, in a tone that was not steady:

"No."

And then he said, immediately afterwards, and he heard himself say it, and he wrote it on page four as well:

"But I am going anyway, and it is because of that note, and I want it on the record that a child wrote *we are all right* on a piece of paper in a drawer yesterday and that is the reason I am going to do this, and that if I had read it at the time I would not have."

He sat down and put his sleeve back over the arm and looked at the schedule, and at seven in the morning on the fifteenth of September, Marcus Harlow began the seven days in which he would tell himself that the thing he was doing was almost over.

## THE SAME MORNING, FOUR HUNDRED METRES AWAY

*Sunday 15 September, 06:20. Academy clinic. Lyra Chen's handset, which she has not looked at in nineteen hours.*

Dr. Osei had not telephoned the dormitory. She had telephoned her own registrar, which was a thing Lyra only learned about in December, and the reason she had not telephoned the dormitory was that there was a bell on the fourth bed and Maya Sandoval had been making noise all night, and Dr. Osei had decided at about four o'clock that the amount of noise one floor could make was a finite resource and she was not spending it on a first-year who was asleep for the first time in four days.

So the message came at seven twelve, to a handset on the table in the common room, and it said this, and Lyra read it twice, and the second time was worse than the first:

> *CHEN J. — second deterioration, 14/09 04:40. Confusion, intermittent, disoriented to time x3. Two memory gaps documented by bedside staff, both retrograde, both concerning to me. I am not going to telephone you to ask whether your gift is responsible, because it is not, and because a doctor who asks that question on a Saturday morning is asking it in order to be able to write it down later.*

> *He asked me for a pen at six and he wrote four words on the pad and asked me whether I had read them, and I had not, and I read them, and I told him the truth, and he said "no, but read them again," and I read them again, and he said "no, read them until they are true."*
