## WHAT WAS IN THE NOTE

Four of them, one working arm, one arm in a cardigan, and a sheet of paper on the table with a list on it that was the entire product of the worst night of four people's lives.

"We do not go to the Headmistress," Eli said.

He had it propped on the table at an angle that was going to hurt him by ten o'clock and he did not adjust it.

"Explain that, because we spent four hours in a basement and the Headmistress has a light on."

"The Headmistress has a light on at five in the morning, which is either a crisis or a *schedule*, and we do not know which, and if it is a schedule then somebody in this building is meeting the Headmistress at five in the morning about something they do not want in the minutes." He put his pen down. "Worse than that. Do you know what a Headmistress is? She is a *signatory*. That is what she is. She can suspend a professor and she can open an inquiry and she cannot authorise a human-subject protocol, and if I walk in there with a photograph and a signature I am asking her to spend the one credit she has, and she will have to spend it on the *inquiry* and not on us, and she will have to do it in writing, and it will take eleven days."

"Eleven days," said Milo.

"Eleven days is fine. Eleven days is nothing." Eli almost smiled. "The point is not the eleven days. The point is that the letter goes from me, a first-year with a bad arm, to a member of the Council, and the *only* route it can take is through this office, and this office is Security, and Security is a person, and the person is *Blackwood*."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside the window the courtyard was still grey and there was a crane on it, and the crane had been there for four days.

"So we do not use the office," said Zara.

"We use the office," said Eli, "and we do it in a way that leaves a mark, and that is what I want to argue about for the next twenty minutes, because it is the only decision we have got and everybody in this room wants a different one and I would like us to do it as an argument rather than as a mood."

## THE ARGUMENT

Zara wanted them to warn the students. Not the Council, not Weaver — the *students*, in the refectory, by name, with the photograph, today, at lunch.

"They will believe me," she said. "Not about the machine. About *him*. If I stand up in front of four hundred people and say that what happened on Saturday night was done to us on purpose, and that it is going to happen again, and that it is a person and not a fault — they will believe that. I have never in my life had that and I am not throwing it away to go and write to a committee."

"That is not a small thing," said Lyra. "That is the biggest thing anybody has said all week. Say why it isn't the plan."

"Because in four hours, every adult in this building will know who said it, and every one of them will have a reason it was wrong, and the *reason it was wrong* is the only part anybody will remember." Zara put both hands flat on the table. "Lyra. If it goes wrong it goes wrong on my name. I am not doing that to Eli."

"Nobody's name is going to—"

"It is absolutely going to be somebody's name, and it is going to be in a file, and in a month there is going to be an inquiry, and at that inquiry somebody is going to ask *why did the first-year with the gift tell four hundred people in a refectory* and I will have to answer it, and the answer will be *because I wanted it to be my fault*." She took her hands off. "I have been the one who wanted that since Wednesday. I am putting it down."

Milo wanted to go to the press. There was no press. What he wanted, it turned out, was to *go outside* — to the gate, to the road, to the eight hundred metres of public footpath where the visitors' cars were, and to stand on it.

"There are forty-one people in the car park from the exchange programme," he said, "and the bus is at nine, and if a coach of forty adults sees four teenagers on a road at eight o'clock in the morning with photocopies, that is not a leak. That is a *witness*, and you cannot put a witness back in a box."

"That gets us killed," said Eli.

"That gets the *file* out of this building."

"That gets the file out of this building and Ms. Sandoval in the fourth bed out of that building too, because if four first-years can get onto a public road then nobody is ever going to believe a medical ward again." Eli was not raising his voice. He was doing something worse, which was being completely reasonable. "Milo. I have been awake for thirty hours and I am not going to insult you by pretending I have not thought about exactly that, and I have rejected it, and here is the actual reason, which is not caution: a coach of forty adults who see four teenagers is a photograph in a newspaper, and a photograph in a newspaper is *a story about four teenagers*, and this is not a story about four teenagers. I have written that sentence down four times in the last two days and I keep writing it because it is the thing I am worst at and I am best at."

"And if the letter goes nowhere?"

"Then it goes nowhere," said Eli, "and we do it anyway, and we do it in a way that leaves a mark, because a mark is the only thing that has ever worked in this building."

## THE MARK

He wrote it out, and it took forty minutes, and here it is in his own hand, and it is two hundred and thirty-three words long, and the second paragraph is the one that matters:

> *To the Council of Threadweaver Academy, from four first-year students, and we are giving you our names because we understand that an unsigned thing can be removed.*
>
> *On 14 September, at approximately 23:47, the Restricted Archives on the lower level registered a dimensional-incident alert and the file "Dimensional Incidents — 1997" was opened. On 15 September, between 03:00 and 04:00, the campus containment protocol was activated for a period of not less than one hour, and in that period the corridors of this building produced physical phenomena consistent with the phenomena described in the report of the Kyoto Collapse of 11 April 1997.*
>
> *We hold four photographs, three sheets of a document numbered 41 in a series whose page 43 is not in our possession, and the signature and date of a Council officer on an authorisation dated 11 June 2009. We will lodge all of it with the Clerk of this Council and with the Registrar of this Academy, in two separate places, at 08:00 today. We are not asking for anything. We are telling you where it is.*
>
> *We are aware that this letter names a person. We are naming him on purpose. We have thought about it for four days and we have decided that a person who has done these things should be named by people he cannot reach, in a building he controls, before the day he intended.*

Eli read it out loud twice and then said, "That last paragraph is stupid."

"That last paragraph is the whole letter," said Lyra.

"That last paragraph is going to be the only reason anybody gets convicted and it is going to be the only thing that gets the four of us thrown out of this Academy." He put the pen down. "Read it again and tell me it is not stupid."

"It's stupid," said Lyra, "and we are sending it."

They sent it at twenty-one minutes past seven from the terminal in the west corridor, addressed to the Clerk of the Council for onward transmission, with a copy to the Registrar of this Academy, by name, and the copy is the part that mattered, and none of the four of them knew that yet.

Eli hit send and then sat back and looked at the ceiling.

"That's the first time I've ever sent anything to anybody," he said.

## WHAT HAPPENED TO IT

Here is what happened to it, and the four of them learned all of it over the following nine days, and none of it was reported to them directly; they reconstructed it out of dates on documents that other people had signed.

The terminal logged the message at 07:21 on 15 September. Every terminal in the building logs to Security. This is not a secret and is not presented as one; it is in the student handbook, on page four, under *conduct*.

Security received it at 07:21. The Chief read it at 07:38, which Eli established from a read-receipt on the copy addressed to the Registrar, which the Registrar's office was obliged to return.

The copy to the Registrar arrived, and Dr. Mei-Hua Chen read it at eight o'clock the next morning, and it is the reason a woman who had known there was a machine under her for eleven years was in her office at eight in the morning on the sixteenth with four photographs and a boy's bad arm and a written list of everything they did not have.

The copy to the Council did not, for eleven hours.

At 11:41 that morning the Council minute authorising a live draw on the seventeen campus collectors — a minute that had been passed at 18:00 the previous evening, in a room with two observers in it — was filed, and the version that went into the minute book carried a marginal note in the Security Chief's hand, which read: *forwarded to Prof. Harlow, 11:40, per standing instruction.*

The four of them did not learn that until the Inquiry, in December, in a room with a recording running, and by then it did not matter to anybody, which was, in the end, the whole of what they had wanted.

## THE NOTE ON THE BACK

The last page of Lyra's notebook for the fifteenth of September is a list, and it is in her hand, and it is short, and it is the thing she actually decided that day:

1. *The letter is in. It has a time on it.*
2. *Milo is right that a mark is the only thing that has ever worked in here and he is wrong about the road and I do not know yet why he is wrong.*
3. *Zara put her name down. I did not put mine down. That is a thing to look at later.*
4. *Eli will not sleep today and will not say so and I am going to have to notice it myself.*
5. *There is a woman forty feet above me who had a light on at five in the morning and I did not knock.*
6. *A boy of fourteen came out of that building yesterday and the Registrar got him out in eleven minutes and nobody had to be brave. A girl of fifteen went into it on the eleventh and has not come out, and there is no margin on her at all, and both of those are true at the same time in the same corridor and nobody has said so out loud.*

She did not write down what any of them were going to do next. She had been going to, four times, and each time she had stopped, because the last time she had written down what she was going to do next she had been fifteen and she had written *ask Dad what to do* and had not done it, and had gone into a hospital alone.

So she did not write it, and at nineteen minutes past seven that morning the notice came under the door, and it was slid rather than pushed, which she noticed, and which she has never worked out.
