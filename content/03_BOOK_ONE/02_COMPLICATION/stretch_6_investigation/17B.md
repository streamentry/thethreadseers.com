*Saturday 14 September, 23:52. The Restricted Archives, and then the stairwell.*

## WHAT THE WALL DID

It did not come down the way Lyra had imagined things coming down, which was the problem, because she had spent four years imagining things coming down and she had a whole vocabulary for it.

The stairwell rearranged itself.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. One second there was a landing with a lamp on it and a window at the bottom, and the next second there was still a landing and a lamp and a window, and the window was nine feet higher, and the flight they were on had become two flights with a turn in the middle that had not been there.

"Protocol," said Milo. "The campus protocol. That is not the Maze, that is the *protocol*, the Maze is the building in the courtyard and this is the whole *building*—"

"I know what it is." Eli had his phone out and it was photographing, and his hands were completely steady, and his face was not. "Forty-one. Forty-three. I have the authorisation and forty-three and the two photographs. That is what I have. Everything else in this room is the Academy's."

"Lyra." Zara was on the floor. "Lyra, the light—"

The emergency lighting came up red. It came up *everywhere*, all at once, in a building with four floors and eleven hundred people in it, and the whole campus went the colour of a photograph of a fire.

And then the corridor began to make sounds, and the sounds were people.

---

## THE HALL

Milo got them into the archive corridor, which was the wrong door, and then into a stairwell that went down, which was worse, and Lyra got them stopped at a landing and made them sit down, and none of them wanted to, and she made them anyway.

"Four things," she said. "One, we do not split up, because the Maze works on people who are alone and I do not know that, I am guessing, but I am not gambling. Two, Eli, phone in your pocket, the photograph is taken, you do not need it again tonight. Three, we are going to be slower than we want to be. Four—"

She had not got to four.

Something came round the corner above them.

It was not a person. That was the thing none of them had been ready for and it was the thing she would remember for the rest of her life: it was shaped like a person and it was about the size of a person and it was made of the *space* where a person would be, and it was walking, and it was walking the way people walk in a corridor at two in the morning when they know where they are going.

Eli Park made a sound that was not a word.

"Threads," said Lyra. "That is threads. That is a bundle of threads at about standing height, and it is moving, and it is *aimed*—"

Zara was screaming.

The screaming was the worst part and it was the part that Lyra kept afterwards: not the thing, but the fact that it worked, that it was designed to, that somebody in this building had built a thing that made a person's fear into a shape and then walked it at them down a corridor.

"Zara. *Zara.* Listen to me—"

"There is — I can *feel*—" She was on her knees with her hands over her ears. "I can feel how much it's *getting*—"

"I know. I know. That is the point of it, that is what it is *for*, and it is not going to work, because being frightened of it is not the same as being alone, and we are not alone, and you are Zara Washington and you stood up in a lecture hall in front of two hundred and eleven people on Tuesday and made a professor of mathematics answer a question."

Zara stopped screaming.

She said, in a very small voice: "I can still feel it."

"I know. Move anyway."

---

## THE STAIRWELL

They lost forty-nine minutes in the stairwell. Lyra could tell afterwards, because the infirmary board and the clock on the wall of the transit office and three separate statements all said 23:52 to about 00:41, and because Milo had kept a count, out loud, of the landings, on the grounds that somebody had to, and because counting was the only thing any of them could do that did not involve being afraid.

They went down eleven landings. They came back up nine. They went down four more and arrived at a floor whose number was painted on the wall in a hand she recognised from the register, and which was three floors *above* where they had started.

The thing came past them twice.

The first time it went by without stopping and Lyra understood that it was not hunting, it was *sweeping*, and that there was a rhythm in the sweeping, and that the rhythm had a gap in it.

The second time, it stopped.

"It has seen us," said Eli.

"It has seen *Zara*," said Lyra.

Because Zara was the one who was making the sound. It was not a scream any more. It was a continuous low sound, the sound of somebody whose fear has become an engine, and it was coming out of her at a rate that made the red light on the walls shimmer, and the thing in the corridor had turned towards it the way a dog turns towards a noise.

"Zara. I need you to do something I am going to be ashamed to ask for."

"If you say *calm down*—"

"I am going to ask you to be frightened of the right thing." Lyra got down on the landing beside her, so they were both on the floor, which made her own terror much harder to hold and she did it anyway. "You are frightened of that thing. It is real and it is coming. Now be frightened of *me*, because I am going to touch your face, and I am going to do the thing my grandmother taught me on Wednesday in a hall, and it is a communion gesture and it is not a control technique, and it works in a room, and it is the only thing in this building that I have ever been taught that I would bet money on."

Zara looked at her.

"Do it," she said.

Lyra put her hands on either side of Zara Washington's face, and did the pattern, and it was not magic and it did not feel like magic, it felt like *asking*, and the cold of the stairwell went out of Zara's face in about nine seconds and the sound stopped.

The thing in the corridor dissolved.

"He'll come back," said Zara.

"Yes."

"Then why did you—"

"Because he was harvesting you and I interrupted the harvesting, and you are still frightened, and both of those are true at once, and we have got to keep moving." Lyra took her hands off. "Eli. It's gone. Now move, and do not stop to write anything down."

## THE BURN

The fourth landing they came up was not a landing.

Lyra got there first and put her left hand flat on the wall to stop and the wall was not stone.

It was the black-silver thread, laid into the stonework, the whole surface of it, the way Harlow laid his wards over a boy in a fold, and it did not hurt her at first — it *read* her, fast, the way you read a page, and it read her and found what it was looking for and it took hold and it did what it did to the boy from Kumasi in ninety seconds and it had her for four.

Zara hit her. Not her arm — her shoulder, with the flat of both hands, at full weight, which was the single most useful thing anybody did for Lyra Chen that month and which nobody has ever properly thanked her for.

Lyra came off the wall and went down onto the boards and the skin on her left forearm had gone the colour and the texture of a page that has been in a fire and it did not hurt at all, which she understood about eleven seconds later to be a very bad sign.

"Left arm," said Eli.

"I know."

"You are not going to be able to use it."

"I *know*, Eli—"

"Water," said Milo. "There is a drinks fountain on every floor of this building because I have been here ten days and I have already drunk from all of them—"

---

## THE LOSS

They made the ground floor at twenty to one.

The corridor outside the Restricted Archives had a ceiling that was not a ceiling any more. It had come down in one piece, and it had come down *across* the archive doorway, and it had come down at some point in the previous eight minutes while they were floors above it in a stairwell going the wrong way.

Eli Park got his phone out and looked at the forty photographs on it and then put it away, and did not say anything, and then said, to nobody:

"Forty-one and forty-three are gone. The authorisation, the margin, the schedules, the two of us in the garden. All of it."

"The photographs," said Lyra.

"Four photographs. Three sheets of forty-one. None of forty-three. None of the margin." He was not shaking. "I have got his handwriting saying *she will be* and I have got a signature and a date, and I have got a photograph of a woman at a wall, and I have got us. That's the whole of it. That's what four of us are taking out of this building."

"And the original," said Lyra.

"Under the stair," said Eli.

They went back for it. It took six minutes and it was the stupidest thing any of them ever did and Lyra has said so herself, in print, at length, for years. They went back down a stairwell in a maze for the *original* of a book, because it was her mother's book, and because Eli said — correctly, and it is the last useful thing he ever said in that building before his shoulder — that a photocopy of a thing is a report of a thing, and a report can be said to be a fabrication, and the original is the original.

They got two-thirds of the way down.

The floor of the stairwell at that point was the *inside* of the collapse and the middle of it was the hole, and the hole was eight feet across and there was a draught coming up out of it that smelled of cold stone and nothing else, and Eli Park went over it.

He went over it sideways, in the way a man goes over a fence, and his left arm went into it and his shoulder came out of the socket on the sound of it, and he made no sound at all except a short breath, and Lyra caught him by the collar with her right hand because her left arm was not available.

It is in the record that the original of Lin Ho Chen's working journal was destroyed on the fourteenth of September, in a stairwell, under four hundred tons of the Academy's own roof. It is also in the record — Lyra insisted on this, and fought a Registrar about it for nine days — that the reason it was not carried out is that it had been on a reading table three floors down, and that the reading table was three floors down *because of a decision taken four hours earlier in a basement*, and not because anybody had been careless.

That is in the record. The Registrar wrote it down. It is on page eleven.

---

## THE LAST SIX MINUTES

They were outside the library at nine minutes past one with Eli's shoulder out, Lyra's arm ruined, Zara shaking, and Eli Park refusing to be helped down a step, and they were in an Academy in which two hundred and eleven people had been in a lecture hall on Thursday and in which, at that moment, somebody had the machine that had done this.

Milo sat down on the bottom step and put his good arm on his knee and said, to the four of them:

"So. I put us in a stairwell."

"Yes," said Lyra.

"It was four numbers and four addresses. I said that." He put his face in his good hand for a second and then took it out again, because he was sixteen and he was going to remember that he had said it. "It was still a good idea."

"It was a good idea," said Lyra.

"Okay." He looked up. "Then what's the plan now, because it is one in the morning and I have not got anything."

"I have," said Eli, from the step below, with his arm held against his chest by Zara with the collar of his own coat. "Four of us, four numbers. And the address for number two is the Headmistress, and the address for number three is not a person, it is a *register*, and Council resolutions have to be minuted inside seventy-two hours or they lapse, and it is Monday."

"Which is what day?"

"Sunday," said Zara.

"Then it is Monday tomorrow." Eli got his breath. "I'll go to the Registrar. She is a registrar and it is her job and she is not in the machine. Eli Park is not important enough to be in the machine."
