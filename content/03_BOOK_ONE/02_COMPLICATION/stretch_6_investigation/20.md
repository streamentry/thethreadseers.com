# Chapter 20: The Trap Springs

*Sunday 15 September, 08:00. Demonstration Hall Seven.*
*Weave-Quake Counter: 54% instability. Node-failure projection: T+13 d. The reference dataset was destroyed in the Maze collapse of the tenth of September; this is the Council's re-run on partial data, and nobody has said out loud that it is a guess.*

## THE SUMMONS

The message arrived at dawn, slipped under dormitory doors while the Academy slept. Official Academy letterhead, Headmistress Weaver's seal, urgent red borders:

*Emergency Assembly - All First-Year Thread Students*
*Demonstration Hall Seven - 8:00 AM Sharp*
*Mandatory Attendance - No Exceptions*
*Subject: Critical Thread Stability Protocols*

Lyra stared at the notice, the jade pendant cold against her skin. After their discoveries in the archives, after witnessing Harlow's alliance with Blackwood, every official summons felt like a trap.

But it was more than that.

Dr. Ekwueme had called at four that morning, from Westbrook, and had not slept. The scan had come back. What the doctors now called *a cardiomyopathy that has been quietly getting worse for probably two years* had a name on it at last, and the name had a number attached: thirty-one percent of the heart muscle dead.

*Your father's heart is failing*, Ekwueme had said. *That is not the thing your gift is doing. I want to be very clear, Lyra, because the two have been written on top of each other for weeks and I do not want them written on top of each other any longer.*

And then, because she was a doctor and not a liar, she had said the rest of it.

*The bleed is real. It is costing him. But on its own it would have given him years, not weeks. The thing that stopped his heart this week was a muscle that was already gone. And a man with a stent and sixty-nine percent of a heart is not a man with a prognosis in days. He is a man with a prognosis in months.* A pause. The sound of a woman choosing her next words carefully, and Lyra had loved her for it. *Three to nine. If he is very lucky and very well-looked-after and does not do anything heroic.*

*I am going to do something heroic,* Lyra had said, into the phone, at four in the morning, in the dark.

*I know, love. I have read your file. That is what I am frightened of.*

Lyra stood in the corridor with the notice in her hand and the word *months* going round and round inside her like a coin in a drum.

It did not help. That was the thing she had not been ready for. Six years of *something is wrong with Dad* had taught her one shape of feeling, and that feeling did not have a slot marked *months*. Months was slower. Months was a thing you could get used to. The horror of it was that she could almost get used to it, and what she wanted — what she had wanted since she was eleven — was a *deadline* she could run at.

*Fifty-four per cent on the board and thirteen days on the projection, and three to nine months in a man's heart, and a machine in Hall Seven that might be able to cut the bleed off entirely.*

*I'll take the heart,* she thought. *God help me. I'll take the whole heart.*

She couldn't have said, then, why that was worse.

"This doesn't feel right," Milo said, examining his own copy. "Why just first-years? Why Demonstration Hall Seven?"

Eli adjusted his glasses, his analytical mind racing. "Hall Seven is in the East Wing. Isolated. Minimal security coverage." He looked up, his face pale. "It's the perfect location for something you don't want witnessed."

Zara's empathic threads were already probing the emotional landscape of the Academy. "There's something wrong with the staff signatures this morning. Fear. Resignation. Like they know something terrible is about to happen but feel powerless to stop it."

Lyra barely heard them. Her mind was racing through possibilities, desperate calculations. If Harlow had developed severance techniques — if he really could cut the connection between her gift and her father's deteriorating brain...

*Maybe this is my chance. Maybe I can end this.*

## THE GATHERING

Demonstration Hall Seven buzzed with nervous energy as first-year students filed in. The circular chamber felt different — colder, more clinical. The usual practice equipment had been removed, replaced by a single observation platform at the centre and banks of monitoring equipment along the walls.

There was a notice on the door. Lyra read it going in, because she read everything, and it took her four steps to understand that she was looking at the reason she was allowed to be here.

> **COUNCIL OF TRADITIONS — EMERGENCY WARRANT 44-B**
> Authorising a supervised demonstration of emergency thread-stabilisation
> protocols upon first-year volunteers, Demonstration Hall Seven.
> Grounds: prospective failure at the Southern Nexus.
> Filed: four days ago. Countersigned: Headmistress E. Weaver.
> Conditions: informed consent of the volunteer; observation by two faculty
> not conducting the procedure; Council observer present.

Two faculty who were not conducting it. A Council observer. And on the platform, when Lyra looked at the chairs set back against the wall, three empty ones, and understood that the Academy's own procedures had been satisfied on paper by people who were not in the room.

*Milo had gone very still beside her.* Zara's hand had found her own collar, which was what Zara did instead of swearing.

Professor Harlow stood at the control panel, his silver scars pulsing with dull light. Security Chief Blackwood flanked the exits, her hand resting casually on her weapon. Other faculty members were notably absent.

"Welcome," Harlow began, his voice carrying easily through the chamber. "Today's demonstration concerns emergency thread stabilization protocols. Recent instabilities in the Southern Nexus require immediate intervention."

Lyra exchanged glances with her friends. The Southern Nexus was stable according to all public reports. This was a lie — but why?

"The procedure requires a volunteer," Harlow continued, his gaze sweeping the assembled students. "Someone with exceptional thread sensitivity and multi-spectrum perception."

"Volunteer," Eli said, from somewhere behind her, in a voice so level it was almost a shout. "He said *volunteer*. Eli Park, second row, can read."

Several students shifted uncomfortably. Lyra felt the weight of Harlow's attention before his eyes found hers.

"Miss Chen," he said, his voice deceptively gentle. "Your unique resonance with the Animus Argenti makes you ideal for this demonstration. Would you join me on the platform?"

It wasn't a request. Blackwood's hand moved to her weapon, and Lyra noticed other security personnel positioned throughout the chamber. The exits were blocked.

But Lyra was already moving, her decision made. This was her chance — her only chance — to save her father.

## THE CHOICE

"Lyra, don't," Milo whispered, but she was already moving toward the platform.

"It's alright," she said, loud enough for everyone to hear. "I trust Professor Harlow's expertise."

The lie tasted bitter, but it bought her time. As she stepped onto the platform, she felt the subtle hum of containment fields activating around her. The trap was complete — but perhaps she could turn it to her advantage.

"Excellent," said Harlow, in the voice of a man reading a result off an instrument, and he did not ask her again. "Now, if the other students would observe from the viewing area..."

Blackwood gestured with her weapon, herding the first-years toward the reinforced observation windows. Lyra caught Milo's eye and mouthed a single word: "Go."

He understood. When the moment came, they needed to get out of the building and get help. She would buy them that chance, whatever the cost.

## THE REVELATION

"The Magnus Conduit," Harlow explained as technicians wheeled in a smaller version of the device Lyra had seen in his laboratory, "represents the pinnacle of thread stabilization technology. By creating a controlled extraction and redistribution matrix, we can prevent catastrophic nexus collapses."

The device hummed to life. It was a working model and a quarter the size, and it had the same fault lines in it, and the black-silver came off it in slow curls, and the curls went out toward Lyra and stopped about a foot short of her and held there, patient, the way water stands at the top of a weir.

"The process requires a resonant bridge," Harlow continued, his voice taking on a lecturing tone even as he prepared to commit atrocity. "Someone whose thread signature can channel and stabilize the chaotic energies."

Lyra felt the current cross the foot of space and find her, and her pendant flared with warning heat. This wasn't a demonstration — it was a live draw. Harlow intended to run twenty minutes of it on her in front of the entire first-year class and call it a demonstration.

But maybe... maybe she could use this. If the machine could take a current off the network, then there was a path the current came in by, and a thing that could take a thing could be made to put it back. Use it to sever her own connection to the thread dimension, break the sympathetic bleed that was killing her father.

"Professor," she said, her voice steady despite her racing heart, "this doesn't feel like a standard demonstration."

"Because it isn't," said Harlow. "There are people in this valley who would rather the Nexus failed than be managed, and I am telling you that they have reached this building, and I am telling you that I hold a warrant, and I am telling you which of those two facts I would rather be able to prove. Desperate times require desperate measures."

The containment field came up again and the air in it got heavier, and she felt the difference in her teeth. Around the observation area, students began to panic as they realized what they were witnessing.

"Let her go!" Milo shouted, pressing against the reinforced glass.

"The demonstration will proceed," Blackwood announced, her weapon now openly displayed. "Anyone who interferes will be considered a threat to Academy security."

## THE DESPERATE GAMBIT

The Magnus Conduit's hum deepened, and the draw began. It felt like a bath filling on the other side of a wall — not much of it, and all of it in one direction, and no way at all to make it go the other.

Pain lanced through her chest as the extraction intensified. This wasn't the gentle communion her mother had taught her — this was violent, invasive, wrong. And the current, which had been coming at her, went back.

But in that moment of connection, Lyra saw her chance. The machine was designed to extract and redistribute thread energy. If she could reverse the flow, redirect it back into herself with enough force...

*I can burn out my own gift. Save Dad. End this.*

"Stop," she gasped, but not to Harlow. She was speaking to herself, gathering courage for what she was about to attempt.

"Fascinating," Harlow murmured, watching the energy readings spike. "Your resonance is even stronger than anticipated. The Conduit is achieving unprecedented stability."

Through the observation windows, Lyra could see her friends fighting against the security barriers, their faces twisted with helpless rage. Milo's musical threads were reaching toward her, trying to offer comfort. Zara's empathic abilities were broadcasting her pain to everyone in the room. Eli's analytical mind was racing, searching for solutions.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, focusing all her will on the connection to the Magnus Conduit. *I'm so sorry.*

## THE REVERSAL ATTEMPT

Lyra stopped resisting. That was the part she would be asked about afterwards, and the part she could not afterwards make sound like anything other than what it was: she had gone *toward* the extraction, into it, with the whole of the gift, and had asked the machine to take the rest.

It took about four seconds and it was not what anybody had come for.

The silver came back down the conduit and into her, and the containment field went, and Lyra heard herself make a noise she had not known she could make.

What she had asked it to cut, it cut. It did not ask her which.

"Lyra!" Milo's voice, from the observation floor, over the noise of it. His musical threads reached for her before he had got there.

The misdirected severance energy found his connection and *snapped* it.

Milo collapsed, and it was instantaneous, and then Demonstration Hall Seven was the quietest place Lyra had ever stood in.

"No!" Lyra's scream echoed through the chamber as she realized what she'd done. In trying to save her father by destroying her own gift, she'd accidentally destroyed Milo's instead.

## THE SILVER RESPONSE

The conduit cracked. Silver came up through the grey in a branching seam, and the black-silver that had been reaching for her stopped where it was and stayed there, and did not go back. Lyra, who had been reading currents since she was thirteen, could not tell what she was looking at, and had never in her life been able to say that about anything in this building.

"What's happening?" Harlow demanded, his fingers flying across the control panel. "The extraction matrix is destabilizing."

Lyra felt it too, and it was the strangest thing that had ever happened to her. It was not fighting. Nothing was fighting. The connection she had tried to cut was still a connection, and the current that had been coming down it had turned round in her hands and was going back up it — past her, past the machine, out into the wards.

"You don't understand," Lyra said, her voice breaking as she looked at Milo's unconscious form. "You are not harvesting anything. You are *moving* it. And it has gone back."

But her words felt hollow. She had done this. In her desperation to save her father, she had become the very thing she'd sworn to fight against — someone who severed connections, who broke the bonds that held the world together.

The core was more silver than black now. Behind Harlow a panel started to sound, and went on sounding, and he did not turn round to find out what it was.

"Shut it down!" he shouted to his technicians, but it was too late.

The silver went out through the observation windows and put a crack in every one of them, and the two hundred and eleven people on the other side of the glass felt it come up through the soles of their feet.

She could have run. The door behind her was open and the corridor beyond it was clear, and she had run before, and she knew to within a few seconds exactly how long it took.

She didn't try to escape. Instead, she knelt beside Milo's still form, placing her hands on his chest where his musical threads had once resonated.

"If you want a bridge," she said, her voice carrying over the noise, "then I'll be one. But not for extraction. For healing."

She put her hands back on him and did the opposite, which is a thing you can only do with the same gift, in the same posture, and which is the reason the gift is worth having at all. The light that came out of her was not the light that had gone into him. It was slower, and it was warm, and it went looking for the thing that had been cut instead of cutting.

*Please,* she whispered to the thread dimension. *Let me fix this. Let me give him back what I took.*

The room went white, and it was not the same white.

## THE WEIGHT OF CHOICE

*Later, in the Academy medical wing...*

They had put him in Bay Four, away from the main ward, because Dr. Osei had looked at the readings and then at the four first-years standing in the middle of her emergency room without a plan, and had said one word — *out* — and then had said, to Zara, the thing Lyra would remember longest:

"You are three, and you have just been the most useful person in this building. Go and sit with him. Go on. He is not going to know the difference for about two days, and then he is going to want somebody there who is not crying, and I would like it to be somebody who is not crying."

So Lyra sat down beside Milo's bed and made herself be that person, and it was the hardest work she had ever done.

He was very still. The monitors were being kind about him, which she understood, about a minute later, to be a thing monitors do.

"The reversal was crude," Osei said, from the foot of the bed. She did not say *uncontrolled*. She did not say *crude* twice. "What I can tell you is that the pathways he used are not scarred. They are *empty*. There is nothing there to scar. It is the difference between a broken bone and an amputation, and it is a real difference, and I am not going to pretend it is a hopeful one."

"Will it grow back," Lyra said.

"I don't know." Osei sat down on the trolley, which put her below Lyra's eye line, deliberately. "In three months I will know more. If the empty space is bounded — if there is healthy tissue all around it — then there is a version of this where the tissue grows back along a new route, and he has something that is not what he had. I would not bet on it. I would also not tell his family that it is unlikely, because in eleven years I have been wrong about both."

"And he'll never be able to—"

"He will be able to hear music," Osei said. "Perfectly well. Better than either of us. He will be able to sing in a choir, play in an orchestra, work out what a key change is, and be bored by all of it. What he will not be able to do is the thing he *does*, which is feel the shape of a room in sound and tell you what is wrong with it." She held Lyra's eye. "Do you understand the difference, Lyra? He is not going to be deaf. He is going to be ordinary. He is going to be the best ordinary musician you have ever met, and he is going to be twenty-two years old before he stops being angry about it."

*Twenty-two years old.* It was such an ordinary number. It was the kind of number that went on a form.

Zara and Eli stood a little way off with their arms folded and their faces closed, and Lyra could read the shape of what they were not saying off their faces from where she sat, and she was grateful to them for not saying it, and she was also — in a small way she was not proud of — angry that they had not said it, because if they had said it she would have had something to fight.

"You were trying to save your father," Zara said at last, very gently. "You didn't know this would happen."

"That doesn't matter."

"That is the first thing that has ever mattered to you, and it is the first thing that has ever been wrong." Zara's voice was not kind. Lyra was grateful for that too. "But I am not going to argue about it at two in the morning with him in that bed. So I will say the true thing, and then we will both go and sit down. You did not know. That is not the same as it not being your fault. You did know *enough*. You knew enough that you had a note from me, from Monday, saying *ask somebody*. You had a father on this campus. You had a father, and a doctor, and three friends, and a machine three floors down — and you went into the machine by yourself, because in about four minutes you had decided that four minutes of being alone was a thing you were allowed."

The ward was quiet. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed.

"I decided it was the only thing that would work."

"Then say that. Say: *I decided the only thing that would work was the only thing I was allowed to do.* That sentence is true, and it is terrible, and it is not the one you were saying."

Lyra did not answer. Osei was looking at the far wall in a way that meant *I am giving you both a moment and I am not going to be here for it.*

"It is my fault," Lyra said.

"It is your fault," Zara agreed. "That is a sentence about what you did. It is not a sentence about who you are. Keep them apart, and you can put the first one down eventually. Smash them together and you get to carry both of them for the rest of your life — and you will not, Lyra, because you are sixteen, and you get tired."

*Milo,* thought the part of her that was not in the conversation, *when you wake up I am going to have to tell you. And you are going to say something kind and foolish. And I am going to have to sit there and let you.*

*And then you are going to get up. And you are going to be angry. And I am going to have to be the person in the room when you are.*

*That is what is actually going to happen. Not forgiveness. That.*

The jade at her throat was cold, and it was a stone, and it had been a stone for four hundred years before anyone had thought to put it round a child's neck, and it was not going to help.

## WHAT THE BOARD DID

*Later still. The East Tower, in the wreck of Hall Seven.*

Nobody told her this at the time. She worked it out afterwards, from the readings, over a period of days.

Here is what had happened to the counter.

The prototype had been drawing — pulling, siphoning, converting — and for eleven minutes it had been pulling *on her*, and she had reached back up the wire with everything she had, in a shape she had not been taught and could not afterwards describe. What had gone down had not been a burst. It had been a *return*. Everything the machine had taken out of the network in an hour of drawing, and everything it had taken out of her, went back into the ward grid in about ninety seconds.

Fifty-four per cent, and thirty hours. And then, for eleven minutes, nothing sensible at all. And then:

*42% instability. 48 h.*

The collector under the gym, which had been sitting at eighty-seven per cent of capacity, emptied to eleven. The Council's model had thrown both arms up, recalibrated against a signature it had no baseline for, and extended its own forecast by eighteen hours, very cautiously, in purple ink.

Security Chief Blackwood had stood in front of that board for a long time, and then had said one word into a wrist device, and then had said, aloud, to nobody at all: "Well."

There would be a great deal of argument about what the Silver Response was. Tanaka would argue for years that it was an artefact of an unmodelled failure and that the Academy had been extraordinarily lucky. Adeyemi would argue — and Lyra would eventually come round to agreeing with him — that the machine had been holding a door open, and that something had come through it that had not been invited, and that the *door* was the important part and the invitation was the entire moral content of the event.

What nobody disputed, then or afterwards, was that a *return* had happened; that it had come from outside the machine; and that Harlow's twenty years of arithmetic had been answered by something that did not do arithmetic at all.

*Weave-Quake Counter: 42% instability. Node-failure projection: T+20 d. Extended by the Silver Response. The Council has minuted that it does not understand the extension and has applied it anyway.*
