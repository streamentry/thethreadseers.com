## THE LOCKED DOOR

Headmistress Weaver's office door was locked at twenty to four in the morning, which Lyra had known it would be, and there was a light on under it, which was new information and worse than the lock.

She stood in the corridor outside it for eleven minutes with four broken ribs of paper in her phone and a left arm she had wrapped in a strip of her own shirt because the clinic had been shut and the boy at the desk had taken one look at the burn and told her to come back at eight, and she had said *it is that kind of burn* and he had said *I know what kind it is, come back at eight.*

There was no plan after that. The plan was on a step outside the library and it was Eli's and it began at eight in the morning at the Registrar's office, and it was eight in the morning and she was here at twenty to four, and the distance between those two facts was about two hundred metres and eleven hours and she could not make it smaller by standing still.

So she went down one floor to find out whose light it was, and the answer to that was on a door at the bottom of the service stair, and the answer was that the Headmistress's night was being spent in Security.

The door to the Security antechamber was ajar. That in itself was information; the antechamber was alarmed and the alarm was off, and Lyra noted that, and then went through, because the two doors at the back of it were shut and there was a ventilation register above the left-hand one about nine feet up.

She got the chair from the antechamber. It was a good chair. She had never resented anything more.

She got it up onto the desk and she put her ear against the register and she heard, in the first two seconds:

"—six per cent, Marcus. Six. You told me four in June."

"I told you four in June in a corridor and I told you seven in a document, and you are now telling me about the six at three in the morning, which tells me that you read the document."

"I read every document you sent me. That is my *position*."

## WHAT SHE HEARD

What follows is what Lyra Chen heard through a ventilation register at twenty to four in the morning on the fifteenth of September, and it is set down here because she set it down herself, that night, in the back of a notebook, in eleven words at a time, and because she was careful about the difference between what she heard and what she worked out, and because the difference turned out to matter for a very long time.

She heard two voices. One was Harlow's, which she knew. The other was a woman of about fifty-five with a slight west-coast flattening on the vowels, and it was not Security's voice — she had heard Security all week — and when the woman finally said a name, out loud, to a man, it was *Marcus*, and it was said the way people say a name they have been saying in private.

She heard the words *thread-burn*, and *sixty-seven per cent*, and *cardiac involvement at eighty*, and she wrote all three down and underlined the third because that was the one that was about a person rather than a percentage.

She heard a photograph described: *a woman of thirty-four in a garden and a child of seven with her hand in her coat pocket*, and she wrote *he told her*, and then she wrote *no: she had it. She already had it. She brought it out*, and she underlined the second version because she had been in a basement four hours ago and she had watched a man put a hairpin back in a box.

She heard the woman say the words *reappropriated, absorbed*, and describe a database with four hundred and six entries in it, and the word she used for the entries was *assets*, and Lyra wrote that down and then put a line through it and then wrote it again, because it was true and because she had a rule now, from four hours ago, about not making a thing worse by making it bigger.

She heard the woman say *Kheredu-Seven* twice and not explain it, and Lyra did not know what it was then and did in March.

She did not know the woman's name that night, and has not been able to put it down since, and she has had it since the nineteenth of October, from the last line of a document four thousand words long that was not in any book until that day, a line that gives a girl's name and then gives her mother's: *Her mother's name is Ines.* She has written down the four other things she has about that woman, and she has not written down what she thinks they come to, and she has been asked twice, by people who were not in the room, whether the mother of a fifteen-year-old girl was in it, and she has answered both times, and both times she has said that she heard two voices and that one of them was a man's.

And she heard the words *Demonstration Hall Seven* said by the woman and *this morning* said by the man, and at that point Lyra Chen put both hands over her own mouth, because she had been standing in Demonstration Hall Seven on the Thursday at half past two in the afternoon with two hundred and eleven people in it when Zara asked a question about a number, and she knew what was in that room, and she had stood in it.

She did not hear either of them express a motive. She wants that written down, because she is going to be asked about it for years and she will be asked it by people who were not there and who want it to be simpler than it was. Nobody in that room said the word *revenge*. Nobody said *for Kyoto*. Nobody said *because they took her from me*. They talked about yield, and cardiorespiratory, and a budget cycle, and a child who lives in Portland.

She has thought about that for a long time. She thinks it is the worst fact she knows.

## THE PART SHE DID NOT HEAR

The last four minutes she had them and she did not understand a word of it, because it was in the register and it was low and it was two voices speaking at once.

What she could get was this: a name, three times, that she wrote as *Lin* and underlined twice. A word she wrote as *tone*. And a sentence, three times, in a flat west-coast voice, that she copied out word for word on the back of a page in a corridor at ten past four in the morning and which read:

*You are not doing this for her. You are doing this because you have spent sixteen years being the only person in every room who knew, and I am offering you the one thing you cannot buy, which is a witness. When it works, you will be believed. And a man who is believed does not have to be right.*

She wrote that down and she did not know what to do with it for nineteen hours.

## THE STAIR

She came down off the chair and the chair made a noise and she had four minutes.

What she did in those four minutes was the thing she has been asked about more than any other, in inquiries and interviews and on the three occasions it has come up with people she was fond of, and the answer is short and it does not make her look good.

She did not go up to the Headmistress's office. The light was still on. The Headmistress was forty feet away behind a locked door, awake, at twenty to five in the morning, on the worst night the Academy had had since nineteen ninety-seven.

She went to the stairs, and down, and across the courtyard, and up to the east dormitory, and she told the others, and she told them everything she had heard, in the order she had heard it, and she did not tell one of them about the four minutes she could not understand, for eleven days.

There is a version of this in which a sixteen-year-old girl bangs on the Headmistress's door at five in the morning with a burnt arm and a notebook full of a conspiracy and it is the beginning of something.

There is a version in which she goes back to her own room with four broken ribs of paper and waits five hours for a boy with a dislocated shoulder to go and see a registrar about a Council resolution, and Lyra Chen chose the second one, on a stair, alone, at ten past five in the morning, and gave as her reason that she did not know whether the woman behind the door was in it.

And the reason she did not give, which she gave instead to Eli Park in October and which is in the record, was this: *if the Headmistress is in it then I have told her I know, and I have four friends. If she is not in it then I have wasted the only four hours I had, and I would rather waste four hours than be wrong about a woman who could otherwise be the only adult in this building.*

She wrote that down at the time, in the notebook, in the eleven-word hand, and underlined it, and then she went to bed and slept for fifty minutes, which she was going to need.

## THE NOTE SHE WROTE AT SEVEN

She was not the one who went to the Registrar's office.

The four of them sent it by terminal, at twenty-one minutes past seven, from the west corridor, with a copy to the Registrar, and then they all went to Demonstration Hall Seven because the notice said 8:00 sharp and no exceptions and because in the end nobody in this account ever found a way to make a rule mean anything other than exactly what it said.

Eli Park went to the Registrar's office on the sixteenth, with his arm in a sling made out of Zara Washington's cardigan, and he was in it for two hours and eleven minutes, and he had already read the letter, because it had gone to her the evening before, which was the last useful thing anybody did for that office in September.

The Registrar of Threadweaver Academy is Dr. Mei-Hua Chen, and Lyra had met her once, for eleven minutes, in a corridor, on the fourth day. Lyra had not told the other three that this was true, on the grounds that she could not be the person who raised it, and the other three had worked it out on their own inside about four minutes and had then been so careful with her about it that she has described them, in print, as *appallingly decent*.

*What none of the four of them knew at twenty-one minutes past seven on the fifteenth of September was that Dr. Chen had already been asked eleven questions about this building by a grandmother in Westbrook in the spring of 2007, and had not answered one of them, and had known there was a machine under her for eleven years, and had been waiting, since the eleventh of September, for somebody to hand her something she was allowed to act on.*
