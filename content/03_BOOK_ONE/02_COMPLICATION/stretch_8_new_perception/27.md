The fragile peace at Threadweaver Academy after the Thread Maze collapse shattered at 2:17 in the morning, and the first thing Lyra noticed was that the corridor floor had gone from *wrong* to *loud*, and that the loud was in her teeth.

She did not need to see any of it. The alarms were the wrong shape for a building — not the chimes, the shriek — and the stone was doing the thing it did when the archway came down, and somewhere east and below something was being turned on that had been off for a long time.

Her bare feet hit cold floor. She went back for Adeyemi's staff, which she had returned that week and had been thinking about every day since. The cowrie shells were warm. She got as far as the stairwell before she understood that she had not taken it because she could use it; she had taken it because it was the only object in that building made by somebody who had not been in the room. In the hallway she collided with Zara, who was already crying and already apologising for crying.

"Syndicate," Eli said, from his door, with a tablet in his hand and the screen doing something he was reading faster than it updated. "They know the building. That is not a breach, that is somebody working from a plan."

Milo came out last, on the crutch he had been told to stop using, moving at a speed the corridor deserved better of.

"I've got nothing," he said, before anybody had asked him anything. "I want that said first, so nobody wastes four seconds. I can't hear any of it. Not the wards, not where it is, not how many. Two weeks ago I would have given you the floor plan and the turnstile count." He stopped, and did not say the rest of it.

Zara had her hand flat on the wall. "East Tower. Big. It's not a person, it's the whole east range. And there's a second thing under the archives, and it's *older* than the first one." She was shaking. "I can feel both. I can't tell which is worse and I'm not going to pretend I can."

Eli, at a screen, said: "Then we take the older one."

"They have a list," Lyra said. "They have had a list for a long time, and everything on it is a room."

The four of them moved through corridors full of students and staff who had been told to stand still and were standing still in the way people do when they have been told to stand still. The operatives came through it in threes and in fives, and they came through it *well*, and the thing Lyra could not get past — the thing that sat under everything else all night — was that they were not frightened. Nobody in that building was frightened of them.

A third-year went down on the East Wing stair with her wards up and the wards came apart, and a man with a wand went past her without slowing, and the nine seconds that bought the fourteen first-years behind her were the whole of what she did that anyone has since written down. Deeper in, somebody from Theoretical held a junction for the length of a corridor and was carried out of it by three fourth-years who are extremely annoyed about it.

The pressure against Lyra's skin was wrong in a way she could not put a word to, and the wrongness was not new; it was the taste of the thing in the housing under the floor, the taste of a person who could do this and had been doing it for years. Her new perception flared with it — heat, behind the eyes, the ringing white static — and she made herself keep walking, because stopping was the thing she was not going to do tonight.

"This way," she said, and took them into a service passage she had found in the days since her sight went, when she had nothing to do but walk the building and learn its shape, and which she had learned entirely by hand.

When forced to engage, Zara's empathic projections put doubts in isolated operatives about whose side they were on, which was worth more and cost her more than she could afford. Eli's barriers put up four seconds of cover each, built from counter-measures he had written at fifteen with a broken arm and no gift. Milo carried the second case, and put a boy of eleven through a doorway, and got them both to the far side, and did it four times; and it was the only thing he was good for all night, and he did not once mention that it was the only thing he was good for.

Under the Archives there was a room the Academy had stopped using, holding a second conduit that had been dormant so long that the interns called it the cold one, and there were four operatives on the door and none of the four were Academy, and inside, two technicians were working on something black.

"Listen," Lyra said, and put her palm flat on the wall, and the stone was screaming, which was not a thing stone did, and which she had felt once before in a corridor on the fourteenth and had not slept properly since. "They have not come for anything in this building. They have come for the cold one and they are turning it on."

"It's not a machine," Eli said. "Look at the cabling. It isn't drawing power, it's *being fed*. Somebody is holding it open from outside and the room is the mouth."

There were four of the academy's own on the floor by the door and none of them had been armed, and in the middle of the room a thing the size of a car was filling up with a light that was the wrong colour and getting wronger, and thick cables ran out of it and into the older stone of the conduit, and it was making a noise Lyra could feel in her back teeth and could not, at that moment, describe to the three people she needed to describe it to.

The light in the room was doing something light does not do, and the wrongness of it was not a colour at all, which was the part Lyra could not explain to anybody afterwards: she could feel that it was *wrong* the way you can tell that a room has somebody in it who is angry, and there was no name for the thing and no shape to point at.

"Open it," said a woman in a black visor, to somebody, in the flat register of somebody reading a checklist. "All of it. I want the signature before the hour."

And Lyra understood, and the understanding arrived in her chest before it arrived anywhere else, as a heat — the silver thing flaring hard enough that she put her hand against the wall to stay upright.

"They are not here for the building," she said. "They have not come for the conduit. They have come for what the conduit is *keyed* to. They have come for the Animus Argenti and they think it is in this room."

The woman's head came round. The visor had a crack in it now, and behind the crack there was a face that was not enjoying itself, and she brought a weapon up that was humming with the same light as the thing on the floor.

"The Silver Soul," she said. "We will have it."

Before she could fire, the ceiling came down over the machine — not all of it, a section, the way a ceiling behaves when something underneath it has been running too hard for too long — and the Registrar of Threadweaver Academy came down through the hole with Elder Adeyemi and four others, and Adeyemi's staff was already making the noise it makes when he means it.

What happened next took about ninety seconds and Lyra has described it four times, in three versions, and none of the four agrees about the order. Adeyemi's staff came down and the shells on it lit and the woman in the visor backed off him, which nobody had expected and which bought the room. Tanaka took the door and did not come past it. Two of the four who came through the ceiling were students, fourth-years, and one of them was crying and fighting at the same time, and the other one was not crying at all and neither has said since why.

"Milo," said Lyra. "The cables. Whatever happens to that thing, do not touch the cables."

"I cannot hear the cables," said Milo. "I am telling you that I cannot hear the cables, and you are going to do it anyway, and I would like it recorded that I said the useful part first."

She went up onto the housing with the regulator in front of her and put both hands on it, and the silver thread went out of her and into the stone — visible, the whole length of it, a cord that anybody in that room with eyes could see and that the four of them had never once seen her do — and what she did with it was not a push.

She has been asked about this the most, of anything, and she has never once given a good answer, and the closest she gets is that the old conduit was a thing that had been *fed* for fifty years by people who were not asking it anything, and that it had a shape, and that she went looking for the shape the way you go looking for a door in a wall you have been touching in the dark.

The machine on the floor made a noise that was not loud and then was very loud. The regulator came off its mount. Something let go in the ceiling, in the floor and in the four people's left ears simultaneously, and the light in the room went out.

"Containment," said the woman in the visor, to nobody, once.

Then the room was a room again.

They went. Not far and not fast — the two technicians went first, and then the woman, and then four men who had come in with a plan and no longer had one. Somebody was dragged. The room was very cold and very quiet and the whole building was screaming its one long note, and the wall-board in the corridor outside had a number on it that was a great deal worse than the one it had carried at two o'clock that morning.

Dr Mei-Hua Chen got to Lyra first. She had a cut over one eye from the ceiling and she did not appear to know it, and she said, quietly, in the voice of somebody reading a label on a jar:

"They were after you. Or what you carry."

Before Lyra could respond, a second alert came up, and Lyra read the header twice and then a third time.

**SECURITY. SUB-LEVEL 2, WARD 41. VISITOR COUNT DISCREPANCY.**

*Twelve days ago,* she thought, *Harlow was moved to Council custody off-site on a warrant countersigned by a woman who is not standing in this corridor. He has not been on this campus since the twenty-fourth. There cannot be a visitor discrepancy in a room that is empty.*

And then, with the awful slowness of somebody fitting together two pieces she did not want to fit:

*Unless the discrepancy is not in Ward 41.*

*Unless somebody has had a count of one in Ward 41 for a fortnight, and has been using the ward to book a lift.*

It was on Council letterhead, and the *off-site* was the only part of it that was not true, and she had known that since the twenty-sixth, in a room with a panel in the window that showed a valley that was not there.

"Not him," Lyra said, and felt the shape of it go cold. "The *route*. He cancelled the draw on his own authority and he published the route. There is a diagram of every door between here and Sub-level 7 in that good room, because he is a man who draws diagrams, and it is signed, and it was read by four Council observers, and I was one of them."

Eli said, very quietly: "And Blackwood has been inside the Council's correspondence chain since May."

Nobody moved for a moment. Somewhere above them a ceiling section settled, and the sound of it going was the most ordinary sound in the world.

"Right," said Milo, and stopped being frightened, which was worse than the fear and which all three of them had seen him do exactly once before. "So we've got until tomorrow night and we know the stairs. Everybody eat something. That's an order from the person who can't hear half of you, so it's the only order in this building that actually counts."
