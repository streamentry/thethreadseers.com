## THE SUMMONS

The message came near midnight, delivered by a thread-construct that dissolved the moment Lyra read it: *Come alone. Sub-level 7. Your father's life depends on it. - H*

Lyra stared at the empty air where the message had been, her heart hammering against her ribs. Her father was fading despite the Academy's best medical care. The doctors spoke in hushed tones about "accelerated deterioration" and "unprecedented neural damage," but Lyra knew the truth. Every moment she spent at the Academy, every thread she touched in her studies, was killing him faster.

She wrote to three people at once, at 11:41, and her hands were steady, and she noticed that they were steady, and she has never afterwards been able to decide whether that was courage.

*I am going. Come alone says come alone. I am not going alone. That is not bravery. That is me keeping a piece of procedure that three people wrote into a piece of paper in an infirmary in September. And if I break it tonight I will have broken it for no reason at all, and I have had enough of doing things for no reason at all.*

The three replies came back inside four minutes, which she had not expected and thought about for a long time afterwards.

The Academy's corridors were different at night — shadows deeper, the thread-light dimmer, the air thick with sleeping dreams and unspoken fears. Lyra made her way to the restricted levels. There was nothing to see. She had spent five weeks finding out what that absence was like and she had stopped expecting anything else of a corridor, and the corridor gave her nothing, which was correct, and which she had to keep reminding herself was not a wound.

But she was not alone, and she knew it in the ordinary way, which is to say that she turned round on the third landing because somebody had not shut a door behind her, and that door was the kind of thing Eli did.

"You didn't really think we'd let you face this alone, did you?" Milo's voice came from the shadows, gentle but determined.

"The message said—" Lyra began.

"The message was from someone who's been lying to us for months," Zara interrupted. She had both hands on the stair rail and had had them there since the second landing. "I do not want to go down there. I want that said out loud, before we go, because I don't know what is down there and I would rather you had the fact than the impression."

Eli emerged from behind a support pillar, his scanner already beeping with concerning readings. "Besides — the readings down here are off the charts and I am the only person in this group who can read them. If you go without me, you walk into a room four people have described to you in a corridor, and you will not know which parts of their description are the parts."

They spent eleven minutes in the last landing, which was too long, and which was the most useful eleven minutes of the entire month.

Milo wanted to stay at the top of the stair with the door. Zara wanted to go in and stay in the room. Eli wanted a list.

"He is not going to the top of the stair," Lyra said. "He is not going to be posted anywhere alone, and he is not going to be a contingency, and if I am saying that it is because I did that in September, and it cost a boy his gift, and I am not doing it twice."

"Then say what I *am*," Milo said.

"You are our floor. You are the only one of us who can count a room by sound, which you have been refusing to admit because it is not a thread, and it is exactly what we need it for, and it is worth more than what you lost." He did not look pleased. He looked like a man handed a coat that did not fit and going to wear it anyway. "And if it goes wrong, you go up, and you send for Weaver, and you do not wait for us, and you do not come back down."

"That's the deal," Eli said, writing it on his own hand. "And we go in together and we come out together. And if we can't come out together, whoever has the least to lose goes first, and we all know who that is, and we are not arguing about it in the corridor."

"Thirty-nine minutes," said Zara. "That's how long before the visitor count in Ward 41 turns into a containment order, and then the stair seals and there is no door at all."

"Then we have gone."

## THE DESCENT

The East Tower's sub-basement stretched deeper than any Academy blueprint suggested, carved from living rock and reinforced with thread-infused steel. The emergency lighting was on its second battery. They went down the hidden staircase behind Harlow's laboratory, and the stairwell was colder than any stairwell has a right to be, and Eli said the temperature out loud twice, because he said things out loud twice.

The air was thick with ozone and with something else. Lyra put her palm flat on the stairwell wall going down, because the wall was the only thing in that building she could still read, and the wall said: *two hundred and eleven separate strains, and eleven of them are being held rather than carried.* She kept her hand there for the whole descent, and did not tell them what it said, and Eli noticed, and did not ask, and she was grateful for that in a way she suspects was not entirely to her credit.

"The readings are off the charts," Eli whispered, his scanner beeping frantically. "Whatever's down here, it's generating massive dimensional distortions."

Zara had stopped three steps short of the chamber floor and was standing with her arms crossed, which is what she did instead of holding on to people. "There is something down here," she said. "I cannot tell you what it is. I can tell you that it is old, and that it is not one thing, and that I would rather not go further into this room than I currently want to, and you should take that as data rather than as drama."

Milo had nothing to offer and said so, which was a thing the four of them had agreed about a week ago and had not expected to have to say in a corridor. "I can't do the reading any more," he said. "I get pressure and direction and the room being wrong. That's all I've got and it's not enough and I'd rather say that now than be useful for about a minute and then go down."

They emerged into a vast chamber that defied the Academy's ancient architecture. Crystalline structures rose from the floor like frozen lightning, connected by cables thick as tree trunks. At the centre there was a chair. It was a chair in the way that a cuff is a cuff: a shape with one purpose, made by somebody who had a specification and no feelings about it.

"This is it," Eli breathed, consulting the blueprints they'd stolen from the archives. "The Magnus Conduit."

## UNDER THE BASE PLATE

The machine was in front of her. That was the first thing.

The second was that she could not see it at all, and had to do this the slow way, with her hands, on the housing, at close range, over about four minutes, with a reading that came in layers and had to be taken one at a time or not at all.

The third was Eli, on the far side, at a screen, in a voice that had gone completely flat:

"Lyra. It's not empty."

"I know."

"It's not empty. There's a subsystem under the base plate that is on neither the 1941 schematic nor the 2009 one, and it is drawing, and it is drawing from *inside itself*, and I think—" he stopped, and started again. "I think it has been drawing from inside itself since 1941. I think that is what it is *for*."

Zara was already on the floor with both hands flat on the plate.

She had her eyes open. Her mouth was open. Nothing was coming out of either.

"Zara."

"I can't get all of them," she said. "I can't. There are too many, they're too deep, and they're not — they're not *in* it, that's the thing, they're *under* it, they're older than it—"

The chamber was very cold. Under the base plate there was a lattice of black-silver, and threaded all the way through the lattice a light that was not a colour Lyra knew, and in the light there were shapes.

Lyra knelt and put her hand on it, because there was nothing else to do with her hand, and the reading came up in one piece, all at once, which it had never done before:

*Nobody had ever asked.*

"What do you mean?" Milo asked, and got nothing from the machine, and got instead the one thing he still got, which was that the room was full of people who had not moved in a long time, and that all of them were facing the same way. He put a hand on the wall to keep the floor where it was.

"He's been harvesting from them," Lyra said, her voice breaking. "Every student who's been affected, every thread signature that's been damaged—he's been siphoning their consciousness to power this thing."

Zara had both hands flat on the plate and she was not coming off it, and Lyra went and got hold of her by the shoulder.

"Zara. Look at me."

"They're still in there," Zara said. "That's the part I keep failing to say out loud. I expected a room. It isn't a room. There are thirty-nine of them and they have been in the same position since before I was born and they are not asleep."

"Zara—"

"And they are not the same age," she said, "and I want that written down somewhere, because everybody is going to say *people* and they are not the same. There is one of them who is much younger than the rest, and I can feel that much, because it is the loudest thing in the room and it is the newest."

The machine pulsed, responding to their presence. Displays flickered to life, showing readouts in languages they didn't recognize. But the central screen was clear enough: "ANIMUS ARGENTI DETECTED. INITIATING CONVERGENCE PROTOCOL."

"We have to destroy it," Eli said, his analytical mind struggling to process the horror before them. "We have to free them."

"I'm afraid I can't allow that."

## THE CONFRONTATION

"I am going to be in the room," said a voice from the stair. "Mrs Okonjo is four steps behind me and she is holding a card that says I am permitted to be here, and she did not enjoy reading it out, and I would like it noted that she did not enjoy it."

He came down the last nine steps by himself. He was in the clothes he had worn on Wednesday. There was a receipt for a sandwich in his breast pocket, which he took out and set on the console, because his hands did not seem to know what else to do with them. Lyra thought: *he has been thirteen days down here and somebody has been feeding him, and he has not mentioned it once, and he is not going to.*

He had lost the composure the way a man loses a coat. There was grey at the edge of his jaw and a plaster across the back of his left hand.

"Professor Harlow," Lyra said, her voice steadier than she felt. "We know what you've been doing. We know about Kyoto, about your family, about the students you've been harvesting from."

"Do you," he said. "Not the arithmetic. The part where I decided."

He put his hand flat on the housing, the way he had put it flat on a desk in Kyoto, and did not look at it.

"I am not asking you for the machine," Harlow said. "I have never once asked anybody for the machine. I have asked for a Council vote. A vote is a thing where the answer can be no. If the answer is no on Thursday then the wall goes on carrying it, and the wall is good for six years, and I will have a different conversation with the Council in the spring and I will lose that one as well."

## WHAT HARLOW SAYS FOR HIMSELF

"They're in there," Milo said. "That's the whole of what I've got. They're in there, and they didn't agree to it, and you know they didn't agree to it, because you've got a number for how long each of them has been in. Which means you've been counting."

"I have been counting for four days," Harlow said. "I am not going to describe what I am trying to do by borrowing a word off a child who has not finished school. I have tried for four days and there isn't one, and I am not going to use a bad one tonight because it is the only one to hand."

"They're prisoners," Zara said, her empathic abilities still reeling from the contact with the trapped minds. "They're suffering, Professor. They want to be free."

"They want to be safe," Harlow snapped. "They want to exist in a reality that isn't collapsing around them. And this is the only way to ensure that."

The Conduit's hum changed pitch. Eli looked up at the number, and then at the number's line on the screen, and Lyra watched him decide not to say the second thing he was thinking, which was that the hum and the number were not the same measurement and he did not yet know which of them was lying.

But even as fear gripped her heart, Lyra felt something else stirring — a deep, unshakeable certainty that there had to be another way. The trapped souls weren't just fuel for Harlow's machine; they were people, with their own hopes and dreams and the right to choose their own fate.

*There has to be another way,* she thought. She was aware of her hand going to the jade at her throat and she left it there, because it was warm, and because it was four hundred years old, and because she was aware that a stone is not an argument and was using it as one.

She put her hand back on the plate.

It took her two minutes and eleven seconds, and it was the first time since the fifteenth of September that she had been the most competent person in a room, and she wants that on the record too.

What she found under her fingers was the shape of what the housing was actually doing. It was not a battery. It was a *hand* — a hand that could hold a weight indefinitely, in a fixed shape, without fatigue, and would not put it down, and could not do anything else at the same time. The translator above it was an entirely different machine. The translator could *carry*: it could take the load off the wall and move it and distribute it and be, in every sense, clever. But carrying was a different verb from holding, and the moment the load stopped coming out of the wall and went onto a circuit that had to hold it in place, the translator's cleverness was worth exactly nothing.

*It is a room with a bucket in it,* she thought, *and somebody has to stand in the bucket.*

"Hey." Eli had his hand on her shoulder. "You've gone somewhere. Talk."

"It can carry," Lyra said. "It can't hold. There's a difference and it is the whole difference, and nobody has ever written it down, because nobody has ever had to." She took her hand off the plate. "In four days they are going to vote on whether to switch it on permanently. And if they switch it on, it will be fine for about six months while the wall is still carrying most of it. And then one morning the wall will be carrying nothing and the machine will be carrying everything, and at that point it will do precisely what it is good at, which is move a load around. It will not do the other thing. It cannot. There is nothing in the design that lets it."

Eli wrote it down. He wrote it twice, and then a third time, and then he read it back to himself, and Lyra watched him understand that he had just been handed the most important sentence anybody had said in that building in twenty-four days, and that it had been said by a girl who could not see.

"Then somebody has to hold it," he said.

"Yes."

"And the housing—" He looked at the lattice, and at the cold light, and did not finish the sentence, because there was exactly one word available and none of them wanted to say it.

"The housing holds it," Lyra said. "That is what the lattice is. It is a hold. It is a hold that works by never letting go of a person who never agreed to be held." She wiped her hand on her coat, which achieved nothing. "And I have just been told, out loud, in a language nobody else in this room can hear, that the answer to that is *no*."

*The pendant pulsed once against her sternum, and for a moment — not a voice; she was very careful about that, not a voice, a *texture*, the way a hand feels through a wall — there was something there that was not a memory of her mother and not the Weave, and it was the sensation of a room where somebody had been waiting a very long time.*

*Right,* she thought, in the flat grammar. *So that is the vote. That is what Thursday is actually about.*

## THE MOMENT OF CHOICE

The Conduit was drawing. Eli gave the number out loud twice, because he gave numbers out loud twice, and the second one was higher.

"You can read it," Harlow said, looking at Lyra's hand on the plate. "That is what I have been watching for since the eleventh of September, and it is the only reason I have not spent four days finding out what happened to the people in it."

"You knew they were in there."

"I knew there was something in there that I could not read, for eleven years, and I have known for thirteen days that it was people." He said it without any self-pity, and Lyra found that she respected him a great deal more for that than for anything else he had done. "Do you want the arithmetic, or do you want to know what I did in May? They are the only two questions left, and I have answered both, and only one of them is on the record."

"I know what you did in May," said Lyra.

"Yes," said Harlow. "I thought you might." And then, unexpectedly, he said: "I have not slept. I am not going to tell you to trust me, and I am not going to tell you the machine is the only option, and I am not going to explain myself to you for another four minutes. Here is the one thing, and then I am going to stop talking, and you can put whatever you like on it."

He turned a console so that Eli could read it.

"Four days," he said. "And in four days a Council is going to vote on whether to bring a machine that carries into permanent service, using a housing that holds, and forty-three people are in the housing, and two of them are ours. One of them is fifteen years old and has been in the machine since the eleventh of September, and her mother is in Bellhaven and thinks she is at a specialist programme. The other one has been there since the eighteenth of May, and he is nineteen, and he is the reason this Council is going to spend nine hours discovering that it has no vocabulary for a person, and I would like somebody to write his name on the board before they do that, because he is in a box and he is nineteen and there is no version of nineteen that can be *noted*." He looked up. "I would like to be in the room when it is opened. I am not asking to be in the room when anything else happens. And if you say no to that, I will understand, and I will not appeal it, and you should know that I am saying that because it is the last thing I have that is not a technique, and I would like somebody to check it."

Forty-three, and not thirty-nine, and Eli Park had had the two numbers in his head for two days and had not been able to make them agree, and he made them agree on the second day, from four lines in a finding he had been shown and had not asked to see: *the other one was under the East Tower, and it had been running continuously since the eighteenth of May, and it had a second housing, and it had been taking four people at about one a month.* Thirty-nine were in the lattice, which had lain underneath Sub-level 7 since 1941, and thirty-seven of them had been dead since the war ended, and the other two were the girl and the boy. The other four were in the second housing, and the second housing was under the East Tower, and all four of them were alive, and the Council's figure of forty-three had been arrived at by adding a lattice to a housing and calling the sum a housing, and nobody had asked for the four separately, because a person taken one at a time, about a month apart, in a machine that is about to be voted into permanent service, is not a list.

"Why do you want to be in the room?"

"Because it is my great-grandfather's crime and my hand is on the seal and somebody should be watching me," said Harlow, "and because I have spent sixteen years being certain that I was the only person in this building who had done anything, and I would like to be shown the list."

"This is her nightmare," Lyra said. "And I won't let you use her memory to justify it."

"She was seven." Harlow had put the legal pad down. "You were not. That is the whole of what I have to say to you and I have been saying it badly for an hour."

"Then say it properly or don't say it."

"I am saying it properly now." He did not move from the console. "Your father drove himself to this building on Monday against a doctor's written advice, on a stick, with a woman following him in a hired car, and he has been sitting in the front of the Lumina Lecture Hall since seven this morning being extremely useful with a napkin and a pencil, and he is going to be there this afternoon as well, and he has not once been asked whether he wants to be. That is what I have to say. That is the thing you can say to a Council tomorrow that nobody in this room can say, because nobody in this room has a father."

He put his hand flat on the console, the way he had put it flat on a desk in Kyoto, and did not look at it.

"Or I can say the other thing, which is that he drove himself here because he would not let you go in alone, and that a man who will not let his daughter go in alone is a man who ought to be asked."

Down the corridor, a door opened that nobody opened, and stayed open, and Lyra counted to four before anybody moved.
