## THE TWO OFFERS

There had been two offers on the table in the last week and Lyra had been given both of them by people who meant well, and she had turned both of them down, and she had understood at the time — with a clarity she had privately been very proud of — exactly why.

The first had been Osei: *you can go home, and your grandmother can be told you are sleeping badly, and I will write that the retreat is on medical advice, which is true, and nobody will look at a document I have signed.*

Lyra had said: *Bà would have to lie to me. To my face, for weeks. And she would do it, and she would do it well, and she would never tell me, and I would spend the rest of my life knowing that the one person who has never lied to me is lying to me.*

The second had been Weaver, in person, on the second day: *Room 41, on Sub-level 2. It has a lock and a corridor outside it and a warder on the corridor, and I will put your grandmother in the next room for as long as you want her there, and nobody comes in who is not on a list, and I will be on the list.*

Lyra had said: *Thank you. I want to think about it.*

And what she had come up with, in the end, in a cold room with a bad view of a car park, was this:

*That is a very good offer and it is an offer to be safe, and I am sixteen, and I do not know one single thing about myself any more, and the only person I have ever been able to find out anything with is in a chair on the other side of a corridor with a stick.*

She had said yes to Room 41 on the twenty-fourth of September, which was a Tuesday, and which she would remember for the rest of her life as *the Tuesday I decided to find out what I was.*

---

She had not, in the days between, told anybody that she was the reason the number on the wall had gone from 45 to 51.

She had not told anybody because she had worked out — with the same small, awful, private clarity that had been her companion all fortnight — that it was not true, and that wanting it to be true was a sort of thinking she was going to have to give up, and that the giving-up was going to take longer than everything else she had to do.

*The machine is running on a dead woman,* she would tell herself, on the bad nights, in the phrasing she had constructed on the third night and had been chewing on ever since. *The load is on the lattice. If I come out of it, the load goes somewhere else, and there is nowhere else, and eleven thousand people find out. That is the arithmetic. That is not a debt I can pay by being brave about it in a corridor. It is a debt that has to be *paid*, and the only currency is somebody in the circuit, and I am the only somebody they have found, and I am sixteen and I am in a hospital.*

And underneath that, the thing she would not say, which was:

*And if I go back in there, and this time somebody is holding a document, and I sign it, then I will have done it twice, and twice is a decision.*

## THE COUNCIL, DAY 32 — THE TWENTY-SEVENTH OF OCTOBER

The Council of Traditions had sat on the thirty-second day and had not ratified the Protocol.

Lyra was not in that room. She was in a corridor outside it, on a chair, with a folder on her knees, because Dr. Chen had asked whether the person appointed as student advocate for the opening of Case 41 would be the person affected, and had not been told no.

What she had been told afterwards, in the corridor, by a woman of nineteen with silver in her hair who had been in the room for eleven hours:

"They're going back to it. In two days. Because the machine is running and they cannot vote on a machine that is not finished, so they have to finish the machine first, and finishing the machine requires the case, and the case requires an advocate, and the advocate is you." The girl had laughed once, without any humour in it at all. "You have accidentally discovered the entire method of this institution. Nothing happens here except through paperwork, and if you *want* something to happen faster you have to supply the paperwork, and the things that happen fastest are always the things somebody has already written down."

"What did they say about the lattice?"

"Nothing, because they do not know what is in it. They have known since the sixteenth that it is people — Osei's board, Ward C, a hundred and ninety-eight names, Zara's *still connected*." The girl wiped her face with the back of her hand. "They have known it is people for sixteen days and they have not opened it, because the *procedure* for opening it requires next of kin, and thirty-seven of them are unlocatable, and the tribunal's position is that sixty-year absence is a *disqualification* rather than a hardship, and your aunt has been fighting that position in writing since the seventeenth, alone, on a form nobody has countersigned."

"And the two who are left?"

The girl looked at her.

"The two who are *locatable*," she said, "are a fifteen-year-old girl held in a care programme at Bellhaven, with her mother in the same town, and a woman dead since 1941 with a sister still living in Sakai who is eighty-one and has not been told. There is a form. It is *on a desk*. Your aunt says the sister will sign, and the mother will not, because the mother has been receiving a letter every fortnight for six weeks explaining that her daughter is in a specialist programme, and the mother is not going to sign a thing that says the opposite."

The folder was heavy on Lyra's knees. It was a copy of her own deposition, and a request that the Council rule that a signed card obtained by a person in a position of authority is not a consent, and a page of Osei's board transcribed, and eight pages of her mother's journal, and a photograph.

The photograph was Elara Vance at a Clinic open day, in a borrowed coat, squinting, with her hand halfway up as though she had been interrupted mid-wave. She was smiling. She had signed a card, four days later, that nobody had read.

They had not shown it to her mother because they were writing a letter every fortnight saying the opposite, and Lyra had spent a long time in the last four days understanding that the letter was not a deception exactly — it was a *holding operation* — and that a holding operation is a thing you do to a person when you cannot afford the truth yet, and that it works, and that it is still a thing you are doing *to* a person.

*Her name is Elara Vance*, Lyra thought. *And she signed a card and went into a machine and said out loud, in a lattice, over four hours, that she wanted to stay — and the only reason she wanted to stay is that sixteen days in there had grown into her, and I asked her a question she could only answer one way and then I honoured her answer, and both of those were right, and one of them was a horror.*

*And her mother is getting a letter about a specialist programme.*

*And in two days a man in Council grey is going to stand in a room and say that a fifteen-year-old's card is not a consent, and that the girl said what she said, and that the girl is alive, and that the girl will be asked again.*

*And I am going to be allowed to be in that room. Because I wrote the note.*

---

The Council had also, on the thirty-second day, done three other things.

It had adopted Osei's name list, in three colours, and required that every one of the hundred and ninety-eight be contacted **by a person who was not the medical officer** before the end of term. There were a hundred and ninety-eight names and eleven days and four trained people, and it was not going to be enough, and it had been done anyway, and that was the whole of what reform looked like in this building at this stage: a thing that was insufficient, minuted.

It had voted, unanimously and with the Headmistress abstaining, to suspend Professor Harlow's faculty rights *without* prejudging the deposition, which was a distinction that had cost Dr. Chen a week of her life to get into the wording.

And it had set a date, and the date was the reason the folder was so heavy.

## THE VISIT

She had seen him once since the fifteenth, in a locked ward on Sub-level 2, for twenty minutes, with a warder on the corridor.

He had stood at the window-panel the entire time, because he had told her he was not going to sit down with her — *you have come here to be talked out of something, and I would rather do it standing up* — and because he had, over nine days in a good room, arrived at a set of opinions about how he would rather be treated than about how he would rather appear.

He had told her four things.

**One.** There were two machines. The one the Council was ratifying ran on Sub-level 2 and had been running since the fifteenth on the load of the lattice alone, because on the twenty-fourth — *last Tuesday, at four in the morning, by my own authority, with no warrant* — he had cancelled the draw on the whole system and the cancellation had held.

**Two.** The other one was under the East Tower, three hundred metres from where she had woken up strapped to a table, and it had been running continuously since the eighteenth of May, and it had a second housing, and it had been taking four people at about one a month.

**Three.** Nobody in the building knew about the second machine except him and the person running it, and that person had been lying to him about it since May in order to keep him funded, and he had believed it, because the alternative was that the network walked and eleven thousand people had a bad week.

**Four.** The four names. Two were on the board in Ward C in the second colour. One was in a care programme in Bellhaven and had been for four months and had cost nothing and had never been spoken, and he was a nineteen-year-old called Tobi Fadipe who had been in Sub-level 3 since the eighteenth of May, and he was the reason Harlow had stood up and not sat down. And the fourth was a boy of fifteen who is alive, and whose mother has been told, and who has not been asked for by name in this building yet.

*That is what the man is for,* Lyra had thought, going down the stairwell. *Not the machine. The man.*

And then, on the fourteenth step, she had understood the rest of it, and had sat down on the step because her legs had stopped being available, and had understood that she now knew three things that nobody in that building had known on the morning of the fifteenth:

that there was a second machine; that somebody was still feeding it; and that a man in a locked ward had cancelled the only system he had legal authority over, on his own signature, and had told nobody, and had then told her.

## THE THREE QUESTIONS

She wrote them on the back of a schedule on the thirty-third day, and she has said, in every account she has given since, that writing them down was the entire difference between this and every other bad week of her life.

**Q1.** What is the load, physically, and what happens to it if the person carrying it says no?

**Q2.** Who is holding the trigger on Sub-level 7, and what is the shortest path from a custody ward to a locked door in this building?

**Q3.** If thirty-seven people have been dead since 1941 and two have not, and the Council cannot open the case because it cannot find the next of kin — who is the person the procedure has failed to count?

She did not write a fourth question, and the reason she did not write a fourth question is that the fourth question was *what do I want to happen*, and she did not trust herself to answer it on her own, and she had spent nine days learning that this particular instinct of hers was the one that killed people.

And underneath, in smaller writing, the thing she did not intend to show anybody:

*I am frightened that the answer to Q3 is me, and that I will be extremely good at it, and that being good at it will feel exactly like being right.*

---

On the thirty-fourth day the Council reconvened on the Nexus Stabilization Protocol, and the case was opened, and the machine was either authorised or decommissioned, and Lyra Chen went into the room at the back with a folder on her knee because she had asked to be allowed and Dr. Chen had written a very short letter saying that the student advocate for the opening of the case had been appointed, and had asked whether the person appointed would be the person affected, and had not been told no.

At 11:40 the night before, a note had gone out from the registrar's office, addressed to three people. It said:

***Come to my room. Nine o'clock. Bring the thing you have been not saying.***

And under it, in a different hand, because the registrar had insisted:

***Not from me. From her. She has written it out. She is frightened to send it. I am sending it. — DC***
