## THE AWAKENING

She came up out of the dark in pieces, and the pieces were in the wrong order.

Cold metal against her spine. A mechanical thrum in the floor, in her teeth, in the backs of her eyes. Indigo light behind her eyelids, pulsing. And a smell like a struck bell left out in the rain.

*Not the infirmary,* was the first whole thought she had. *Wrong thrum. Too slow.*

She opened her eyes.

She was on a table in a room she had not been in before, and it was not Harlow's East Tower laboratory, and it was much larger than any room she was supposed to exist in.

The Conduit stood at the centre of it. She knew it by the sound. She had heard that sound through one wall and three hundred metres of stone, that morning, while it was being rewritten by her.

Black-silver filaments came off its surface in slow curls, and where they touched the air they *bent*, and Lyra understood without being told that this was not a machine that pulled. It was a machine that listened. And that it had been built to pull, and that something had recently got into the room.

Her wrists and temples and sternum were held down by thread-reinforced straps, and medical leads ran from them to the machine, and she could feel the drain — not pain, exactly. *Thinness.* The feeling of a word being used up.

Professor Harlow stood at a console with his back to her. Half his aura was gone. The black-silver had got into him, up past the elbow now, and it moved when he breathed.

"Ah," he said, without turning. "You're awake. Good. Conscious participation gives a much cleaner trace."

"You moved me."

"I did."

"From the medical wing." Her voice came out as gravel. "This morning. With a Council warrant in your pocket that says *supervised demonstration* and a man lying in Bay Four who is not being supervised at all."

She felt him decide not to be insulted.

"Yes," he said. "That is precisely what I did, and it is the first thing you have said to me that I have not had to argue myself out of doing. Keep going. You are more use awake."

---

*Sunday, 15 September, somewhere past noon.*

She had been unconscious since morning. She worked that out from the drip and the light in the stairwell and the state of her own hands, and the working-out took her some time, and she made herself do it slowly, because it was the only thing available to her that was entirely hers.

*They will have looked. Of course they will have looked. Osei will have raised it. Weaver will have raised it. And Blackwood will have produced a transfer order with a signature on it, and a signature is a door that opens from both sides.*

"So this is it," she said. "This is what you were always going to do."

"Not quite." He turned. He looked worse than he had in the lecture hall. The last sixteen years had begun to show in a way they had not been showing. "Do you know what this machine is, Lyra? Not what it is called. It was called a great many things by the men who paid for it. Do you know what it *does*?"

"You're going to tell me."

"I am going to tell you the part that will make you want to cooperate, because I am not a patient man and we have until Thursday." He turned a screen toward her. "Southern Nexus. Forty-five per cent local distortion as of six hours ago, and that is not a network figure, that is one node, and that node is the load-bearing wall under eleven thousand people. It has been failing since Kyoto. It fails in about nine days. On Thursday it fails in one."

He put one finger on the screen. "Nine is the wall with nothing standing in front of it. One is what is left after a procedure, and the procedure is on Thursday. I am telling you both numbers now so that you cannot tell me afterwards that I only gave you one."

"And your machine stops that."

"My machine *carries* it. That is all. Not stops — carries. It moves the load off the wall and onto a held circuit. That is what the sixty years of this project have been for, and it works, and I have run the numbers four thousand times." He took his hand off the console. "What it needs is a resonance to hold the circuit open. Not power. A *tuning fork.* Something in this room that the Weave itself recognises, so that when the load comes off the wall the circuit does not ring itself apart."

"You mean me."

"I mean you."

"And the cost."

"The cost," Harlow said, "is that the tuning fork is consumed. Or rather — the tuning fork is *spent*. It spends itself. It will come out of that table with a great deal of what it is and not all of what it can do, and it will spend the rest of its life doing that."

He said it evenly. He had clearly practised it. That was the thing that stayed with her, afterwards: that it had been practised, in front of a mirror, more than once, with the words adjusted each time so that they did not quite lie.

"How much of it," Lyra said.

"Unknown. I have three prior cases and two of my patients are alive and unrecognisable to themselves."

"So the honest number is: some of me, and I won't know which part, and you won't either, and you have decided that I am the only thing in this building that can go in the hole."

"Yes," Harlow said. "I have decided that. I decided it about nine days ago, in a corridor, after a lecture, and I have not slept particularly well since, and I would ask you to spare me the observation that I have thought about it."

## THE SYSTEM

Something else was happening in the room and Lyra could not stop noticing it.

The core of the Conduit was veined. Where it had been obsidian, it was now half silver, in long marbled faults, and the filaments that came off it had changed their behaviour. They bent. They listened. They turned, very slightly, toward her, the way a dog turns toward a door.

"There is silver in it," she said.

"There is *you* in it," said Harlow, "going both ways."

"Did you put it there?"

"I put a *machine* there. Your mother's notes put a *possibility* there, eleven years ago, in an appendix nobody read, and I have spent four years trying to make the possibility into a machine." He came closer. "You did the rest, in a demonstration hall, in front of two hundred witnesses, without meaning to, and against my instructions."

"I was trying to burn it out."

"I know. I watched. I have watched that footage four times and I have not been able to stop watching it." He stopped about a metre away. "You reached *up the wire*. Do you understand what that is? Every other case in the literature, the subject is *fed*. You reversed the flow. You pushed a hundred and eighty kilovolts of extraction back down a machine that was pulling it, and it went into the ward grid instead of into you, and then it went into the *network*." He said the word network the way other people said prayer. "In sixteen years. Through you. On a *Sunday*."

Lyra thought about Milo, in Bay Four, and about the sound he had made when his hand had come up short.

"Then why are you doing it the slow way," she said.

"Because a Sunday is not a method," Harlow said. "It is an accident with a witness. I want Thursday to be a *procedure*, documented, reproducible, and defensible in front of a Council that will otherwise hang me and everything I have learned. And because—" and here, for the first time, something in his voice came off the lecturing plane — "because a procedure is something I can teach. A Sunday I can only watch happen to a sixteen-year-old girl who was in a hurry to get to an appointment."

Lyra lay in the straps and looked at him and understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that he was not lying to her now, and that this was worse, and that she had absolutely no way of acting on the difference.

## THE BREACH

The door came open at 14:20, and there was noise in the outer corridor, and somebody shouted something about the ward grid, and Lyra's heart went out from under her.

Through the reinforced glass she saw four people, and she knew three of them, and it was the fourth that made her close her eyes.

Eli was carrying a case. Eli, who could not climb anything without a plan, was carrying a case up a service stair with a weight that was wrong for him, and behind him Zara was doing something with the recognition scanner that made the door's little green light go red, go green, go red, in a rhythm that was almost musical, and behind *them* was Milo Reyes.

Milo, who could not hear the building any more. Milo's ears were pressed against a door he could not read. Milo's hands were flat on the frame, and he was not doing anything at all with thread or light or sound, he was simply standing there with his shoulder against a locked door that he could no longer sense, being a body in the way.

*He knows,* Lyra thought. *He knows he can't do his job and he came anyway and he is standing there doing nothing useful because somebody has to stand there.*

The lock went. The containment field came down in stages. Harlow did not fight them with his hands; Harlow had never fought anybody with his hands.

He dropped the room.

It was not an explosion. It was a *reorganisation*. Every thread-reinforced surface in the chamber took its load inward at once — the ceiling came down eighteen inches and stopped, the far wall came across four metres and stopped, and the whole floor tilted a degree and a half and stopped, and the three of them went down the ramp into the wall in a heap.

Eli's case split open on the corner. Something inside it went across the floor and under the console.

Through the glass, muffled, Zara's voice: "— out, out, *out*, we are out, we did not get her—"

And Milo's voice, close to her ear, steady and completely useless and the only one she could actually hear through two layers of glass: "We didn't get her. We didn't get her. We didn't get her, we didn't get her, we didn't—"

Then the ward grid slammed down between them and the light went.

## THE WITHDRAWAL

*Fifteen floors up, in a service corridor with the heating broken.*

Eli was on the floor with his sleeve gone and his forearm open to the elbow, and his tablet was gone, and he was saying numbers.

"—seven minutes, that's what I had, seven minutes of the scanner's cycle before the grid noticed I was in it, and I spent them on a door that wasn't the door. The door was the *outer* door. The inner one has a different—"

"Stop," Zara said. She was crouched with her hand flat on his chest, holding him together by pressure and by not looking. "Eli. Stop counting."

"If I'd had the case open—"

"If you'd had the case open you'd be dead, and so would we, and so would she, because you'd have spent the seven minutes on something that worked." Zara's voice cracked, once, and came back harder. "We have what we didn't come with. That's the bit. Milo. Milo, look at me."

Milo looked at her. His face was wet and he did not appear to know it.

"I couldn't hear it," he said. "That's all. That's the whole thing. Everyone else went through two sets of doors and I could not hear a *single thing*, and I stood there and I pressed my hands on a door like an *idiot*—"

"You stood there," Zara said, "which is why we lost four minutes instead of the corridor."

"We lost *her*."

"You did not lose her. She is eleven floors down and she is alive, because a man with thread-burn is currently terrified that he has damaged something irreplaceable, and terrified men do not terminate experiments." She put her hand on the back of his neck. "Milo. Milo, look at me. I need you to do one thing and I need you to do it now."

"What."

"The report. Eli can't—" She looked at Eli, who was already saying numbers again, in a voice that had gone somewhere else. "Eli. Eli, can you—"

"Twenty-two minutes," Eli said, from the floor. "If the grid is on a twenty-two-minute observation cycle and I can't prove we were in a locked stairwell, the whole thing logs as an unexplained access. Twenty-two minutes. And if we're still on this floor when it logs, it logs *four* of us, in a stairwell, at the same time as a network event, and no security chief in the history of this Academy is going to write that down the way we'd like it written down."

"Twelve minutes," said Zara.

"Twelve minutes is not enough to reach Weaver and not enough to reach the Council observatory, and the one thing—" and Eli stopped, on his back, on the floor, bleeding, and said it very clearly, "the one thing that is *not* optional is that it has to be in writing, and it has to be in writing somewhere that Security Chief Blackwood cannot get to it first, and there is exactly one such place on this campus, and she can get to the other one in nine minutes."

They looked at each other.

"The registrar," Eli said. "Dr. Chen. She is a *civil servant*, Blackwood cannot touch a civil servant's intake log without a Council countersignature, and Chen has been sitting on an unissued student-safety complaint since the day Lyra walked in, because nobody signs it."

"She is Lyra's *aunt*," Zara said.

"Which is exactly why nobody signs it," Eli said, "and exactly why it will not get quietly lost, because she has been *waiting* to sign it, and the moment somebody hands her a *reason*—"

"I've got the communal terminal," said Milo.

He had it before anyone had argued about it, because he had been the one sitting still for four minutes on a stairwell floor with a dead gift and his hands in his hair, and he had used the time to work out the building.

"Corridor's twenty metres that way. There's an under-service lane, it's not warded, it goes out past the chapel wall. Nine minutes, if nobody stops me." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "I'm the only one of us she can't put in a sedation chair, and I know that, and I'm not going to be coy about it. I'm the fastest thing in the building right now and I have nothing else to offer anybody. So."

"Mil—"

"*So.*"

Zara let him go. That was the part Lyra could never explain to anyone afterwards — the part that went into the report and into the inquest and into everything: they let him go, because he had correctly identified the one thing he was still good for, and because a person who has just lost the thing that made them useful has an enormous amount of energy and nowhere to put it, and Zara Washington understood that better than anyone alive.

"Twelve minutes," she said. "Then you *run*."

He ran.

## THE HOUR

*Fifteen floors down, and the grid shut, and the room still.*

They came twice more, over the next three hours, and the second time they came with a Council warrant and a *faculty* observer, and that observer was an elderly woman with grey hair and the exhausted, particular calm of somebody who had been doing this for a very long time.

Headmistress Weaver.

She stood in the doorway for eleven minutes and did not come in, and Lyra watched her read the Council's conditions of four days earlier, and watched her look at the three empty observation chairs, and watched her take out a device and speak into it in a voice with no expression in it at all.

"There are two hundred and eleven students in that hall," Weaver said, when she had finished, "and of them one hundred and ninety-eight have never seen a machine like this one, and every single one of them was told this morning that they were attending a *demonstration*, and none of them were told anything else, and I signed that. Let the record show that I signed it and that I believed it, and that the belief was given to me."

"Headmistress—" Harlow began.

"Marcus." She said his first name, and Lyra saw him flinch, because nobody in that building had said it in sixteen years. "Look at me and tell me it is a demonstration. I will put it in the file either way and I would prefer to be told."

Harlow looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said.

"Dr. Osei," said Weaver, to the device. "I want the full neurological sweep of every student who has come into contact with a Conduit, Phase One, in the last thirty days. I want it by Friday. I want the names in a locked file, not a shared one. And I want you to ring Westbrook Neurological Institute and ask them, in writing, what exactly was said to a Miss Chen's medical file in the last two years, and by whom, and I want the answer before the Council sits on Thursday."

She turned and went out.

Lyra lay in the straps in the indigo light and understood that something enormous had just happened and that it had not been done for her.

*She is not rescuing me,* she thought, with a clarity that was almost comfort. *She is building a case, because she has to, and because that is the only weapon she has, and because if I am freed by a rescue it will be argued later that I was freed improperly. She is doing paperwork. On purpose. With the door in the room.*

Eleven floors above, a boy with no gift ran out of a chapel under-service lane in the rain, and put a sheet of paper into a civil servant's intake tray, and stood there shaking while it was stamped.

*Electronic filing of student-safety intake: CHE-1147-OVERRIDE. Filed 17:04. Not routed to Security. Not routed to Academic Affairs. Routed to: all standing Council observers. Copy: Headmistress.*

Which is how the three of them got out of the stairwell, and how a hundred and ninety-eight students' names got into a locked file, and how the Council ended up in a corridor outside Sub-level 7 at twenty past nine that night with a paper weight and a woman who did not have to be there.

Lyra did not know that yet. She knew the room, and the drain, and the sound of a man who had said *no* to a headmistress and had not looked away when he said it.

There had been a note on her door on Monday. It had said *ask somebody* in a hand that was not her father's and not her grandmother's, and it had been under a door handle for six days, and it was in her coat pocket, and she had read it eleven times and had not asked anybody anything.

She got it out then, in the room, and held it, and did not put it down for about four minutes. It was not her father's hand and it was not her grandmother's. It was the hand of the man who had said *no* to a headmistress and had not looked away when he said it, and it said two words, and Lyra sat on the floor of a locked room and understood that in a few hours she was going to be about a man's heart, at four in the morning, and that nobody had told her that she was allowed to be.
