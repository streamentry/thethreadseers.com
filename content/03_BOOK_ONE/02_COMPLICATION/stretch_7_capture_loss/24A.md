## THE ROOM

Darkness.

Not the night's texture. Not the thing behind her own eyelids. This was *absence* — void where a vibrant threaded world had been dancing, every day of her life, in front of her without her permission.

She sat in her dormitory room in the dark because the light was unbearable, which was not the same as not being able to see it. Her eyes worked. Her eyes had always worked. It was the *other* thing that was gone, and the trouble was that the other thing had been in the room with her since she was six years old, and now it was not, and so the room was a small warm box with a window and a bed and a girl in it, and there was nothing behind any of it.

She reached for her charcoal.

*Draw,* said the part of her that was Nai Nai, and had been Nai Nai since she was a baby, and was the only voice in her head that had never once been embarrassed. *You have always drawn. You have drawn through crying and through chemistry and through the year after your mother went. Whatever your eyes are doing, your hands are still doing.*

The charcoal snapped. It was the wrong length. She had broken the only two decent sticks in September and had not replaced them because she had not been in this room since.

*Fine.*

She drew the window.

Not what was in it — the actual view, the roofs, the treeline, the Weather, all of that still there and still perfectly ordinary, and she looked at it with ordinary eyes and it looked like roofs. She drew what was *behind* it. She drew the absence. She drew a rectangle of paper and then she went over it and over it with the flat of the charcoal until the paper was warm and the surface had gone furry and dull, and there was a black field where the light should have been doing something interesting.

Then she stopped, because her hand had started to report.

---

That was the discovery, and it was smaller than the shape of it would suggest, and it took her nine days to be able to hold it.

It was not a new sense. It was the old one, moved. She had spent sixteen years reading threads the way you read a page — everything at once, all of it, the whole dense interference of a room, from across it, in an instant, like looking at a photograph. She could not do that now. She could not do it across a room. She could not do it at all unless she *touched* something, and then she could read that thing, and only that thing, and only as far as her hand reached.

She tried it on the window frame. Cool paint, slightly ridged where the brush had gone over, a faint greasy warmth where a hand had rested that morning, and — under that, very faint, very far down, so faint that she was not certain it was there at all — the residue of a person. A temperature of attention. Not a colour. Not a name. A *weather*.

She tried it on the bed. Old linen. Her own, four nights of it, a heavy wool blanket that smelled of the room and of her, and beneath that nothing, because a bed is a bed and does not care.

She tried it on her own wrist, and got her own pulse, which she already had, and put the charcoal down for a while.

The whole of it, she worked out over those nine days, could be summarised as: *it is like being given the ability to read a book, in a language, with your hands, one page at a time, in a library where somebody has taken all the light out.* And the second part, which took longer: *it is not the gift, and it is not nothing, and I am going to have to be extremely careful for the rest of my life not to pretend otherwise.*

She wrote that on the back of a schedule.

---

Nai Nai had telephoned on Wednesday. Lyra had not told anybody that she was not eating, and had not told anybody that she was not sleeping either, and had been eating and not sleeping for nine days, and had answered the phone in a voice she had built.

*Con là của con.*

*Yes, Bà.*

*Did you eat?*

*Yes, Bà.*

*Lyra.*

*I had toast.*

There was a silence on the line of the particular Vietnamese kind, which is not empty.

*Nai Nai listens to your Bàba on the telephone on Sunday and he says the same thing. He says *I am managing*. He has said *I am managing* every Sunday for eleven years and I have never once asked him a question about it because I was afraid of the answer, and now my granddaughter is doing the same thing eleven thousand miles from her kitchen and I have run out of ways to be afraid quietly.* A rustle. Jasmine, always, even in October, because she put the dried leaves in her cardigan pocket and Lyra had never told her that she could smell it over the telephone, and neither of them had ever mentioned it. *So I will ask you the question I did not ask him. What do you need, and it may not be a person.*

*I need—*

*Say the true thing. Not the brave thing. The true thing.*

Lyra had stood in a cold corridor at the end of a landline in a student union with a receiver against her ear and a burn in her hand, at the age of sixteen, and had discovered that the true thing was very much smaller and much more embarrassing than the brave thing.

*I need somebody to not be kind at me for about a week,* she said. *Because everyone's being kind at me and I am going to lose my mind. And I need somebody to be angry. Not at me. Just — somebody else being angry about it, out loud, where I can hear it, because I can't be angry, Bà, I am so frightened that if I let the anger out there won't be anything left and then what will I use.*

Nai Nai was quiet for a long moment.

*Yes,* she said. *That I can do. I have been saving it for eleven years and I have got quite good at it. Your Bàba thinks I am a calm woman.*

---

The knock came on the fourth day, and Lyra knew who it was from the way it was done, which was twice, unhurried, with a pause in the middle, because Eli Park knocked the way he talked.

She had a sheet of charcoal in her hand and a bad drawing on the desk and eleven days of nothing on her face, and she thought about saying she was asleep, and then thought about the fact that she had said *I am managing* to her grandmother on Wednesday and that this was a house where that phrase was now a family joke.

"Coming."

Milo was in the corridor. He was on a stick, in a way he was going to be on a stick for another six weeks, and he had a folder under his arm, and he had not come in without knocking twice, and he had not asked how she was.

"I've been told I can't be here more than twenty minutes," he said. "Osei. It's in writing. I've got it in the folder, I made her put it in writing."

"You made—"

"I made her put it in writing," Milo said, "because I have discovered that I now have administrative skills, Lyra. That is what has happened to me. I am going to be a man who photocopies things. I have accepted it."

He came in and looked at the drawing, and did not say *it's beautiful*, and Lyra was so grateful for that that she almost said something.

"That's not threads," he said.

"No."

"That's the space where the threads were."

"Yes."

He put the folder down and pulled the chair round so that it was facing her, and sat in it — carefully, with the stick, in the way a person does a thing carefully that they will be praised for not doing, and then said:

"Right. Two things, and I want to do them in this order because the first one is the one I'm bad at."

"Okay."

"One. I can't hear it." He looked at his own hands. "Any of it. I've been in this room for forty seconds and I don't know what this room is doing. I have not known since Sunday. Osei says there's a chance it grows along a new route, and she says it in a voice that means she doesn't think so, and I have decided that I'm going to be the sort of person who plans for the boring answer." He looked up. "I can play. I'm going to be *ordinary*, Lyra, and I'm going to be an extremely good ordinary musician, and I have decided to decide that in advance so that nobody else gets to do it for me."

"Milo—"

"And two." He reached into the folder and put a single page on the desk, on top of her bad drawing. "Osei wants you to have this before the Council gets here, and she made me bring it, and she said, and I am quoting, *tell her the twelve-week review is in December and I am not going to soften it, she should plan her year around the real number and not the kind one.*"

It was a schedule. Twelve weeks, laid out in columns: review dates, a hearing, a named keyworker, an accommodation, a review of the accommodation, and a line in the bottom right corner that said **reassess: partial return possible; do not plan around it.**

"That's kinder than a straight no."

"It's the opposite of kind," Milo said. "It's *the same as what you just did to me*." He tapped it. "Lyra. You have spent a week deciding to be fine before anyone can tell you that you're not, and you are so good at it that I didn't notice for four days. I'm not going to be managed. Neither are you. If we're going to be a thing then the *thing* is that we say the real number out loud even when it costs us."

They looked at each other in a dark room.

"Okay," Lyra said.

"Say it."

"December. Twelve weeks. Partial return possible, don't plan around it." She took the page. "And you can play, and you're going to be ordinary, and you decided that in advance so that nobody could do it for you."

"Yes."

"That's the most frightening thing anybody has said to me in a fortnight."

"Good," said Milo. "Now show me the drawing, and tell me the thing you are not saying, and then I have to go, because I am counting the minutes, because the folder says twenty and I have been here for fourteen."

---

She worked out the rest over the following five days, alone, badly, and with a small mother-of-pearl box that had been in a drawer of her desk since the eighteenth of August and that she had not opened since she was a child.

Inside it: spools of silk. Red, silver, undyed. Not a magic kit. A *granny* kit, bought in a shop in a Chinatown in a city three hundred miles from her mother's grave by a woman who had looked at a boy of about nine drawing on a tablecloth and had said, *You have the hands for this. Do not let anyone sell you a better one.*

*The eye sees the ripple, the hand feels the tide.*

She had tied knots in silk for years, the way you learn a thing because somebody older is sitting next to you. She had never once tied one and *meant* it.

She tied one now. Badly. In the dark, by feel, with a charcoal stump and her teeth. A plain overhand, four times round, pulled tight.

Nothing happened.

She laughed, which was the first thing she had done in the room that was not thinking.

Then she tied another one, and this time she thought about the fact that her grandmother's grandmother had tied silk knots in Chợ Lớn in a room with a table and a lamp and four people in it, and that the *point* had never been the knot, and that the point had been that you did it in a room with other people in it, and that there was no version of this that anybody had ever intended to be done alone on purpose.

So she sent a message to three people.

It said: *Come to my room. Bring your own hands. Bring nothing else. I want to try something and I want to do it with you in the room and I have absolutely no idea if it is going to work and I would rather it did not.*

Eli arrived in nine minutes with a diagram. Zara arrived in eleven, out of breath, apologetic, with her shoes in her hand for no reason she could later explain. Milo arrived last, forty minutes late, on a stick, and had brought soup.

It did not work.

That was the first evening, and the only thing any of them learned on it was that a knot is a knot, and that a girl who had spent her whole life being told that her way of seeing was real, and had just lost it, was not going to get it back by wanting it in a dormitory room with three of her friends and a bowl of soup.

But it did not work *in front of them*, and on the fourth evening Zara said, absently, without looking up, while tying something: "Oh — Lyra, don't move,"

and Lyra was sitting with her palm flat on the desk, and the lamp had been knocked half off the table, and Zara had tied herself a knot with silk Lyra had handed her, and there was something under Lyra's hand that was not the desk.

Warmth. Not heat. A warmth with a direction.

*Oh,* Lyra thought. *Oh, it's like —*

*It is like the fork,* she said out loud. *It's like the tuning fork. It's not a thread. It's a* resonance *, and it's in the silk because the silk is a thing that was made by hand and a hand leaves—*

"A hand leaves *what*?" said Eli, who had a diagram, and who would not be talked out of it.

And Lyra said: "I don't know. I don't know yet. But it's not in me. It's in the *thing*."

And Eli Park, who had been holding a diagram, and who had spent nine days in a building with a broken arm and no gift and had been quietly, privately certain that he was now the useless one, put the diagram down on the floor and said, in a voice with a crack in it:

"Say that again. Slowly. Because I've been thinking in the wrong direction for nine days and I think I've been wrong about *who* has the hands."

## WHAT IT COSTS

The things she learned in October, and the things she wrote at the back of the schedule, were these:

**It is close range.** Touch to skin, or to metal, or to a doorframe. She could not read a room. She could not read a crowd. She could not do the one thing she had come here to do, which was to walk into a hall full of frightened children and see what was happening to them, and there was no version of this that got it back, and she had better stop hoping there was within the year.

**It is slow.** Where the old sight had been an instant photograph, this took four seconds to five, and then a decision about whether to believe it. Dr. Osei's word for it was *interpretive*, and Lyra's word for it was *guesswork*, and neither of them was wrong.

**It is exhausting and it is not symmetric.** An hour of it left her with the specific headache of someone who has been concentrating very hard in a language they only half speak. Two hours and she got it wrong — confidently wrong, in detail, the way she had used to be confidently right, and the specific horror of that was its own thing, and she wrote it on the back of the schedule in a hand that was not steady.

**It is useless without a hand.** Which she found funny, and then did not.

**It is hers.** The jade at her throat had gone cold some time in the last week and had stayed cold, and one morning in October she noticed that it had warmed when she put her thumb against the back of it, and did not warm otherwise, and she understood that her grandmother's mother's stone had been doing this for four hundred years for exactly one person at a time, and that she was not going to be told about it, and that she was not going to ask.

She did not tell anybody about the jade.

She told somebody about everything else.
