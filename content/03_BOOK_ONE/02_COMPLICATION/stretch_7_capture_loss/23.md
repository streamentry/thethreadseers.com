# Chapter 23: The Healing and the Harm

*Monday 16 September. The Academy clinic.*
*Weave-Quake Counter: 44% instability. Node-failure projection: T+34 d. Rebaselined against a held circuit. The load is off the wall and onto a machine, and the model has never been run on this basis before.*

The infirmary smelled of the inside of a peach and of the disinfectant somebody had put on her face, and what she could still feel in her teeth was crystal, and ozone, and a bell left out in the rain. She lay on a cot with the blanket up to her chin. There was a cold under the blanket that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room, and she had stopped mentioning it on the second day.

Her friends were there — all three, which had taken some arranging, and which Dr Osei had permitted only on the condition that nobody asked Lyra a single question about the last thirty hours. Milo was upright. He was on the arm of a chair rather than on the floor, and he had not once mentioned that he was doing the other thing, and Lyra loved him a great deal for not mentioning it. Dr Mei-Hua Chen was in the doorway, and had been in the doorway for most of the previous four minutes, in the posture of a woman who had decided to be present and had not yet decided whether to be a visitor.

Eli was cleaning a scrape on her temple that she did not remember getting. He worked at it with the concentration he brings to a document in two languages, and he was not looking at her, and his hands were completely steady, and Lyra found that this was the most restful thing that had happened to her in a month.

"The scrape is nothing," he said. "It is what Osei is looking at that I would like somebody in this room to look at." He put the swab down and disposed of it with more care than the swab deserved. "How much can you feel? Not how much you can see. I want the number you would give somebody else."

"About a hand's width," said Lyra. "That is what the eleventh minute of me turned into. I can hold a cup and I know that it is a cup. I cannot tell you whose hand is on it, and I would not have told you that on Friday, and I am telling you now because you have asked me a question with a number in it, which is the only way I know how to be answered."

*That was the first thing, and it went round the room. Eli wrote it on the back of his own hand, where he keeps things, and did not ask her one question about how she could tell that it was a cup. He will be asked, in his own time, by a person with more authority than a cot, and what he says then will matter more than anything I manage this week.*

Zara was sitting on the edge of the cot with her hand on Lyra's forearm, which was the warmest thing in the room, and she had gone very quiet in a way Lyra had learned to read in about four days.

"I am not going to ask you how it was," said Zara. "Osei said not to, and I am not going to. I am going to tell you that I can feel what you are, and that what you are is roughly four hundred degrees of not asking a question, and that I would like you to know that I can hear it, and that you are allowed to let me hear it."

"Say more about the bridge," said Lyra. "Not about me. About the bridge. Eli has been kind to me for about an hour and has not told me one thing, which means he has been thinking about it since the demonstration hall, and I would rather have the thing he has been thinking about than the thing he has been not saying."

He looked at her for a moment. "Your mother wrote that a seam is not a place you cut. It is a place where two things have agreed not to be separate. She wrote that in nineteen ninety-six, and the woman in the archive on Friday had that page in her hand, and you have been inside the thing it describes and you got out. I am going to ask this badly because I do not have a better way of asking it. Do you know what I am asking?"

"No," said Lyra. "Ask me the other one."

"Would you have come out if we had not been in the room?"

"Yes," said Lyra, "and I want it recorded that the answer took about a second, and that Eli Park put his pen down when I said it, and that he did not argue, which I would have understood and which I would not have forgiven."

"For what?"

"For asking me in an infirmary. You will be asked, properly, by somebody with a stamp, and I will answer properly, and it will not be four people I love deciding in a room with a curtain round it whether I am allowed to be a person who went into a machine on purpose. That is not the difference between asking and not asking, Eli. That is the difference between a question and a favour."

Milo turned the cup over. It made its sound at the corner, and everybody in the room heard it, which was the entire point of the cup and the entire reason he had it.

"Some dialogue," he said. "Felt more like two people arguing in a stairwell and neither of them winning, except that one of them was in a machine and the other one was me, and I lost, so."

"You did not lose," said Lyra. "You are the only one of us who can count a room by sound and you have been refusing to admit it since Tuesday because it is not a thread and you do not want to want it. It is exactly what we needed on Saturday. It was worth more than the thing you lost and I would like it minuted."

He looked at the cup for a while.

"I am not going to be able to be pleased about that," he said. "I am going to be able to be useful about it, which is worse, and I would like everybody to stop looking at me." And somebody stopped looking at him, and it was Lyra, and she did it because he had asked.

Zara had not moved. "Lyra. I need to say a thing and I need to say it before I think better of it."

"Say it."

"Yesterday, when you were on the table, the thing in the room went quiet. All of it. Two hundred people in that hall and every one of them stopped at the same moment, and then about four hundred of us started shouting at once because the silence was worse." She had her hand on Lyra's arm and she was not moving it. "I felt you go out. I am not going to describe how, because the description is not mine to give. But I felt you go *out*, and not *through*, and those are different, and I have been carrying the difference since yesterday and I cannot put it anywhere, and I would like it noted that a girl of sixteen is lying on this cot being extremely brave about a thing she does not have the vocabulary for and that I have never once asked her to explain."

"Thank you," said Lyra.

"Do not thank me, I am being *accurate*."

"I know you are. It is the only thing anybody has been this week."

## THE ARGUMENT THAT PAID FOR THE PACT

"I want to say one thing and then I never want to have this conversation again," Eli said.

He had the cup back. He had been turning it for eleven minutes and it had kept him from doing anything with his face, and now he put it down on the floor, and the silence got much worse.

"On the Wednesday morning you did not tell us about the letter. In your sketchbook. On the shuttle." He said it carefully, the way you handle something that might detonate. "You have been carrying it since the twenty-seventh of August. Three weeks. And I want to be very clear that I am not blaming you for that, because on the twenty-seventh I would have told you to burn it, and you would have been right not to."

"That's not—"

"I know it isn't. I'm not finishing. I'm starting." He turned his hands over. "Here is what I have actually been angry about for six weeks, Lyra, and it is not the letter. It is that on Monday, at about one in the afternoon, you had a piece of paper that was addressed to you, and four people in this room who would have been *extremely* careful with it, and you decided — alone, in a corridor, in about four minutes — that the four of us were a worse risk than a security chief with a wrist device and an Academy full of people who did not know what they were walking into."

"I decided I was a target and that anyone I told would be a target too—"

"I know. I know that, and I have thought about it, and I have decided that I do not care." He said it flatly, and it was the first time in three days that Eli Park had raised his voice, and the effect was like a lamp. "You did not have to care. You did not have to consider us at all. That was never on the table. What was on the table was *whether you were going to tell us*, and you made that decision in a corridor, and then you told us afterwards, on the Wednesday, in a common room, when it was already too late to help you, and then you *apologised* for it, which was the single most infuriating available option, because an apology is a receipt."

Milo said, very quietly, "Eli."

"I'm not finished." He was breathing hard. "He cannot hear the building. He has not said one word about it since yesterday and he is going to be twenty-two before he stops being angry about it. And it happened because *four of us were on a lawn on the Monday* and one of us was not, and we all know which one, and the reason we all know which one is that we were *told* we were a team on a lawn on the first day, and nobody said out loud what the team was for."

Zara had gone very still. "That's not fair."

"It is completely fair. It is the only fair thing anybody has said in three days." He looked round at them. "I do not want to be carried. I want to be *carried with*. Those are different and I have got a fairly specific objection to the first one, because the first one means that when I am in a stairwell and I am the fastest thing in the building, I am doing it because somebody decided I was, and not because somebody said so out loud and let me argue."

"That's not what happened," Lyra said. "You were the fastest thing. I was the one who was—"

"Yes," said Eli. "And I want to say the rest of it, and then I want to stop, and I want you to actually hear it, because I have been rehearsing it since the Wednesday and you have been unconscious and I have had nothing else to do." He put his hands flat on his knees. "You did not decide that you were alone in order to protect us. You decided it because you were frightened, and because being alone felt like being in control, and because somebody wrote *ask somebody* on your door and you took the paper off and you did not ask anybody. I have watched you do the paper thing four times in six weeks. You take the paper off the door and you keep it."

"That isn't—"

"It is." He said it without any anger at all now, which was worse. "I am not angry that you lied. Everyone lies, and you lied badly, which at sixteen is a normal standard. I am angry that you did not believe that three people who liked you would rather know something terrible than be polite about it. That is the whole of it. That is the entire content, and I would like it minuted."

The room got very quiet. Zara's hand was still on the back of Milo's neck, where she had put it minutes ago and had apparently forgotten about.

"An anchor," Lyra said. "Something to hold onto when the storm hits."

"Exactly," Eli said. Lyra reached out, her hand finding Eli's. It was cool, steady. Then she reached for Zara's, then Milo's, until their four hands were clasped together in the centre of their small circle.

"If one of us falls," Lyra said, her voice clear and strong despite the tremor that ran through her, "if one of us is lost in the darkness, or overwhelmed by the storm..."

Eli's fingers tightened around hers. "The others will be the anchor."

Zara's voice joined his, firm and unwavering. "We hold fast."

Milo did not complete it. He was turning the paper cup over again and looking at the corner of it, and after a moment Lyra understood that he was not refusing; he was waiting to see whether anybody was going to help him say it.

Zara put her hand flat on the cup and stilled it.

"We pull them back," Milo said — to her hand, not to Lyra — and his voice was rough and it was not steady, and he finished the sentence anyway, which was the entire content of Milo Reyes at sixteen and would be for some considerable time afterwards.

"If one falls," Lyra repeated, "we anchor."

They held on for a while. Nobody counted it.

And it was not a solemn thing, and it was not a vow, and it was not forgiveness; Eli had not forgiven her and he had said so, out loud, and was not going to unsay it. It was a piece of procedure, agreed by four people who had just discovered they needed some: that nobody in this room would ever again take a piece of paper off a door without showing the three of them, and that nobody in this room would ever again decide, alone, what another one of them could afford to know.

*If one falls,* Lyra thought, *somebody has to have been told. That is all a pact is. Somebody has to have been told.*

## THE INFIRMARY INVESTIGATION

*Later. The expanded infirmary, Ward C.*

Dr. Amara Osei moved between beds with a healer's signature extended ahead of her like a lamp carried into a dark room. The Academy infirmary had become a different building. Emergency protocols, four extra beds in a space built for six, a whiteboard at the end of the ward with a hundred and ninety-eight names on it in three colours, and thread-infused walls that had been soothing blue in September and were now pulsing a slow, embarrassed amber.

"Stable," she said, at the fourth bed. "And I want the word *stable* on the record as meaning what it says on a chart, which is that the heart is doing its job and the thread signature is not coming back on its own. Ninety-eight to sixty per cent on visual. Three at total. And a cochlear bleed in two, which will heal, which I am including because otherwise the only thread loss anyone mentions will be the thread loss."

"Thread-blind," said Dr. Mei-Hua Chen.

"Thread-blind. Yes. And I would like it said out loud in a room with these children in it, that thread-blind is not the same as blind, and is not the same as dead, and is not a thing they did to themselves by working too hard in a laboratory." Osei wrote something on the board in the third colour. "That is the third colour. That is what I am actually saying. Every one of these names is going to leave here able to do something. It will not be the thing they came for."

Zara was in the doorway. She had asked, and had been told no, and had asked a second time in a different direction, which is a skill she had.

"Supervised," Chen said. "No direct contact without Dr. Osei's clearance, and not on the first two days, and not with anybody in the first six."

"I can be careful."

"You can be *supervised*," said Osei, "which is a different thing, and I know it is a different thing, and I am still doing it."

Zara came to the fourth bed. Maya Sandoval, third-year, who had collapsed in a corridor on the tenth of September and had not opened her eyes since. Zara's threads went out — Lyra felt that too, the way you feel somebody's hand on a door in another room — and stopped about a foot short of the girl, and came back wrong.

"There's something in the way," Zara said.

"Obviously."

"It's not *in* her, it's *on* her." Zara's voice had gone very flat. "It's a mesh. It's not scar tissue, it's not residue. It's *laid against her signature*, the way you'd lay wire against a wall to keep a door shut. And it's still connected to something. It's still—" She put her hand flat on her own sternum, and then took it off again, fast, as though it had burned. "It's still *drawing*."

"Drawing what."

"I don't know. I could feel it taking and I couldn't feel where it was putting it." Zara's face had gone the particular colour of somebody who has understood something and would very much like to be wrong. "It felt like it was feeding something that isn't her."

The four of them looked at each other in the particular silence of people who have all, independently, been thinking about a sealed case in a sub-level with an inventory number on it.

"There is a containment case," Dr. Chen said slowly, "which was removed from a sub-level at approximately half past ten last night by order of the Council's compliance office, and which contains an object of unknown function that was found inside a housing that was not on any inventory, and which I have been trying to get into a locked store since six o'clock this morning."

"Is it—?" Zara started.

"I do not know. I am not permitted to open it, and I have asked to be permitted to open it, and the answer I have received is that a student advocate will attend the opening and that the student advocate has not yet been appointed." Chen's jaw tightened. "Which is the whole of the difficulty, isn't it. Everything this week has come down to somebody competent who was not quite competent *enough*, and being a corridor away from the decision."

Eli had come in without anyone noticing, which he later denied.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday the Council sits on a vote," said Chen. "On the Nexus Stabilization Protocol. Which was drafted in 1994, amended in 2009, and which every member of this Academy voted for, and which is going to be ratified on Thursday on the grounds that the alternative is worse." She looked at the whiteboard. "And on Thursday they will be told that a machine under this mountain was rewritten last night, and they will be told what it now costs, and they will be told it costs her, and they will ratify it, because it is the only number they have."

"Then somebody had better have a second number," Eli said, "before Thursday morning."

Osei wrote **CASE 41** on the board, in the fourth colour, and underlined it.

"Supervised," she said again, to Zara, and it was not a refusal this time.
