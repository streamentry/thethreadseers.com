# Chapter 25: Infirmary Reconciliation

*Weave-Quake Counter: 45% instability. Node-failure projection: T+34 d. Rebaselined against a held circuit. The load is off the wall and onto a machine, and the model has never been run on this basis before.*
*Wednesday 25 September.*

The sterile smell of the infirmary had become, over eleven days, a kind of punctuation. It meant: *here is where they put the people the building hurt.*

Lyra had been awake for four hours and out of bed for two, and the two facts were not connected in the way she had expected, because out of bed turned out to be about standing at the end of a bed and holding the rail for eleven minutes, and the world being *horizontal* was not the problem.

The world being horizontal was fine. The world being full of a slightly wrong sort of *detail* was the problem, and it was worst when she was touching things, and it was worst of all in a hospital.

He came in at half past three with a chair.

That was the first thing. He came in with a chair, and then he sat down in it, and then he put a worn leather journal on the cover of the blanket, and Lyra put her hand flat on the blanket by the journal without touching it, and felt about six inches of institutional cotton, and a great deal of *him*.

He was different and he was not different. That was the thing that undid her, in the end, and not the apology.

He smelled like a station platform. He had come on the 14:40, which was a train from Westbrook, which meant he had left the house at six in the morning, which meant he had caught a taxi or walked the length of Maple Street to the station, and Lyra knew what the kitchen looked like at six in the morning because she had been in it four days ago.

He had lost something. She could feel the shape of it in his hand where it rested on the journal — a thin place, a place where somebody had been holding on and had stopped.

He was frightened. She had known that for eleven years. It had never once been on the *surface*.

"Mila," he said, and stopped, and started again the way he did when he had prepared something. "I have read everything. I want to be clear about that first, because I know it sounds like a word, and it is not a word, it is five days. I have read everything in the Clinic correspondence, everything in the Academy's release, and everything of hers that is not under seal, and I have read it in the order she would have read it in, which is chronological, because she was a pedant about order, and I have got quite good at it."

He opened the journal. The paper made its dry sound.

"I want to say three things and I have written them down so that I cannot do the thing I do, which is to make a fourth one that is about me."

"Okay."

"One." He turned a page towards her, and she leaned, and did not touch it. "Your mother was a scientist. That is the sentence I have been not saying for eleven years because I thought it would make it worse, and it does not make it worse, it makes it *possible*, and I am sorry — I am so sorry, Lyra — for eleven years of making it worse."

"She drew pictures of us," Lyra said.

"She drew pictures of us and she wrote down what she could and could not establish, and she numbered her pages, and she has *three* pages in this journal on the ethics of taking a measurement from a child without the child knowing, and the three pages are dated the February before you were born, and they are about a *seer of eleven* she was observing, and there is no note of whose eleven-year-old, and I have spent two days on it, and I am not going to find out, and I want to say out loud that I have looked."

"She was watching somebody's child," Lyra said. "She did it to me."

"Yes."

"Bàba."

"I know."

"She put it in a *journal*."

"I know," said her father, and put his hand over his eyes for a moment, and Lyra felt what that cost him, and did not reach over, because she had learned eleven days ago that reaching over is a thing she had been doing to people for three years.

"Two." He took his hand down. "What I said to you in the corridor, and in the demonstration hall, and on the day of the recital, and at the breakfast table for a decade — that it is mysticism, that it is not testable, that it is not real in the way science demands —"

"Her way of seeing was not testable *that* way," Lyra said. "And neither was yours. You had four publications and she had a *thread dimension*."

"Yes," he said. "And I was not comparing methods, Lyra. That is what I have been telling myself for eleven years, and it is a thing I could say to a colleague. It is not a thing I could say to my wife over a breakfast table, and it is not a thing I was entitled to say to my daughter, and I have apologised for a great many things this week to people who did not ask."

"You were frightened."

"I was *arrogant*," her father said. "Those are different, and the difference is the entire subject of your mother's work, and I had it in my hands for eleven years and I read it as a metaphor about rivers. Because a metaphor about rivers did not require me to change anything."

He turned to a page near the middle and put one finger on it, and Lyra leaned, and read it upside down.

> *控制不是解决方案。共融是唯一的道路。*
>
> *Control is not the solution. Communion is the only path.*
>
> *He says this is sentimental. It is not sentimental. It is the only arithmetic that survives contact with a person. A system that requires a payment from someone who has not agreed to make it is not a system. It is a queue.*

"That is the argument," said her father. "That is the whole of it. It is *arithmetic*. It is not about being nice. A measurement that costs a person something they did not agree to is a *debt*, and you cannot have a science of a thing that goes bankrupt every time somebody finds out what it is made of, and that sentence is *in her journal*, in her hand, in February, and he was a colleague of hers, and he read this, and he built a machine that takes payment from people who did not agree to it, and he called it *necessary*."

They sat with that for a moment.

"Three," said Wei Chen.

He did not look at her. He looked at the journal, at his own handwriting in the margin of a page, in a small cramped script that Lyra did not know and that she recognised absolutely, because it was the script her father used on the backs of grocery lists when he was worried.

"I owe you an apology that is not about your mother and not about the science, and I have been avoiding it since the eleventh of September because I have been telling myself that it is too large to say, which is a thing that fathers say when the apology is not large but *embarrassing*."

He turned the page round and pushed the journal across the blanket, and she put her hand on the cover, and got a fortnight of him through the leather — the train, and a kitchen at six in the morning, and a telephone conversation on Friday with a woman in another state who was being, in his own words, *extremely brave about all of it*.

"I dismissed you," said Wei Chen.

Lyra did not move.

"Not the gift. The *you*. From the day you were six. When you drew a thread between two children in your class and I told you it was a nice drawing and that connections do not work like that, and you went out to the garden, and you came back in and you did not show me — for eleven years." He was looking at the wall. "I made a decision in about four seconds. I did not ask you a single question. I took a thing my child had made — something *she had made* — and I filed it, and I went back to my work, and I have been a professor for twenty-one years, Lyra, and I have never once been wrong about anything the way I was wrong about that."

"Four seconds," Lyra said.

"Four seconds is the lag. I *know* what the lag is now. A girl in a lecture hall told me the number on the twelfth and I have not been able to put it down." He almost laughed. "Four tenths of a second, and I have spent eleven years on the other side of it, and the thing on this side of it was a nine-year-old who stopped bringing her drawings home."

He stopped.

"Mr. Reyes brought me something," he said, after a moment. "On the fifteenth. He asked to take a piece of paper to the registrar and I said no, and he said, *sir, I know it is a stupid thing to ask for*, and I said no again, and he left it in my hand anyway. It was the intake form for a student-safety complaint. It was blank except for the date. He had written *is this the right desk* at the top, and put an arrow pointing at the box marked **INCIDENT**."

Lyra's eyes came up.

"He wrote an arrow," her father said, "pointing at *INCIDENT*, at a desk belonging to a civil servant, at four o'clock in the afternoon, on the fifteenth, because a boy with no gift and a stick could not work out that the complaint he needed to file was not the one he had been told to file. He was not filing it for you. He did not know who to file it for. He was filing it because you were not asking for anything, and he was not going to let that stand."

"There was a note," Lyra said. Her voice had gone somewhere she did not like. "On my door. *Ask somebody.* Zara—"

"You do not need to know who," said her father. "You need to know that a woman who has never once spoken to you, and who is not kind to you, and who is extremely annoying, wrote it on your door. And that you took it off. And that I have been thinking about that for five days, and I have arrived at something, and I am going to say it, and you are not going to like it."

"Okay."

"You did not take it off because you thought you could manage," her father said. "You took it off because you did not believe that anybody who was not paid to care about you would turn up. And the reason you did not believe that is a table in a kitchen and a sentence about a drawing. And the sentence was mine."

He stopped. And waited, and did not rescue her, and did not fill the space with anything at all, which was — Lyra understood, distantly, several days later — the single most remarkable thing her father had ever done.

"Do you want me to say it," she said. "The word."

"I am not going to say it."

"Why not."

"Because you did not *do* anything to me that could be forgiven, so there is nothing for me to be forgiven for, and if I say the word then I have made it about me." He turned the journal back. "What I did was withdraw from a nine-year-old. There is no forgiveness for that. There is only a child who has to live in a house with it, and then a teenager, and then a woman. And the only thing available is what comes after, which is showing up at half past three with a chair for the rest of my life."

"That is not an answer," Lyra said.

"No," her father agreed. "It is a *job*. I am not offering you the end of it. I am offering you the next four months of it, badly, on a train, with a bad back, and I would like it noted that I have a very poor record of doing things I say I will do, and that I have never in my life told you I would do something and then not done it, and that this is the entire extent of my claim to be worth speaking to."

"And then what happened," Lyra said. "On the fifteenth. You were at the front."

Her father put his hands flat on the table.

"I was at the front because a Headmistress with a bad back and no legal training stood up in a corridor at ten past nine in the morning and said, *I am going to be in that room, and I would like it noted that I am not a lawyer, and I am going to sit there anyway, and I am going to ask one question a week for a year.*" He said it carefully. "And then four wardens carried you past me on a trolley, and I said your name, and you were not able to hear me, and I have been told that I was told to. That is the entirety of what happened to me in this building, Lyra. I was a man with a chair in a corridor."

"You should have been."

"I know. That is what I have been writing on the backs of pages for eleven days." He turned the journal round again. "Sit in this chair. Do not be a hero. You are extremely bad at being a hero, and every single thing that has happened in this building since the fourteenth has happened *because* you were bad at it and somebody else had to come and hold the other end. That is not a criticism. That is a job description. Mine."

---

The light through the window had gone the colour of the inside of a peach. Somewhere down the corridor a door closed, and somebody laughed at something, and it was an ordinary sound in an ordinary corridor and Lyra found she could not account for how she had got here.

"Can I ask for one thing," she said.

"Yes."

"Next time," said Lyra, "ask me. Before you decide anything. About anything at all. Even something small."

Wei Chen's hand stopped on the journal.

"I will not be able to do that straight away," he said, "and I would rather tell you the true version than the brave one, because you would see through the brave one, and because it is a thing I will have to practise like a scale, and I have not used one since 1998."

"Then practise it on something small."

"What?"

"Something *small*," Lyra said. "Something that does not matter. Practise on the thing where you decide for me whether to have the last of the rice, and see whether you notice that you have decided. And then do it again, and then one day try it on something that matters." She pushed the journal back across the blanket. "Because I am not — I am not going to be all right with you straight away, Bàba. And I think you have decided that you are going to be forgiven, or that I am going to be *all right*, and neither of those is a thing I can give you, and I would rather tell you now than in six weeks when you have written eleven more pages about it."

Her father was quiet for a long moment.

"Six weeks is about right," he said.

"Then I will say something now, and I want it minuted, because you are a man who minutes things." Lyra put both hands flat on the blanket. "I do not forgive you. I am not going to say that word to you for a long time, possibly ever, and if I ever do say it I will not do it in a hospital."

"That is—"

"I am not finished. I am also not going to be *away* from you. Those are separate decisions and I have thought about which of them I am making." She turned her own hands over, and looked at them, and they looked like her mother's, and had looked like her mother's since about 2008. "You are allowed to be in the room. That is what I am offering. Not the room where I was nine. You get the room where I am sixteen and I am frightened, and I am going to be frightened in front of you for a while, and it is going to be *unpleasant*, and you will not be able to fix any of it, and you will have to be in it anyway. That is what showing up with a chair means. It is not a reward. It is a chore."

"Yes," said Wei Chen.

"Say it back to me. In case I am inventing it."

He did not look away, which cost him something she could feel through the blanket.

"You are not forgiving me," he said. "You are allowing me to be in the room. And it will be unpleasant. And I will not be able to fix it. And I am not going to be away."

"Good." Lyra's throat had closed up and she made herself go on anyway, because she had found out nine days ago that going on was the only skill she had left. "Now the other thing. Which is that it is not just you."

Her father went still.

"Miss Vance is in a study programme in Bellhaven because a man in this building did not read a card. Mr. Reyes cannot hear rooms. A girl called Maya Sandoval has not opened her eyes since the tenth. A boy called James Chen, who is my cousin, has been in Ward C since the twentieth with a signature the model cannot read, and his name has been in a building the entire time, and it has cost nothing, and nobody has said his name out loud to a person who is not an accountant." She heard her own voice go up and did not stop it. "There is one boy in this hospital who has to be told I am sorry, and I have not told him, and I am not going to be able to for weeks. And I would like you to know that I am not going to be able to. And I would like you to *not fix it*, Bàba, because you will want to, and it is not yours."

Wei Chen took his hands off the journal.

"Eleven names," he said.

"Twenty-three," said Lyra. "On Dr. Osei's board. Three colours. And one line in the fourth colour that says **case 41**, and nobody will open it, and before October is out they are going to vote on a machine that is carried by a person, and I am going to go and sit at the back of that room and say what I am allowed to say and no more, and you are going to be in the front, and I would like you to be in the front, and I would like it to be *unbearable* for you."

He bowed his head. It was not a small movement.

"Yes," he said.

---

He stayed until visiting hours were over. He read out loud from the journal — not all of it; there were pages she was not yet ready for, and he did not read them, and did not comment, and the not-commenting was so complete that Lyra noticed it and could not stop noticing it for a week.

At the door he stopped with his hand on the frame.

"She is in there," he said. "In the translator. In the room that is running the whole valley on a current that is not paying anybody. Every minute that machine holds, somebody who is dead on a list somewhere is being *carried*."

"Then that is a debt," Lyra said, "and debts have an end, and the end is not me standing in a corridor feeling bad about it."

He nodded, and went, and the door closed, and the ward went on being the inside of a peach.
