# Chapter 24B: The Moment of Choice

*Tuesday 24 September. Sub-level 2.*

> "The most dangerous hour of my working life is not the one in which I do the thing I intend to do. It is the one in which I discover that I have never been the one who decides."
>
> — Marcus Harlow, Council deposition, week 3

*Weave-Quake Counter: 45% instability. Node-failure projection: T+34 d. Rebaselined against a held circuit. The load is off the wall and onto a machine, and the model has never been run on this basis before.*
*Harlow's detention: Sub-level 2, medical wing, ward locked, no devices, no unsupervised contact.*

## 04:23

He stood in front of a machine he had not built, in a room he had never been cleared for, at twenty past four in the morning, and he put his hand flat on a cold crystal housing and said the word that started the end of the most important project of his life.

"Cancel."

And it worked.

That was the part nobody ever believed afterwards, including the four hundred people who read the transcript: it *worked*. The draw schedule, which he had spent eleven years building, terminated in four seconds. The extraction matrix, which had been running continuously since the fifteenth of September, went quiet. The intake readings, which he had known by heart, dropped to nil.

Marcus Harlow stood in the dark and listened to the machine not be on, and felt something go out of him that he had not known was load-bearing.

*All right,* he thought. *All right. Then it was never necessary and I was never necessary and it can be unmade.*

Then he sat down on the floor of an unclassifiable room under a mountain, in a building where he had been a professor for eleven years, and put his face in his hands for the first time in sixteen years.

---

He had been in a locked ward on Sub-level 2 for nine days.

It was a good room. That was the thing that undid him, over and over: it was a *good* room. There was a bed with a cotton sheet. There was a window, which was a panel that showed a recorded valley, and he had watched it nine days running and had begun, in the afternoons, to find that he believed in the light in it. There was a chair, a table, a jug of water, a book nobody had selected, and at 07:00 and at 19:00 a woman named Osei came in with a blood-pressure cuff and asked him how he was, and meant it in the small administrative way that meant it.

Two guards outside. No devices. No visitors except the four on a list, all of them from the Council, all of them from outside the institution.

The Headmistress had said one sentence to him on the second day and then had never come again, and he had thought about that sentence every day since, and it was not the one he expected.

*Marcus, I am not going to ask you whether you are sorry, because you will say yes and I have no way of testing it, and I have watched a great many people say yes. I am going to ask you one question every week, and it is always the same question, and I would like you to get ready for it.*

*What is the first thing you did on the fifteenth of September, and who told you to?*

He had got that one out on the fifth day. He was working on the second.

And in between — in the long, unstructured, supervised middle of it — he had begun, helplessly and stupidly, to *do the thing he had always told himself he was doing*.

He had written to Emerson Harlow's sealed inventory list. He had begun a chronology of the 1941 housing, from the secondary sources and from his own family's papers, and he had found, in a ledger in the reading room in 1943, an entry in a hand that was not his great-grandfather's, recording the removal of a lattice "in unsatisfactory condition" and its reassignment. He had worked out who else in the world had a 1941 lattice, and there were four, and three of them were in museums, and the fourth was under a car park in Lyon.

He had written to Aiko.

He had written eleven letters to Mei and had sent none of them, and the reason he had sent none of them was not courage or cowardice but something he had only been able to name in the eleventh: because he did not know what he wanted from her, and because he wanted *everything*, and because everything was a twenty-three-year-old in a coastal city whom he had located in nineteen ninety-nine and approached once, in a municipal library, and who had asked him — politely, and in perfect good faith, and with a perfectly pleasant smile — whether he might help her find a book on tidal erosion, and who had then telephoned the police, and whose restraining order he had signed a receipt for in the same week.

And then, on the ninth night, Mirembe Blackwood had come through the door of his locked ward with a paper seal and a smile, and had shut it behind her, and Harlow had understood with total clarity that his life was about to resume.

## THE VISITOR

"You are not on the list."

"No. I am the Security Chief, and I signed off your transfer, and I have not been near this room, and I am going to be able to say that truthfully for about eleven more hours." Blackwood sat down in the visitor's chair without being offered it. "Do not sit up. You look dreadful and I have a schedule."

"Blackwood."

"Mirembe. Marcus, we are past surnames." She set a small case on the table. "The Council has a question for you every week, and the question is always the same, and it is always the fifteenth of September, and it is always about who told you. Do you know why?"

"Because they are building a case."

"Because they are building a *chain*." She opened the case. Inside, on grey foam, was a black-silver filament about the length of a pencil. "You are the last link that can be cut, and the reason you are the last link is that in eleven years you never once committed an act that could be evidenced. You severed. You did not harvest. You did not take a child. You built a machine, and drew power through other people's accidents, and let somebody else's hand do the last inch, and by the time the Council asks you what you did you have an extremely good answer, which is *nothing I could prove*."

"I did it deliberately."

"I know you did. There are four people in this building who know you did, and two of them are sixteen." She took the filament out. "And I have spent nine days watching a woman in a good room write letters she does not send, and I want you to understand something about the Council's weekly question, Marcus, because nobody has told you and I would like to be the one."

He waited.

"Five weeks," said Blackwood. "After the fifth question, the tribunal stops asking and starts testifying. Your deposition closes. And you go to the vote, and there is a vote, and the vote is on the Nexus Stabilization Protocol, and every member of the Academy is on that Council, including the woman who asks the questions." She put the filament on the table between them. "So the question is what you would like to happen on Thursday, and I would like to know the answer before Thursday, because I need to be able to promise it."

"Promise me what."

"That the machine comes out of that sub-level and runs in the open, with a Council observer, with a charter, with a stop condition and a name on the paperwork." Her face did not move. "That is the promise. I can get it. It is the only thing on offer. And in exchange you will do one thing, and it is a small thing, and it will take four minutes."

He looked at the filament on the table.

"Case 41," he said.

"Case 41."

"The sealed lattice. Case 41. The object the Council cannot open because they cannot agree who opens it, because they cannot agree who is affected, because *there is nobody left to be affected*, Marcus, that is the entire reason it is sitting in a store room — thirty-nine people, and thirty-seven of them were in there before I was born, and the two who were not, were put there by me, one on the eleventh of September and one on the eighteenth of May, and I know their names and I know what they wanted and I can hear them say it, and there is nobody to *ask*."

"Case 41," said Blackwood, "goes back into the machine. Not destructively. As a *component*. It is a seventy-two-year-old power source, Marcus, it is the only one of its kind on this continent, and the machine is a translator with no power source, and the Council will not ratify a protocol that is carried by a dead woman. If the lattice is not in the circuit, the machine cannot be argued into existence. If it is, everything you and I have built for sixteen years has a legal standing and a budget and a *name*."

"You want to put thirty-nine people back in a cage because the paperwork is easier with a power supply."

"I want to put *two* people back in a cage," said Blackwood, "and I want the other thirty-seven to have their deaths confirmed, and I want that signed by a tribunal rather than argued by a student, and I would like it done by a man who signed a warrant he knew to be false rather than by an eighteen-year-old with a folder."

"They said no," said Harlow. "In the circuit. I heard them. They said no, and I have been sitting in this room for nine days with the two no's in my head and I have not been able to put them anywhere—"

"They said no in a lattice with no power and no listener and no way to know whether the no would survive the morning." Blackwood stood up. "Marcus, I am the enemy and I am telling you this as a fact rather than as a threat: if the Council opens that case, the two of them are released, and they are *released*, and the machine has no source, and the vote goes against us, and the immediate collapse is held by nothing, and somewhere in this valley eleven thousand people find out about it in the worst possible way. And if the case goes back into the circuit, the two of them stay where they are, and the machine has a source, and the vote goes the other way, and eleven thousand people do not have a bad week."

"And the two of them stay."

"Yes."

Marcus Harlow looked at a black-silver filament on a table for a long time.

"You are asking me," he said, "to do to a person what I have spent sixteen years telling myself was a *calculation*, in a room where nobody can hear me refuse."

"No," said Blackwood. "I am asking you to do what you did on the fifteenth of September, one more time, having spent nine days of a good room finding out what you actually believe." She picked up the case. "I will be at the sub-level door at five-fifteen. You have fifty minutes to decide, and I would like you to decide in them, and not to lie to yourself in a comfortable chair and then spend the rest of the week being a better man than you are."

She went.

## THE FIFTY MINUTES

He did not decide in fifty minutes. He had given himself a very long time to decide, over sixteen years.

He sat in the good room with the recorded valley behind him, and he thought about his daughter's voice, and he thought about Emerson Harlow's ledger entry — *reassigned* — and he understood, with a clarity that was almost funny, that his great-grandfather had not written *reassigned*. Somebody had edited it, later, in a different hand, and his great-grandfather's guilt was therefore sixty years older than he had believed and had been sitting in a ledger the whole time.

He thought about a girl in a room under a mountain saying, in a register that was not words, *tell me what is happening*.

He thought about Lyra, at sixteen, in a demonstration hall, reaching up a wire in a shape nobody had taught her, in order to burn out her own gift, because a doctor had told her at four in the morning that her father was dying of the thing she was.

*That is the one,* he thought. *That is the one that is the answer. Not a calculation. A girl with a deadline she invented because the real one was too slow to run at.*

He got up. He washed his face in the basin. He looked at the man in the mirror, who had a scar like frozen lightning running up both forearms, and did not particularly recognise him.

At five-fifteen he went down to Sub-level 7 on his own, because refusing to be at the door was also a decision, and he wanted to be the kind of man who stood at doors.

Blackwood was there with the case. Beyond her, four wardens and a very tired woman in grey with a port seal on her collar.

"Marcus," said Tarrant.

"You are eleven hours early, Mr. Tarrant."

"We are always eleven hours early for you." He looked at the case. "What is that."

"Four minutes," said Blackwood.

"Do not," said Harlow.

He stood in the corridor of Sub-level 7, in front of four wardens and a compliance officer and a woman who was not even his friend, and he made the decision.

"I am not putting the case back," he said.

He felt it go. Sixteen years, and it went in a sentence, and the sentence was not even a sacrifice; it was a piece of arithmetic he had got wrong for a very long time and had only now finished.

Then he said: "And I am not going to let you take it away either, because the lattice has been feeding that machine for seventy-two years and taking the payment out of people who did not consent, and I do not know a single way of opening it that does not kill all of them."

"There's another way," said Tarrant, to his collar. "Dr. Chen?"

"Three," said Mei-Hua Chen's voice. "I have a form. It is a power-of-attorney-plus-authorisation of continued custody, and it requires a person to be *appointed by a living near-relative* of each person in the lattice, and there is not one living near-relative the tribunal will accept of the 1941 intake, and the tribunal's position is that sixty-year absence is a *disqualification* rather than a hardship, and I have been fighting that position in writing since the seventeenth of September and I am going to keep fighting it until somebody with a *gavel* agrees with me."

"So it is the second two," said Tarrant. "It is the two from this year. One of them has been in there since the eleventh and is fifteen years old, and has grown into the substrate of that lattice, and cannot be moved without killing her, and has not been told that anything she says in there is a legally admissible statement. The other has been in there since the eighteenth of May, and is nineteen, and cannot hear, and there is no relative on my sheet and there is no form, and I have left the line blank rather than put a name on it, or a pronoun, and I am aware that a form which cannot be completed without a pronoun is not a form. And I am obliged to tell you, Mr. Harlow, that I have those dates on my sheet and you do not have them on yours."

Harlow closed his eyes.

"Elara Vance," he said.

"Elara Vance," said Blackwood. "Signed the card at the Clinic on the eleventh of September. *Volunteer*, in her own hand, and she was fifteen, and she has a mother in Bellhaven who has been receiving a letter every fortnight from the Clinic explaining that her daughter is in a specialist programme for gifted young people." She put the case down on the corridor floor. "Elena Vance, Marcus, is a person. She is a person with a sister and a mother and a signed card and a statement. And on Thursday the Council is going to be asked whether a machine can be held open by consent or only by the fact that nobody can be *reached*."

Nobody spoke.

"Get me the form," said Harlow.

---

*They gave it to me at nine o'clock on the Tuesday,* he wrote, in the good room, on the seventeenth page, in the space where the letter to Mei would have gone. *A legal pad, and a pen, and a girl of nineteen called Mila Rowntree, who is the Academy's student advocate and who has silver in her hair and who held the paper flat, because I could not get my hand to be steady enough by myself.*

*Elara Vance signed a card at the Clinic on the eleventh. I did not read it. That is the crime. Not that I extracted a girl who refused — she came to me, and she said yes, and she wrote it down herself, and I did not read the card, and I have spent four months telling myself she understood.*

*She understood that she wanted to see. She is fifteen. There is no version of fifteen that understands, and there is no version of a fifteen-year-old that cannot be nineteen when somebody sits in a cage for four hours and is asked what she wants. I do not get to decide which of those two sentences is the true one. I was the one with the machine.*

*Elara Vance has been in a lattice since the eleventh of September. On the twenty-second I telephoned her mother. I did not tell her. I told her that her daughter was safe and that she had been offered a specialist programme and that she would be home in the spring, and the mother thanked me, and I have been sick every day since.*

*I am going to ring her back. I am going to tell her the truth. I am going to say the words* my daughter is a component *to a woman whose name is Vance, because if nobody says the words to her then the words do not exist.*

*And then I am going to sit in this room and wait, because that is all that is left of this, and I have not earned the rest.*

*Mei used to say: some things are supposed to be broken. That is how the light gets in.*

*I have broken something. I do not yet know what the light gets in. I intend to find out, and I have eleven months, which is not very long, and I am not going to be a man who pretends the light is a reward.*
