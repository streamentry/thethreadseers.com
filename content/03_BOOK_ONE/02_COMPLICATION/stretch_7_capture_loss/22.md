# Chapter 22: The Sacrifice

*Sunday 15 September, 23:20. Sub-level 7.*
*Weave-Quake Counter: 43% instability. Node-failure projection: T+34 d. Rebaselined against a held circuit. The load is off the wall and onto a machine, and the model has never been run on this basis before.*

## THE LONG AFTERNOON

There were eleven hours between the headmistress and the door, and Lyra spent them, because she could not stop her mind doing it, arriving in order at every single thing she could have done differently — and because it turned out that a mind with nothing to look at turns itself into a *verdict*.

She went through it four times.

The first time, the note on her door on Monday. *Ask somebody.*

The second time, Milo's face at half past one on Monday, and the thing he had said that she had not let herself hear, which was: *You keep arriving at the answer and then you keep pretending you got there by yourself, and I don't know how to tell you that's a problem because every time I say it you agree and then you do it again.*

The third time, Dr. Ekwueme at four in the morning, saying *I know, love* with such exhausted precision that Lyra had understood, and had chosen not to understand, what she was actually being told.

The fourth time, and this was the one that stayed: the moment in Hall Seven when she had turned round and looked at her three friends behind the glass, and had had a thought. Not a decision. Harlow's needle was already in her and the machine already had her, and there was no time, and so it had not been a decision — but it had been a *thought*, and it had been this:

*If I tell them, they will stop me.*

And they would have. Eli would have produced a reason. Zara would have produced a *feeling*. And she would have had to weigh it against a man's heart, and at some point in the last nine days she had stopped being willing to weigh it, and that was the whole of it, and no machine had done any part of it.

*Four tenths of a second,* she thought, from a table in a room under a mountain. *The lag between intending a thing and doing it. He put it on the board on Monday. He didn't know.*

By six o'clock her hands had stopped shaking, which frightened her more than the shaking had.

At 21:15 the door opened and it was not Harlow.

It was a stocky man in Council grey with a port seal on his collar and the flat, unhurried manner of somebody who has walked into a great many rooms that were worse than this one. Behind him came four more, and two of them were carrying something that hummed.

"Dr. Osei sent for us," he said, to nobody in particular. "Nobody has sent for us in nine days. I want that in the record, because somebody is going to tell you we were slow, and we were not."

"You're the Council's compliance office," said Harlow. "You're four people. I have a warrant and a machine."

"You have a warrant that we have read, and a machine that we have a technician in, and no observer," said the man, "and I have a Headmistress on a line, and Dr. Osei, and a paper that was filed into an unsecure route at seventeen hundred and four this afternoon by a first-year student who has since been identified and is currently sitting in a corridor being looked after by two women from the medical faculty, and who is sixteen, so I would be obliged if you would stop."

Somewhere behind Lyra's eyes, in a part of her that had been doing arithmetic for eleven hours, something let go.

*They came.*

Sixteen. He was sixteen. She had not — she had not even —

*He was in the stairwell with a dead gift and they sent him through a wall.*

"I want the machine off her," said the compliance officer, "and then everybody in this room is going to be extremely boring at one another for about four hours, and I would like to start that process now."

"They can't," said Harlow.

He had turned back to his console, and he said it quietly, without any of the theatre, and it was the single most frightening thing that had happened to Lyra all day.

"They can't take it off her. Look at the trace."

## THE TRACE

They put a screen where she could see it, and she saw it, and she did not understand it for perhaps forty seconds, and then she did, and the understanding arrived the way a floor arrives.

Two traces.

One of them was hers: a silver line, running from the machine's core to the leads on her body, pulsing, and doing what the man had said it would do — holding a circuit open, carrying the load, spending itself. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever been connected to.

The other one ran *beneath* it. It came out of a housing under the console that was not on the diagram, went down into the floor, and returned somewhere under the Conduit's base — and it was black-silver, and it was *cold*, and it was carrying an amount of power that the extraction trace beside it could not have produced in a hundred years.

And it was not connected to her.

She had been in the room most of the day. She had put her gift into this machine and had felt it flow, and the whole of the enormous silver river of it had gone into the *upper* circuit, and not one drop of it had gone down.

"What is that," Lyra said.

Harlow did not answer.

"Dr. Chen?" said the compliance officer, to his collar.

"There is a second system," Dr. Mei-Hua Chen said, from the line, in a voice with no expression in it at all, "which is not on the Phase One schematic, which is not in any Council inventory I have ever been shown, and which I have now had flagged in front of two wardens. Professor Harlow, in about ninety seconds you are going to have to answer the question, and I would like you to notice that you have already not answered it."

"I cannot answer it," Harlow said. "I have never been able to read it. It came with the site."

"Whose site."

"Emerson Harlow's," said Harlow, and his voice did something Lyra had not heard it do, which was to stop, entirely, in the middle. "1941. My great-grandfather. It was sealed when the sub-levels were sealed, and I have never had the clearance, and I have spent eleven years trying to get it open, and I could not get it open, and four months ago somebody *did* open it, and told me it would answer a question if I ever needed one, and I did not ask what question."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Somewhere far off a door was being taken off its hinges.

"You knew," Mei-Hua said. "You knew there was a second system."

"I knew there was *something*."

"That is not what I asked and you know that it is not what I asked."

"Yes," Harlow said. "I knew. For four months I have known there was something down here that I could not read, and I have not asked about it, because I was working, and because the work was more important, and because I am a coward." He put both hands flat on the console. "Open it. Take the machine off her. Open the housing, and whatever is in the housing, put it in front of a warden at nine o'clock tomorrow morning and put my name on it."

"Professor—"

"*Open it.*"

## THE LEADS

They took the leads off her. It took eleven minutes, and it was the most frightening eleven minutes of her life, because the drain did not stop — it merely changed shape, and she lay there and felt the machine drinking from something that was not the leads any more, and understood that her body had been a convenient tap the whole time and the reservoir was her.

Then they had her off the table, and she could not stand, and two of the wardens took an arm each.

And then a girl of nineteen with silver in her hair came through the door behind the compliance officer and stopped, and looked at her, and did not come closer.

"Mila," Weaver said. "You are not authorised in this room."

"I am the only person in this building whose clearance covers what is in that housing, and Dr. Chen has just asked me to witness it, so yes, I am." The girl's voice cracked in the middle of the word. "I'm sorry. I'm not — I'm sorry. I'm not any good at this."

"You are the best in the building at it," said Mei-Hua Chen's voice from the line, and Lyra understood that this girl was Mei-Hua Chen's student, or her junior, or something that did not have a name, and that this was Lin's sister-in-law in a corridor at nine o'clock at night choosing a person to send.

And Lyra said: "Wait."

Eleven people stopped.

"Wait," Lyra said, from the floor, with two wardens holding her up. "You want to put that thing in a box. And you want me to go to a hospital. And in five hours you are going to sit in front of a Council and tell them the immediate collapse has been *addressed*, and somebody is going to say *addressed by what*, and you are not going to have an answer, because the answer is going to be *by a sixteen-year-old who was in a corridor alone*." She got a breath. "And then it will be a procedure. And somebody will write it down the way you write things down, and one day a room will have a boy in it instead of me, and he will not have anybody's name on the note on the door."

She had not planned any of it. It came out the way a held note comes out of an instrument that has been played too hard.

"Alternatively," she said, "you open the housing *with me in the circuit* — and I find out what's in it, and you find out what's in it, and whatever it turns out to be, it turns out to be in front of the Council on Thursday with the rest of it, instead of in a box on Friday."

Silence.

"Ms. Chen," said the compliance officer, in a voice of pure administrative horror, "that is absolutely not—"

"Mr. Tarrant." Dr. Chen's voice, from the line, was very quiet. "I want to record that I was asked to open a housing I have never been authorised to open. I have. I have recorded it. Mr. Tarrant, I have also recorded that the woman on that table has just offered to be the mechanism of an uncontrolled investigation into a device that this Academy has denied exists for four months, and that she has done so in front of four wardens, and that if I refuse, there is a second system under this mountain that nobody in this building can read."

"Dr. Chen—"

"Mr. Tarrant, I have been a civil servant for nineteen years and I have spent all of them being extremely careful, and I am now going to be extremely careful *about this specifically*, and I would like it noted that the alternative on offer is that this Council has authorised a voluntary sacrificial procedure designed by a man who signed a warrant he knew to be false, in a room with no observer, using a diagram from 1941. We will be asked about all of that. I have been asking about all of that since Tuesday, in writing, and nobody has signed anything."

The girl with silver in her hair was crying. Nobody had told her to stop.

"Ask me," said Lyra.

The compliance officer looked at the Headmistress.

"Mr. Tarrant," said Weaver, from a corridor, "I have been Headmistress of this institution for six years and for five of them I have believed that everything in this building was documented somewhere. I would like it noted that I no longer believe that. I cannot authorise what is happening. I am going to come down there."

"Half an hour, madam," said Tarrant.

"And Mr. Tarrant." Weaver's voice did not change at all. "Whatever that housing contains, you do not open it alone. You open it in a room with the Council observer, the medical officer, and a student advocate, and you minute all four, and I will sit in that room if there is anywhere to sit. Do you understand me."

"It will be crowded, madam."

"Crowded," said Weaver, "is the whole of my administration."

---

They were not waiting for the Headmistress.

Lyra lay in the traces for fifty minutes with the compliance officer's wrist against her forehead, because he had done it on her instructions and she had asked him, and because a man who files paperwork will hold a pulse for you if you explain why and he takes you seriously.

"Tell me what it feels like," he said, at some point.

"It feels like being the correct answer to a question I never asked."

"That's the description I'd have given," he said. "For what it's worth — in nineteen years of this, that is the first time I have heard anybody use the word *uncontrolled* as a *reason to refuse*."

"They're about to open it," said Lyra. "Sir. Whatever's down there. It is not going to be machinery."

"No," said Tarrant. "It isn't, is it."

## THE HOUSING

When she went back in, the housing was already off.

They had had to take a chisel to it. It was not bolted; it was *grown* — a black-silver accretion around a shape that had once been a service panel, and it came away in pieces like dried coral, and inside it was a lattice.

Not a machine. A *cage*. A lattice of the same black-silver as the Conduit's filaments, run through with a cold light that was not the same colour as any light Lyra had ever seen, and threaded all the way through with —

*Voices.*

Not words. Pressures. Dozens of them, layered, some of them very close.

"They're people," said Lyra.

"They are not people," Harlow said. His voice had gone somewhere else. "They are — the readings are — I have never been able to read this, Lyra, I have tried for eleven years, and I could not read it because it is not a *dimensional* signature, it does not report in any band the instruments—"

"They're people," said the girl with silver in her hair, "and they have been there the whole time, and the man who is arguing with you has known there was a *room*."

"And I have told you I could not read it."

"You said you could not read it," said Mei-Hua Chen's voice from the collar, "and I have put that in the record in those words, and it is in the record *with your name on it*, Professor, and it will be read out on Thursday, and I have thought about that all week and I find that I am not sorry."

Lyra put her hand on the lattice.

Nobody was ready. That is the part she has never been able to explain to anyone, including the four advocates, including the woman who would eventually write the report: *nobody in that room was ready, and she did it anyway, and the reason she did it anyway was not courage.*

The reason was that the fifth voice from the left was *afraid*, and was not asking to be let out, and was asking — very clearly, in a register that had nothing to do with words — to be *told what was happening*.

Nobody had told her anything. In eleven years of being a case file, nobody had told her anything.

So Lyra put her hand on a black-silver lattice with four wardens in the room and a Headmistress on the stairs and a man who had built the thing standing eight feet away, and she did the only thing in the whole of that night that was actually hers to do.

*I'm here,* she said, in the only direction that had ever worked for her. *I don't know what this is. I'm going to find out. I'm not going to do anything to you that I can't undo.*

And she felt the whole silver river of herself turn over, and go *down*.

## THE TRANSFORMATION

The pain lanced up her arm and she did not scream, which she would think about for a long time, because there was nowhere in her for it — the whole of her was down there, in a circuit, doing the thing she had said she would do, which was the only thing she had ever been asked to do, and which she was doing for a room full of strangers she could not see, and not, in that moment, for her father at all.

The silver went into the Conduit's core and it did not fight it.

That was the thing. She had spent nine days bracing for a fight, and there was no fight. The core opened for her the way a hand opens. The black-silver in the upper circuit — the extraction matrix, Harlow's beautiful cruel arithmetic, twenty years of it — came apart along its own fault lines and was *put away*, gently, the way you put down something that is very tired and has been held for too long.

And underneath it, the lattice — the cage, the cold light, the five voices and then thirty and then a number Lyra stopped being able to hold in her head — did not open, and she felt the machine stop.

*No. Not you. You are not what I came for.*

She had gone into the wrong circuit. She had put herself into the machine's upper matrix because that was where the leads had been, and the lattice was somewhere else, down and under, in a housing that had been grown shut since 1941, and to reach it she would have to go through the one part of this machine that had never once been opened.

*Weaver's rule. Crowded.*

"Mr. Tarrant." She could barely hear her own voice; it was coming from somewhere behind her ribs. "I have to go through the core. I have to go *through* the core to reach them, and I cannot do that and still be in one piece, and I am not going to be able to stop halfway."

"Then don't do it," said Harlow, fast, and for the first time in thirty hours he was not performing anything at all. "Lyra. Lyra, listen to me. There is a version of this where you walk out of this room with a great deal of what you are. There is no version where you walk out of this room. I have looked. For eleven years I have looked, and I have not found it, and I am telling you as the man who built it and as the man who has read everything anybody has ever written about this: *there is no version.*"

"Then I would like to leave a note," said Lyra.

Nobody made a sound.

"Not on the door," she said, and her voice cracked and she let it. "Somewhere it will be *read*. Somebody will find it, and they will know that a girl did this and not that she was made to. That's all. That's the entire content."

"Eli Park," said the girl with silver in her hair.

"Eli Park," Lyra agreed. "He'll know what it means. He knows the sheet of paper is what did it. He'll know."

Tarrant looked at the doorway. The Headmistress was on the last four steps, out of breath, with her hair come loose.

"Madam," he said, "I am going to need you to make a decision in the next ninety seconds and I am going to have to write down that I asked you for it, and I want you to know before I ask it that I have already minuted the student's offer, the refusal, the offer again, and the two conditions attached to it, and that the minute does not say what anyone *ought* to have done."

"Miss Chen," said Weaver.

"Ma'am."

"You have heard the terms. Refusal, coercion, or your own free and considered act, and I am required to be able to certify which of those three it was, and I will not certify a thing I have not seen." Weaver's voice was very steady and her eyes were wet. "So I will ask you the way the Act requires me to. Do you understand what is being proposed?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you understand that you may say no and be entirely blameless and that the four wardens will take you to Westbrook in an ambulance tonight, and that the Council will be told that the immediate collapse has been held by other means, and that this will probably cost more lives than it saves, and that you will never be told how many?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"And?"

"And I want to go to my father," said Lyra.

---

He was on the last step. She had not heard him come in; the room was too loud.

He looked sixteen years older than he had at the recital in the corridor, and greyer, and his hand was on the doorframe in a way that told her the hand hurt, and his face when he saw her on the floor said every single thing he had ever failed to say, all at once, with none of the usual editing.

"She can't take you out of it," he said. "The Act won't permit it. The strain alone—"

"Dad—"

"Lyra, I have read her papers." He was not shouting. He had never shouted; it was one of the things that had made his distance so costly. "Every one of them. Including the one I could not read, which is the one she has never told me exists. I have read them in the last nine days because the last nine days is what I had, and I want you to know that in eleven years of marriage your mother wrote *"she'll need understanding, not protection"* on a drawing of you as an infant, and I have had that sentence in my head continuously since Friday, and it has been no use whatsoever."

"Bàba—"

"So if you are going to do this," said Wei Chen, "then I am going to do the only thing I have ever been qualified for, and we are going to do it out loud, and I am going to be in the room."

---

It took him four minutes and it was the only time in his life he used what he knew, and what he knew was arithmetic.

The core, he said, without touching it: it was a *load-bearing* problem. She could not go through the extraction matrix because the extraction matrix was a *filter* — it was designed to pass a current and take a payment, and a person trying to pass a current *against* the payment would be taken apart by the difference, which was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was why Harlow had told her it was impossible, and Harlow was not a liar about arithmetic.

"But it's no longer a filter," Wei said. "You broke it. It's a *conductor* now — a plain conductor with no payment mechanism, which means there is no difference to take you apart on. He told you it was impossible because it was impossible *two days ago*, and you have spent two days making it true, and he hasn't updated the model, and neither has the Act, because nobody has told either of them."

"Tarrant," said Harlow. He had not moved. "That is not possible. The strain differential alone would—"

"Mr. Harlow, I am a statistician and your wife's student, and I have been doing this for nine days, and I am going to say the thing that I think you have known for four months and have not said." Wei's voice did not rise at all. "You did not read the housing because you could not read it. That is true. But you could not read it because you did not have a *daughter* in the circuit. Eleven years alone and you could not read it. Four months with a fifteen-year-old on your staff and you still could not read it, and the second time is because you were looking at her and not at the lattice, and I would ask you — as a colleague — not to look at her now."

The room went very quiet.

"In," said Wei Chen, to his daughter. "Straight down. Not through the matrix. Through the fault, the way water goes through a crack. And Lyra — the fault is *narrow*. It is one passage wide, because the fracture followed your hand."

"And the cost?"

"Unknown," said her father. "The honest cost. I have no idea, and I am not going to tell you that I do, because you have had four people lie to you about numbers this week and I refuse to be the fifth."

"That's a weird thing to say to somebody who's about to do something enormous."

"It is," her father agreed, "and it is the only sentence I have that is worth anything. In. Go on. And when it is finished, you will be in that lattice, and you will be extremely frightened, and you will find that you cannot tell anybody anything, and you will be *very* tired, and that is what it costs. It is not a price. It is a *bill*, and nobody in this room is going to tell you it is small, because we do not know."

He held out his hand.

That was all. He held out his hand, in a room full of wardens and a girl with silver in her hair and a man who had built the machine, and he held out his hand, and it was the first time in three days that he had been able to do one single useful thing, and it was not enough, and it was everything.

Lyra took it.

And went down through the fault, into a circuit that was no longer a machine at all, and let go of the hand.

---

What she found, in there, is not in this record, because most of what she found belonged to people who are not hers to publish, and because the only part she could bear to say out loud afterwards, to four people in a corridor, three months later, was this:

*They had been there for seventy-two years. Not because anyone had put them there on purpose. Because a man in 1941 had built a cage and had put his dying brother in it, and had died, and had sealed the housing, and had left a note in his own hand saying that he had done a great and necessary thing and that he was sorry.*

*And the lattice had been feeding on them ever since, and passing the energy up, and the energy had been counted, and the counting had become the only reason anything held, and the reason it held was that it was the only reason the cage did not open.*

*That was the arithmetic. That was the whole arithmetic. It was not cruelty. It was a man's grief, sustained for seventy-two years by a machine that could not tell the difference between holding something and keeping it.*

*I could not undo seventy-two years. I could not open it from the inside without it closing, and I could not close it without them, and I could not do it in the time we had.*

*So I did the third thing.*

*I asked them.*

*Thirty-nine people in a cage in a mountain decided, one at a time, and over four hours, what they wanted. Thirty-seven of them said out loud, in words, or in whatever they had instead of words, that they wanted to be let go, and thirty-seven of them are dead, and thirty-seven sets of next of kin are going to be told something next month, and every one of those letters is going to be wrong.*

*Two of them said no.*

*One of them had been in there for sixteen days. She had not been in there for seventy-two years. She had been in there since the eleventh of September, and she was fifteen, and she had walked into my building with a signed card, and the no she said to me was the only answer available to her, because on the sixteenth day her consciousness had grown into the substrate of that lattice the way a hand grows into a glove, and if I had opened her channel then, she would have died on the way out.*

*I want that written down in the precise words, because the whole of the rest of this is going to be people being kind to each other about it. I asked her a question she could only answer one way, and then I honoured her answer, and both of those were correct, and one of them was a horror. I have not worked out which.*

*And one of them could not hear the question at all, and I do not know what he would have said, and I am not going to guess about a man I never met.*

And that is the part that made the rest of it possible, and the part nobody at the Council had a box for.

The Conduit, when the four wardens and the compliance officer and one Headmistress and a girl with silver in her hair got her back out of it, was not a machine any more. It was a long crystalline room that hummed in a minor key, and it was *translating* — carrying the load, holding the circuit, and doing it with no payment mechanism at all, because the thing that had been paying for seventy-two years was on its way out of the building in a sealed case with an inventory number on it and a girl's signature on the form.

Harlow was secured at twenty past ten, in a room with his hands free, because Weaver would not have them bound and Tarrant thought she was right and neither of them told him the reason.

## THE COST

She was in Westbrook by six the next morning and asleep by ten, and the last thing she knew was her father's hand on her wrist and a sound she had not heard since she was six years old, which was Nai Nai, in Vietnamese, on the telephone, saying a sentence over and over into a receiver in a hospital corridor in another state.

*Con là của con. It is yours. Whatever it turns out to be — it is yours, and you do not owe anybody the shape of it.*

The bill, when it came, was in three parts and it took eleven months to arrive.

She could not see threads. She was never going to see threads again.

She could, if she touched something, know a great deal about it — texture, temperature, weight of attention, the residue of what people had felt near it. Close range. Slow. Exhausting. Useless at distance, useless in a crowd, useless in a corridor of two hundred people, which was to say useless for the only thing she had ever actually wanted to do.

And it was *hers*, and it was not the gift, and it was not nothing, and she would spend a year learning to hold the difference between those two facts in her mouth without biting through it.
