## THE CRATER

The site was three point two kilometres north-east of the Academy and it was not a crater so much as a *fold*, and Marcus Harlow had seen four folds in his life and knew the difference.

He stood at the rim with his breath going silver in the cold and his instruments reading sixty-seven per cent local distortion, which was a number he had seen once before, in a garden in Kyoto, in April, sixteen years and five months ago.

The micro-fractures were still walking outward. That was the part that mattered. A collapse that has finished is a hole. A collapse that is still propagating is a *process*, and processes can be told to stop by something with a pulse.

His tablet said: *Site secured. No survivors detected. Recommend immediate quarantine.*

He had read that sentence in a different hand, in a different month, in a report that had come back over a telex, and he had gone on to spend three days in the wreckage with his hands because a piece of paper had said the same thing about two people who were not dead.

He was not going to do that again. He was going to do it differently and he was going to do it now, and the difference was going to cost somebody, and he knew which somebody, and he had written the name down in a notebook he carried and did not look at.

There was a noise.

It took him a while to accept what it was, because it was such a small noise and the microphones on his belt were picking up the folding itself, which sounds like a cloth being drawn slowly across a table.

A child, whimpering, about two hundred metres down the western face.

## THE BOY

He went down it fast and badly, and his left knee took a piece of the wall that would be a bruise for a week.

The boy was wedged where a section of the Academy's boundary wall had come down across a service road, and he was about eight, and he was wearing a coat with a school badge on it that Harlow did not recognise, and there was a great deal of blood in the coat and none of it seemed to be his.

Kenji Nakamura, aged eight, of Hokkaido, and his sister Ren, aged nine, who was under a metre of the same wall and who was not coming out, and whom the site team had found at nine o'clock the previous night and had written *no survivors detected* at nine o'clock the previous night, and the order those two entries had been written in was the single worst administrative fact Harlow had ever encountered.

The boy's threads were a wreck in a way that Harlow had only seen once.

He knelt.

Standard medicine would not work. He had been the one who established that, four years ago, in front of a committee, and he had been shouted down by the committee, and the committee had been right, and the boy under his hands was going to die in about six minutes.

There was the other way. The splice.

It took your own coherence and pushed it into somebody else's torn places, and made a bridge, and the bridge held while the child's own system came up. Nobody had ever established what the bridge was afterwards. That was the part the committee had actually been frightened of.

Harlow put his palm flat on the boy's sternum and did it, and it was warm, and it was the last warm thing he did that he was ever going to be proud of in an uncomplicated way.

The boy's eyes came open. Brown. Startled.

"Who's—" He coughed. "Where's Ren?"

Harlow's perception went out across the fold, fast, the way he had taught it to, and it found nothing, and he had known it would find nothing before he started and he did it anyway because a person who has already lost two people will check, every time, for as long as they are alive.

"Ren is not here," he said. "I am sorry. Look at me. You are all right. You are going to be all right."

"...sister."

"I know. I'm sorry. Her name was Ren and I have written it down." He said it because he had learned in Kyoto that the thing people are not told about the ones they have lost is not the loss, it is the fact that anybody bothered to learn the name. "What's yours?"

"Kenji." The boy got it out and then held on to it, the way children do. "Nakamura."

"Mr. Nakamura." Harlow gathered him up, and the splice settled, and it did not feel like a temporary thing, and he noticed that it did not and he did not do anything about it for eleven days. "Can you hold on to my neck? I am going to take you up about thirty feet and it will be the worst ten minutes of your life and I am sorry about that."

"Ten minutes?"

"Under-exaggerating," said Harlow, and started to climb.

## WHAT HE DID WITH THE BOY

He had him in the ambulance at 07:11 and on a gurney in the Academy infirmary at 07:34, and at 07:36 he was standing in a corridor outside the ward with a boy who was asleep and eleven items on a clipboard and nowhere to put any of them.

*The site report says no survivors.* That meant the site team's report, which was signed, and which was now contradicted by a living child on a gurney in the Academy's own building, by a stretcher-bearer, and by a nurse who had written the time on a board.

He could say the boy was found at 06:20 and let the record sort itself out.

He could take the boy somewhere the record did not reach, keep him, and keep the connection, and have a child of eight who could not stop feeling his chest for the rest of both their lives.

He did not want to do the second one. He was aware, in a distant and unhelpful way, that not wanting to do it was evidence, and that it was the only evidence he had ever had that he was a person and not a procedure.

He filled in the eleven items. He wrote *08:40, child recovered alive from the western face at 06:20, condition stable, spliced* — and then he took the pen back and wrote the name, and the age, and the school, and the sister's name, and the time the site team had logged *no survivors detected*, because somebody was going to read this file in twenty years and they were going to want to know what he had known and when.

Then he did the thing he had been putting off since 06:20, which was to send it upward, and it took him forty minutes and four refusals, and by 09:20 the boy was in a ward with a security officer outside the door and a notation on his file that read *do not transfer without the written consent of Prof. M. Harlow*, which he had caused, and which he had done deliberately, and which he was going to have to be able to explain.

*He would be able to explain it. That was the whole of his defence, and it was not a good one, and it was the only one he had.*

His tablet chimed. Academy emergency response, Priority Alpha: *Report to the Restricted Archive. Council observers in attendance. Bring nothing.*

He read it twice. Then he read the time on it, which was 09:20, and the time he had spent forty minutes getting a child onto a form, and he understood that he had just missed an appointment that was more important than one child, and he understood that this was a *decision* and not an oversight, and he wrote it down as a decision.

*Nine minutes.* In a garden in Kyoto he had been nine minutes late getting to a bench where two people were standing, and everything since had been the correction of those nine minutes, and here they were again, in a different field, with a different child, and he had spent forty minutes, and the forty minutes had not been for the boy.

He put the tablet in his pocket and went to the Archive, and on the way he stopped at a window and looked out at a building in which two hundred and eleven people had been in a hall yesterday learning what a number meant, and he felt, clearly, for about four seconds, that he was the villain of the story.

Then the four seconds ended, the way it always did, and he went to the Archive.
