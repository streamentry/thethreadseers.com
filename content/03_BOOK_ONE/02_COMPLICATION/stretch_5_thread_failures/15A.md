## THE WINDOW

The infirmary had four windows and Lyra had been standing at the third of them for twenty-six minutes, which she knew because she had a watch and because there was nothing else in the corridor to look at.

The first bed held Maya Sandoval, who was twenty-one and in her third year and who had been walking past the Hall of Inherited Practice on Tuesday lunchtime with a paper cup of coffee and had stopped in the doorway to say that the coffee was terrible, and had then fallen down.

The second held Sam Whitfield, eighteen, who had been in the demonstration hall, and who was breathing on his own, and whose chart said *stable*, and who had not woken up.

The third held Thandiwe Mbeki, seventeen, second-year, who had four visitors at once, none of whom could say anything to each other.

The fourth held Ben Ruiz, seventeen, a senior from Westbrook High who had gone down on the carpet in a bookshop on the thirty-first of August in front of sixty people, and whom Lyra had talked to for about ninety seconds that evening without knowing who he was, and who had been kind to her.

She had been thinking about the ninety seconds for two days.

"You will be in the way," said Zara, behind her, not unkindly.

"I know."

Zara came and stood at the second window, which was the one with the better view of nothing. She had been awake for thirty-one hours and it showed in the way she kept touching her own wrist, as though checking it was still there.

"Zara." Lyra did not turn round. "What do you do when someone's hurt and you can feel it?"

Zara was quiet for long enough that Lyra turned round.

"I make tea," Zara said. "That is the truth and I have thought about it and I would like it recorded that I have thought about it. I make tea, and I put it where they can reach it, and I do not put my hand on them, because the last three times somebody who is a stranger has put a hand on Maya Sandoval in this building she has got worse, and everybody knows that, and everybody does it anyway."

"That is not what I asked."

"Then I do not know what you asked, and you should go and be told, because there is a man in this building who is extremely good at telling people what they asked for." Zara's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Come on. Adeyemi has been in that hall since five and he has sent a student to find us twice, and the second time the student was crying, so I think we should go."

## THE HALL OF INHERITED PRACTICE

The Hall was a long room with a stone floor and no windows, and there were eleven people in it who should not have been there: six students who had been sent from the demonstration hall to be looked after by somebody, and five members of an exchange group from a partner college in Kumasi who had arrived on Wednesday for a fortnight of instruction in the practice of their own tradition and who had been in this building when the archway came down.

They were sitting on the floor against the walls, which was where you put people in a room with a floor like that.

Elder Adebayo Adeyemi was standing in the middle of it with his staff.

He was not old in the way that people meant when they said it to be polite. He was seventy-one and he stood with his weight on his back leg and he had a way of holding perfectly still that made the rest of the room feel careless.

"Morning," he said. "Sit down. You are on the stone and the stone is colder than it looks and I am not going to apologise for the building."

Lyra sat. The stone was colder than it looked.

"You want to know what happened," Adeyemi said, "and there are four answers to that and three of them are worse than useless, so I am going to give you the fourth and you can check it."

He put the butt of the staff on the floor.

The cowrie shells on the shaft woke up. Not brightly. They woke the way a person wakes who has been asleep and does not want to be: reluctantly, and with an opinion about it.

"This is my reading, and I want the word *mine* on it, because the Council will tell you it is a tradition and it is not a tradition, it is one man's account of what he has seen, and I have been wrong in public before." The shells drew a line in the air, and a flat map of the Academy came up out of nothing, about two feet across, and hovering about a foot off the floor. "My teachers called the thing I work with òwú ìmọ̀lára. That is a name and not a definition. What I do is look at it. What they called the condition it is in when it is like this, ẹ̀mí àgbájọ, which is a gathering of breaths, which is also a name and not a definition, and if you tell anybody I have told you that I do not know whether the word is old, I will deny it."

The map had a dark spot in the middle of it, and the dark spot was beating.

"Tell me what you see."

"It's hurting," Lyra said.

"It is not hurting. Look again. It is *being asked for*." He turned the staff a little and the map turned with it. "There. The dark part is not damage. Damage spreads out from a point and it does not have a rhythm. That has a rhythm. That is a pull. Something in this building is taking, and it has been taking since Tuesday, and it took rather more on Tuesday night than it took on Tuesday morning, and I know the number for that because the technician from the Council read it out in this room at six o'clock this morning in front of eleven witnesses and then asked me not to write it down."

"What is the number?"

"Forty-three. And when I came in at five this morning it was forty-two. That is not my number, that is the board's number, and the board is telling you a thing and I will tell you what the thing is, because somebody in this building is going to tell you the wrong thing and it will be me, in about four days, when I am frightened." He put the staff down flat on his palm. "The number goes up when load goes up. It does not go up because you found something out. That is not how a machine behaves. If it went up when you found something out then this hall would be the most dangerous room in the Academy and I would not be standing in it."

Eli, behind her, said, "That is what I said in the cellar."

"You said it in a cellar with the door shut, which is worth something, and it is worth rather less out here." Adeyemi did not look at him. "The load is on the wards. Seventeen of them, in the walls of this building, in the stone, and on Tuesday night somebody ran all seventeen at once at full, and that is the whole of what happened, and the number is up because the drawing is up, and the drawing is up because somebody is drawing."

"Who?"

"That," said Adeyemi, "is the question, and I have been in this building since five o'clock and I have not answered it, and if you are about to tell me that you can, I would rather you did not, because you are sixteen and you have been here ten days and I have been here forty-one years and I have not managed it."

## SEVENTEEN

"The model is wrong," Lyra said, after a while.

Adeyemi's eyebrows went up about a millimetre, which from him was applause.

"The Academy's model says the thread dimension is a field. Passive. Something you take readings from." She pointed at the map, at the dark beating spot. "That is not a field. That is a room with something in it, and the something in it is tired, and the reason the number is going up is that the room keeps being asked the same question and nobody has asked it anything else."

"Go on."

"And your model is wrong too." She said it carefully, because he had not been unkind and she wanted to be as accurate as she could. "You have a ring with seventeen strands going into the middle. That is still a *drawing* of something pulling on something. It is the same picture from the other side of the table."

He was quiet for four or five seconds.

"Yes," he said. "Now you have it, and I am going to be annoyed with you about it for the rest of the day, because I have been drawing that ring for thirty years and I did not have a student."

"That is not a ring," Lyra said.

"No. Sit down — you are already sitting. Look at it again. Seventeen lines into a centre. That is not a ring, that is a *net*, and a net is not a picture of a thing taking, it is a picture of a thing *spread out*, and it has been spread out since the year I came here, and I have never once asked who was holding the other ends." He lifted the staff and the seventeen strands came off the map and hung in the air, and where they had been attached there was a small clean disc of nothing, seventeen of them, at seventeen different places. "There. That is the part of my model I did not want to look at, and you have made me look at it, so I owe you one and here it is: I have been teaching people that the load is the problem, and the load is not the problem, the load is a *shape*, and the shape is somebody's."

Eli had got a notebook out and was writing in it with both hands.

"Magnus Conduit," he said.

Adeyemi turned his head.

"That is not in your hall, is it."

"No."

"Then where did you get it."

Eli did not look up. "It is on the building. It is the name on the machine that is under the Hall of Records, and it is on the 1997 incident report, and it is on a brass plate in the sub-basement that somebody took down and put back up, and it is the name of a man called Arvid Magnus, who was a person, in nineteen forty-one, in this building." He turned the notebook round. "The net is his. The seventeen strands are his. The Academy did not discover a thread dimension with seventeen collectors in it. It inherited one, from a man who put it here on purpose, and then forgot, and then wrote four hundred pages of theory about the shape of the thing it was doing."

Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere off the corridor a lift arrived and went away again.

"Mr. Park." Adeyemi took the staff back. "You should be careful how much of that you say to the Council, because the Council will ask you the second question, which is *how do you know it is Magnus's*, and the answer is going to be something about a plate, and a plate is not a foundation."

"I know," said Eli. "I have a reason."

## THE STAFF

He gave her the staff at ten past eight, and he made a ceremony of it, which she was not ready for.

"You will want to put it down immediately," he said. "Everyone does. Do not put it down immediately, and do not put it down in a way that is showing off."

Lyra held it. The wood was lighter than it looked and much colder.

Then the shells lit, all at once, the way a room lights when somebody comes in who is expected.

"Say what is happening to you," said Adeyemi, "and say it badly, because I would rather have a bad description from you than a good one from me."

"Everything stopped." Lyra was aware of the eleven people against the walls, and of Zara not breathing, and of Eli closing his notebook. "Not stopped — *waited*. There has been a noise in it since Tuesday, a sort of — it is like standing near a machine. It has not stopped. It has stopped *insisting*."

"And the pendant?"

"The pendant is doing nothing. It has been doing nothing since the archway came down and I did not notice until this second."

"Then it is a compass and it has no north." Adeyemi held out his hand and she gave the staff back, and he put it in the crook of his arm like a man putting a coat over a chair. "Your mother's name was Lin Ho Chen. I am going to say her name once and I am not going to be careful about it, because I have been careful about it for five years and I have had enough."

He said it. Then he said the next part.

"She came here in two thousand and two and she was thirty-three and she was the only person in this building in eleven years who had written down the phrase *the dimension is answering*. She found it in a sub-basement drawer, and she told me about it in a corridor, and I asked her what her evidence was and she said *none, Ade, it is a phrase, I have not proved it and I am going to*, and then she laughed, and then she stopped laughing, and she said *there is a man in the East Tower who has been looking for this for five years and he has got it wrong, and I do not think he knows he has got it wrong, and that is the frightening one.*"

"And?"

"And she went into a chamber on the eleventh of September two thousand and eight in order to close a hole, and she did not come out of it." Adeyemi's voice did not change. "And there is a gap in the Council's file at the position where her name should be, and I have written to four offices about it, and three of them have written to me to say that there is no gap."

Lyra found that she was standing.

"Sit down," said Adeyemi. "You are sixteen and there are eleven people in this room and five of them came over on Wednesday from the partner college and two of them are not going to be told anything today, and the exchange group has been in this building since the archway came down and nobody has been in the room with them. Somebody should decide what they are told before it is decided by whoever is nearest. That is a job. Do that job and then come back and be as angry as you like, because you have earned it and I have not."

Lyra sat down.

"One more thing, and then I have to go and be the man who says it in the wrong room." He was already turning away. "You asked me what you could do. Do not answer that question today. It is a good question and it is very large and if you answer it today you will answer it in front of eleven people who have nothing to do with it. Come back on Sunday and ask me again, and I will have had three days to be frightened and it will be a better answer and a worse one."
