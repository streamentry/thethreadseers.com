## THE DOORWAY

*Tuesday 10 September.*

"First-years, into the courtyard, and stand in a line and do not talk."

Professor Tanaka had a way of arriving that Lyra only later understood was a technique: she never walked into a room, she arrived in it, and the room had about four seconds in which to decide what it was.

Fifty of them stood in the wet on flagstones. The East Tower was behind them, a dome of stone and glass with a doorway cut into it by somebody who had wanted, very badly, to be fair to everybody, and the doorway was a list.

Chinese knots, deep crimson, tying and untieing themselves in a slow and beautiful rhythm, and Lyra understood after four seconds that they were *wrong* — that they were knots seen from the outside, from a book, by somebody who had never had to tie one with cold hands.

Below them, yantras in pale gold, geometric to a degree that was almost aggressive.

Adinkra, in flat black on the pale stone, stamped rather than drawn, and near the right-hand post one of them had been left half-erased at some point and was coming back.

And framing all of it, holding all of it, running round the arch in three straight bands: Latin script. Sans serif. A classification index.

"The arch has been rebuilt four times," Tanaka said, without looking at it. "The last time was 1954. I have asked for the 1954 drawings and been told that the 1954 drawings are in the 1954 drawer. The whole of this institution's relationship with your grandmother's practice is in that archway, and it is a good archway, and I have never once seen a first-year look at it and see anything except decoration, so I am going to say the thing out loud, once, and then never again: *half of that stonework was made by people who were not permitted to write anything down, and we have kept the stonework.*"

Ninety seconds of silence.

"Morning," Milo said, cheerfully. "Everyone's fine. Nobody's sick. It's raining."

"He'll do that all term," said Zara, low. "It's his whole—"

"I know. It's *disgusting*." She was watching Lyra. "You've gone white. Which bit."

"The knots," Lyra said. "They've been done from a picture."

## THE CALIBRATION

"Late awakeners go first," said Tanaka. "Not as a kindness. Because the field is calibrated against the cohort and a late awakener is the only person in the room whose number is not already in the file, and I would like one honest reading before the day gets a shape to it."

Fifty heads turned to Lyra, which was the single most unpleasant eleven seconds of her life to date, and which she was to remember for the rest of her life as *the moment I understood that a gift is a scheduling problem before it is anything else*.

"Chen, Lyra."

"Why me—"

"Mr. Reyes has been explaining it to you for forty seconds and doing it badly," said Tanaka. "You are the only person here whose instrument has not been calibrated this term. Come and be honest at me for ninety seconds and then go and be terrified in private like everybody else."

---

The archway was cool, and the stone under her hand was cool, and the air going in smelled of a dry cellar and old paper and, very faintly, of cardamom.

She put her hand on the left-hand post, where a Chinese knot was tying itself in crimson, and it was *loose*.

That was the whole of it. Everything else — the light, the density, the way the Maze seemed to be made of a thousand overlapping systems all talking at once — was the most extraordinary thing Lyra Chen had ever stood inside. But what she actually remembered, for the rest of her life, was that the knot on the left-hand post was loose, and that nobody in the building had looked at it in fifty-nine years.

"Ms. Chen." The voice was not in the room. It came from the stone, or from behind her sternum, or from some third place that she would spend a lot of the following week trying to describe to Osei. "The Maze sees your signature. Multi-spectrum, with a strong silver component. Late awakening, four months, which is late. Deep ancestral structure — four lines, two of them very old and one of them in a country I am not going to name." A pause that felt like a held breath in a machine. "Begin."

---

It was not frightening. She had prepared to be frightened for eleven days.

The first chamber had a table in it and three objects on the table, and the room asked her to find what joined them, and she found it in eleven seconds and did not enjoy it. The second asked something about weight. The third about sequence. The fourth was about a thing she had done, badly, in an art room in April, and the Maze had a copy of it, and it showed her the copy, and Lyra stood in the middle of a chamber under a mountain and looked at a diagram of herself pulling two loose ends together and knotting them, and had to sit down.

*It has been keeping records.*

The fifth chamber was a corridor of standing figures, and as she went down it they turned, one after another, and each of them was somebody she knew, and each of them had a thing in their face she had not seen, and she got to the end of it having learned nothing and having felt everything, and stood in the archway afterwards shaking so hard that Milo had to hold her by the shoulders.

"That's four," he said. "That's four minutes. You okay?"

"No."

"All right." He handed her the flask. "Brew made that an hour ago and it's gone cold and it's got a skin on it, and you're going to drink it anyway, because the alternative is that you pass out in front of Tanaka and she writes *5 — deferred* on a clipboard and then feels bad about it for a week."

---

The sixth chamber asked her to hold something still.

She got it wrong for about forty seconds, and then she got it right, and the reason she got it right was the sentence a woman in a kitchen on a Sunday had put in her hand with a piece of silk and refused to explain.

*The eye sees the ripple. The hand feels the tide.*

The Maze seemed to consider this for a while.

"Perception test. Unconventional approach." Its voice had changed fractionally, which she noticed and would later be unable to persuade anyone of. "Proceed."

The seventh chamber was empty, and almost entirely dark, and it asked her to find a path that was not lit.

She almost made it. She got about two-thirds of the way along a passage of patterns too fine to see properly, by closing her eyes and walking on the faint resistance of the air against her skin — and then the pattern *moved*, and the air went wrong, and every thread in the passage came alive at once and shrieked, and Lyra went down on the polished floor in the dark with her hands over her ears.

And in the middle of that, a silver line.

Not in the wall. Not on the floor. In the air, about a foot in front of her knees, at the height of a person who was sitting down.

She had never seen anything like it. Everything else in the Maze was *about* something. That line was a thing in itself: quiet, fine, older than the stone, and going on without her, and it did not care in the slightest whether she looked at it.

"Oh," said Lyra Chen.

She followed it, and it took her to a door that was not one of the seventeen, and the door opened, and beyond it was a room with patterns on every surface and a stone pedestal in the middle of it and a crystal the size of a fist lying on the pedestal, silver, and turning very slowly.

*Animus Argenti chamber. Restricted access. Signature recognition: Chen, Lin. Accessed once before. Eleven years prior. Similar resonant signature. Access window: four months.*

*Four months.* Spring 2002. The months her father did not talk about. The months in which, by every other account, her mother had been extremely happy.

The silver line came off the crystal and reached for her, and where it reached her she felt the thing she had felt in a kitchen and in a school corridor and in a hospital corridor, and it was the thing the jade did: the room got *deeper*.

The crystal was cold. It was also, unmistakably, *pleased*.

"Animus Argenti fragment," said the Maze, and the voice had come a great deal closer. "A silver bridge. Remnant. Left by previous visitor. She was here for one hour and eleven minutes and she did not take it, and she wrote on the wall behind you, in English, and I have not shown it to anybody in eleven years, and I am going to show it to you, and then this chamber is going to stop existing, and I would like you to understand that I am not being kind. I am closing a file."

Lyra turned round.

There was pencil on the wall, very old, very faint, and it said:

> *It is a door. I am not going to be the one who opens it, because I have worked out that the reason nobody has opened it in eighty years is that everyone who gets here has been told it is the only way. — L.C., 4 May*

---

She reached for the crystal with her right hand.

She was two inches from it when the floor came up, and everything went white, and there was a noise like a bar of iron being bent, and the wall behind her — the wall with the pencil on it — went.

"Exit now," said the Maze, and it was not calm any more. "Exit. *Now.*"

The pedestal dropped into the floor. A compartment came up out of the place the pedestal had been — a narrow one, a service one, with a pneumatic hiss — and inside it there were four rolled tubes of paper, and Lyra's hand went in and came out with one of them, and the compartment shut on her fingers, and the crystal went down, and the archway at the far end turned red.

She ran.

Behind her, something very large and very old finished coming apart, and the sound of it went on in her right ear for the rest of her life. The arch itself held. She did not know that yet, and neither did anybody else in the courtyard, and the difference between those two facts was the whole of the next three days.

---

She came out of the archway into the courtyard into ordinary rain, holding a roll of paper, and stood in the middle of flagstones with two hundred people in a building and the east half of a stone arch down across the flagstones in pieces, and the span still up, and the two posts still standing in it, and the passage through it open to the weather.

And standing about four metres away, in the rain, in a dark uniform, absolutely still, was a woman Lyra had seen once, on a ramp, on Monday.

Security Chief Blackwood.

She had not been there when Lyra went in. Lyra was certain of that in the way you are certain of things you have checked. And Blackwood was not looking at the archway.

Blackwood was looking at the paper in Lyra's hand, and then at Lyra's face, and then — and this was the part that Lyra would take into every room for the rest of the year — Blackwood's left hand went, very briefly, to a silk charm at her wrist, and gave it a small deliberate tap with her thumb, and stopped.

It was a gesture. It was not a tic.

"Ms. Chen." Professor Tanaka was coming across the flagstones at a speed that was not dignified and did not pretend to be. "Are you hurt. Look at me. Are you hurt."

"I'm fine."

"Say it properly."

"I'm not hurt." Lyra heard how it sounded and could not improve it. "There was an eighth room. It wasn't on the list. And the board in the antechamber went from twenty to twenty-three in about nine seconds, and the word NOMINAL off the end of it, and then the floor—"

Tanaka stopped.

She stood in the rain and looked at the archway, and then at the board over the anteroom door, and something went across her face that Lyra could not read and would think about for a long time.

"Twenty-three," Tanaka said, very evenly. "That is nine seconds."

"That's what I—"

"Ms. Chen, the wall board has not read twenty-three in the eleven years I have been on this staff." She turned. "Security Chief, I am going to need the sub-level instruments for the East Tower, not the campus board, the ones on the buried line, and I am going to need them now, and I am going to need a witness for the request, because I have learned this year how to ask for that."

Blackwood did not move.

"Chief."

"The buried line is sealed under an order from the Council's own office," said Blackwood. Her voice was level and pleasant and had a smile somewhere inside it that Lyra could not locate. "Signed in 2009. I would be glad to raise a request, Professor. It would take some days."

"Would it."

"It would take some days," said Blackwood, "and there is a form, and I am sure the registrar would be glad to countersign it." She turned, and looked at Lyra one more time, unhurried, the way you look at something you have already decided about. "It is very hot for September."

And she went, into the colonnade, and Lyra stood in the rain and understood two things at once.

The first was that the woman had known about the eighth room.

The second was that she had told somebody, in that gesture, in that second, that she had known.

---

Milo came out of the archway with his hair on end and his shirt half out of his trousers and the enormous uncomplicated happiness of a boy who has just done a thing well.

"**Nine**," he announced, to nobody, to everybody. "Nine chambers. *Nine.* There's a first-year record of seven and I have beaten a first-year record of seven by *two* and I want it written down before somebody comes out here and tells me it doesn't count."

"Milo—"

"I know. I *know.* The arch. Half of it came down." He stopped, and looked at the archway, and at the rubble, and up at the span, which was still there, and then at the two hundred faces appearing along the colonnade, and then at Lyra, and then at the roll of paper under her arm, which she had not realised she was still holding. "What have you got?"

"Nothing."

"You've got a—"

"I said I've got nothing," Lyra said, and heard her own voice come out in a register she did not recognise, and Milo Reyes, who was sixteen and deaf to about a third of what people said and missed none of the rest, went completely still.

"Okay," he said.

"Milo—"

"Chur," said Milo Reyes, and took his shirt out of her hand, and did not ask again, and that was the single kindest thing anybody did for her that entire week.

## THE BLUEPRINT

*Her room. That evening. Lights off.*

The paper came apart in her hands like something alive, because it was old, and because it had been rolled inside a warm stone room for eleven years, and because Lyra had been carrying it against her ribs since four o'clock in the afternoon and had sweated through it.

Four sheets. The largest was about eighteen inches by thirty and it was covered in a dense black mesh of drawings that made no sense to her at all: concentric circles, transfer functions, something that looked like a spine laid flat, and a great many numbers.

The second was a diagram of a machine, and the machine was the archway. She was certain of that within four seconds, because the archway had a socket in the second course of stone on the left-hand post and the diagram had a socket in the second course on the left-hand post, and it had a name next to it, and the name was—

*Animus Argenti chamber. Rim node. Cache: 4.*

The third sheet was a list. Lyra read it four times and her hands went cold on the paper.

> *INTERVENTIONS, CUMULATIVE. N = 11.*
> *Soft load identified: 11. Vessel intact: 11. Perception absent after: 11.*
> *Consent: signed 9. Not obtainable 2 (subject in extremis, no relation present). Not sought 0.*
> *Second responder required by s.9: 0 of 11.*

The fourth sheet was a note in three hands, and the third of them was her mother's.

> *This is not a machine. I want that on the front of it. A machine has a purpose and can be switched off and does not have a schedule of appointments. This is a piece of arithmetic that has been running since 1941 and has acquired an appetite, and the appetite is not the arithmetic's, and I cannot find where it was put in.*
>
> *Control is not the solution. Communion is the only road.*
>
> *He has built the stabiliser around a person-shaped hole. I have found the person-shaped hole. It is not in the Council's inventory. Ask the Registrar. Ask her twice, and then ask her in writing, because that is the only way anything in this building has ever moved, and she is a good woman and she is frightened, and those two facts have been the whole of my life in this place.*
>
> *I am going to be reckless in May. I am telling nobody because I have promised.*

Lyra read the last line four times.

*I am going to be reckless in May.*

April. The four months. Her mother's best months.

She got out her phone and found, in a photograph her grandmother had sent her in June with no message attached, the back of a card in a hand she knew. MEI-HUA CHEN, REGISTRAR. And underneath, in a much smaller line: *For enquiries regarding records, appointments, and the rights of persons under the Care of Persons of Perceived Thread, ask at the desk.*

She typed out a message to three people, and looked at it, and added one line at the bottom, and sent it, because she had promised a boy in the rain that she would.

> *Nai Nai says I have to tell you things. I have three. I have had one of them in my bag for twenty-three days and I am telling you about it in this message and I am not going to read your replies tonight, and if that is cowardice then it is cowardice and it is at least consistent.*
>
> *Eli — I have a list. Eleven. It has a column headed "consent". Nine are signed. Two are "not obtainable (subject in extremis, no relation present)". I want to know what the second one is.*
>
> *Zara — I cannot see threads in a crowd any more, or in a room from across it, or at all unless I touch something, and I have had three days to work out why and it is because the Maze had a copy of what I did to Katie Doyle in April and showed it to me, and I have been sick about it, and I want you to ask me about it in a room, not on a telephone, because I do not think I will manage it on a telephone.*
>
> *Milo — thank you for not asking. I am going to tell you anyway, and not tonight. Station five was about a door. That is all I am going to say about it tonight.*
>
> *One more thing. My mother wrote on a wall in 2002: "It is a door. I am not going to be the one who opens it, because I have worked out that the reason nobody has opened it in eighty years is that everyone who gets here has been told it is the only way."*
>
> *I would like somebody in this school to tell me, to my face, in a room with witnesses, whether that is still true.*

She put the phone face down on the bed and lay on top of the covers in her coat with her boots on.

At ten to two, the board on the wall — the small one, the one that showed a severity and a date and nothing else — changed.

*20% · MODERATE* went to *23% · MODERATE* in about four seconds, and then to *31%*, and held, and then began, very slowly, to climb.

> **COUNTER** — 31% · CRITICAL
> **NODE PROJECTION** — T+13 d, 14:00 *(re-run on partial data)*
> **LOCAL DISTORTION (EAST TOWER)** — 9.1% · elevated
> **COLLECTOR** — 87% of capacity

The fourth board was the one nobody had explained. The collector under the gym had been at four per cent on Monday.

Lyra got up, and put her coat on over her pyjamas, and went down three floors in her bare feet, and read the fourth board for a long time, and then went back up and lay on top of the covers in her coat with her boots on, and did not sleep, and did not take the paper off the door.

## THE CORDON

*Wednesday 11 September - Thursday 12 September. The courtyard, and then the file.*

Nobody went back under the arch on the Tuesday night.

The Consultant Engineer came on the Wednesday with two men and a torch, and what he wrote is short, and the file contains the whole of it. What was still traversable was named, and the naming is the whole of the document: the span, the left-hand post, the threshold, and the passage between them. What was not traversable was named the same way: the east side, the rim, the housing, and the eighth room. The eighth room was not damaged and was not shored and was not going to be shored, because there was nothing left of it to shore.

The reference set was in the Maze. That is why the Council's counter has said *partial data* in every header since Wednesday morning, and it is why nobody since has been able to say what the number means, and it is why a boy's number in a room in the East Tower could be taken for the Academy's.

They ran a cordon along the colonnade and propped the span on the Wednesday, and the timber was called temporary in every document that mentions it, including the one in which somebody asked for it to be called something else and was refused.

The arch was not declared safe. It was declared traversable, on the Thursday, by a man with a clipboard, and those are not the same sentence, and it is the second one that a Registrar signed an access condition on the strength of, in the courtyard office, on the Thursday afternoon, in a hand that is not difficult to read.
