# Chapter 7: The Father's Shadow

*Saturday 31 August.*

## THE CLINIC

Elena Vance had found Priya Raman face-down in a corridor outside the west showers at twenty past nine at night, which was where the first-years went when the field in their chest got too loud to sit inside, and which was therefore, in Elena Vance's considered opinion, the single most useful corridor in the entire building.

She knelt down beside her and did not touch her, which was the first thing she got right, and which she had learned the hard way in her second week.

"Priya. Prija. I'm Elena, third-year, and I'm not going to touch you, and I'm going to talk for about ninety seconds and you can tell me to stop."

"...oh."

"That's it. That's the whole floor. Do you know where you are?"

Priya's thread was a bad colour and getting worse — not a colour Lyra would have had a name for, and not a colour Elena had a name for either, and this was the part of her gift that nobody had ever wanted: she did not read the *content* of people. She read the *pressure*. She knew, standing in a corridor, that the girl on the floor had been frightened for four days and had run out of room.

"Do you know what day it is?"

"It's Sunday." Priya's voice was very small and very sarcastic, which was, Elena thought, extremely good. "Because the food's rubbish and the chapel's shut and I can't work out why the whole building's *wrong*."

"It is Saturday," said Elena, "and you are in a corridor, and you are not the first person this month to have got that wrong, and I would rather you got it wrong than that you decided you were ill. It is not you, and it is not the shower."

That got her up. Elena got her into the first-aid cubicle with its door that did not lock properly, and shut it anyway, and put the light off, and then spent forty minutes doing the single most useful thing she had ever learned to do, which was to count.

Count out loud. Count the tiles. Count the seconds between the light and the dark. It is a child's trick and it is in a book somewhere and Elena Vance had found it in a grandmother's kitchen, and it worked on Priya Raman, and it worked on Elena Vance, which was the difference between them.

"You've had it four days," Elena said, when Priya could hold a sentence. "You came in on the twenty-eighth and you have not been able to turn it down since, and you have been lying about it because you thought you were doing something wrong."

"How did you—"

"Because I did the same thing in September of last year and a second-year found me in a laundry and did not say a single word about it, just started counting, and I have wanted to say thank you to her for about eleven months and I do not know her name." Priya's shoulder had come down half an inch. "The thing nobody tells you — and I am telling you because you will not believe it from a handbook — is that it does not go away. It never goes away. What changes is that you stop being *in* it, and that takes about a year, and the year is not fun, and the only thing that made mine survivable was that somebody knew I was in it and did not make it a moral event."

Nurse Thorne came and went twice and was, as usual, efficient and faintly appalled.

"You don't have to do that," Thorne said in the doorway, when Priya had gone. "The discharge notes."

"I know."

"She's a first-year who can't turn a gift off, Elena. That's not an emergency, that's September."

"Sure." Elena wiped the chair down. "It's September."

---

And then, because she was Elena Vance and because she had four hours of unsupervised access approved by a man who had not read the form, she went along the corridor to the medication console outside Priya's cubicle and put her hand on it.

It was an old model. Scheduled for replacement next term. Less secure than the ones in the main wing, and the difference between the two was a quarter of an inch of bezel and a person who knew which quarter of an inch to look at.

She took a chip out of her sleeve. Non-conductive silk against the skin; she had had the gloves made, which had taken nine months and a great deal of money and a conversation with her uncle that she had ended by hanging up on.

*Protocol Kheredu, item seven. Diagnostic logs — thread degradation and severance — thirty days, thread-blind excluded, self-erasing at twelve hours.*

The chip went in with a click she felt in her teeth.

And then she stood in an empty corridor with her hand on the console and asked herself the question she had been asking for four years, which was: *what are you actually doing, and would you do it if nobody ever found out.*

The answer, as usual, was in the other room.

---

She was the last of three, and the last of the last, which was the part nobody outside the family understood and the part she had never once been able to say to a classmate.

*Elara.*

Her younger sister was fifteen and had a gift like a lit window and had been at a demonstration in May and had asked Elena — asked her, in a corridor, in April, in the specific way that a fourteen-year-old asks a person they have decided is the adult in a room — whether it was *really* going to help, and Elena had said yes.

Elena had said yes because Elena had not known, at fourteen, that there was a question.

Tobias Vance had been a curator in the oldest continuously-operating collection of displaced thread material on the continent, and he had gone into the Kheredu vault on a Tuesday in 2011 and had not come out, and the collection had been in the family ever since, in a room above a garage, in eleven crates, and every item in it had been taken by an institution and not returned, and every item in it had come out of a community that could not afford to buy it back.

*Three cousins. Two hundred years of somebody's work, in crates, in a garage, in a town where nobody had ever heard of any of it.*

And the Syndicate wanted her. That had been true since she was sixteen. They wanted the Vance archive and they wanted the granddaughter who could find it, and they had offered her — very politely, in a restaurant, in a tone of great civility — a great deal of money, access, and a copy of everything.

And so Elena Vance had been useful to two people who were destroying each other, for four years, by being a person who could sit in a corridor and be patient and say nothing to either of them.

Her uncle had used the word *Steward* and had cried, and had been right, and she had said *I will do it properly and I will do it alone*, and had then, at some point in the third year, stopped being able to tell the difference between the second sentence and the first.

She put her hand on the console again, and the charm on her wrist — her mother's, a knot her mother had tied on a beach, which was nothing at all, and which she wore because it was hers and not anybody's — gave off its small warmth.

*Twelve hours*, she thought. *And then the chip is gone. And nobody in this building knows that the boy in the rug and the girl on the floor in Ward C and the two in the second-year dorm are the same kind of damage, and in about three weeks somebody senior is going to stand up in a hall and tell two hundred students that what they saw was a medical event.*

She took her hand off the console.

And then, because she was eighteen and alone and had a four-hour window in a restricted building and no adult in the world who would have understood what she was about to do, she went up two floors and let herself into a room, and started reading.

## THE FATHER'S SHADOW

*Professor Harlow's office, East Tower. Saturday 31 August, 11:52 p.m.*

He was not in his office.

He was in the training room at the end of it, which had a practice field in the middle of it and a glass wall on three sides, and he was on his knees on a mat with a sixteen-year-old boy in front of him who had been at this school for nine days and who had just tried to hold a connection and had made it scream.

"All right," Harlow said. "Stop."

"Am I doing it wrong?"

"You are doing it *hard*, which is the same mistake in a better costume."

Rafi Oyelaran put his hands in his lap. He was very dark and very slight and had spent nine days being frightened in a manner that was, Harlow thought, more disciplined than anything he had seen in thirty years of teaching, including his own daughter's. When the first-years came in, most of them looked at the field as though it might notice them. Rafi looked at it as though it was a document he would have to sign.

"You keep trying to make the connection hold," Harlow said, "because it slipped. But the connection did not slip. *You* slipped. You went to hold a thing you have not read yet, and when it did not arrive in the shape you expected, you pulled, and a pull is the only thing in this room that a thread cannot survive."

"So what do I do instead?"

"Read it."

"Read it *loosely*?"

"No." Harlow shut the practice field off. It went out. The room was suddenly an ordinary room with a glass wall and a table. "You are doing it loosely. Loosely is a word for people who have decided that accuracy is a chore. I want you to do it *exactly*, and take as long as it takes, and find out what is actually there before you decide what it should be."

"That'll take a week."

"Yes."

"That's ridiculous."

"Yes," said Harlow. "It is. Sit down. I am going to tell you a thing and then I am going to make you write it down, and you are going to hate it, and in four years you are going to have it on the wall."

Rafi sat down.

"In 1997, five people ran a controlled draw under a populated valley, in a garden, in April, with a child in the garden." Harlow kept his hands on the table where they could be seen. "Four years before that, a woman of twenty-three ran the numbers that predicted it, correctly, on incomplete data, and wrote the word *fails* in the margin, and put her name to it, and then left the institution and did not publish. Nobody has ever been able to establish whether she was frightened, or negligent, or correct and unloved. All three readings are defensible and I have read all three."

He turned his head and looked at the boy's face, which was seventeen kinds of puzzled.

"She did one thing right," he said. "She was *slow*. She took a whole season over a field notebook. Everybody in that building wanted a number by Thursday and she gave them a number in March, and by March two people had died, and the number was the only thing anybody in this field has ever had that has not turned out to be a lie." He put one finger on the table. "You have nine days. You are going to be here four years. You have exactly the amount of time available to be slow, and it is *all* of it, and you will spend it on gossip if I let you, and gossip is what people do here when they are frightened and there is nothing else to do with their hands."

"...so read it loosely."

"*Exactly*," said Harlow. "Write it down. And put the date on it, and the date is the important part, because in ten years the only thing that will have saved your life is being able to prove that you were careful on a particular afternoon."

Rafi wrote it down. He wrote it down carefully, with the pen held wrong, and it took him four minutes, and Harlow let it.

And then, halfway through, the boy looked up.

"Professor — my abuela says the field is an instrument. She says you only get to calibrate an instrument, you don't get to *argue* with it."

"Yes."

"And she says the ones who argue with instruments end up in the newspapers."

"Your abuela," said Harlow, "is describing my entire discipline and is not aware that it is a compliment, and I would like you to remember that she was right and that I said so, because there is a document coming in November and I am going to have to be read to a committee, and I would like one witness."

---

He walked Rafi out to the dormitory at half past midnight and stood at the bottom of the stairs in the rain, and the boy's footsteps went up, and he stood there a good deal longer than a man standing in rain requires.

There was a version of this where he told them. There was a version where he told the whole first-year cohort, in a hall, in September, and watched the room go cold. He had in fact drafted it, in March, in the blue book, and the draft was four pages long and the fourth page was the reason he had never sent it, and the fourth page was a paragraph about a girl of fifteen.

Her name was Elara Vance. She was fifteen and in the first-year group and she had walked into his office in April and asked him a direct question and had been, for four months, the best student he had had in nine years, and her uncle was a curator who had gone into a vault in 2011 and had not come out, and the family had a collection of two centuries in eleven crates, and the Syndicate had offered that girl a great deal of money for a list.

*The only person who has ever been inside that vault is a man who is not a member of this family and who has been extremely careful, and I do not know his name, and I have not asked, and Elena Vance has never told me, and I have not asked her either, and I would like the Committee to understand that I have not asked her.*

He had four months of her. He had the fourth page. He had a machine that needed a resonance and eleven years of not asking anybody for help, and a boy in a corridor on a Saturday who had said *I'm not going to ask to see it* and had known, somehow, that there was a piece of paper.

*Two of them,* he thought, on the stairs, in the rain. *Two people in this building under eighteen who have found out that the adults are running something, and both of them have decided to manage it themselves, and they have both done it in the last four days, and I did the second one.*

He got back up to his office at ten to one and there were two things on the desk.

The first was a message from the clinic that had been routed oddly, via a records clerk, flagged *family*:

> *CHEN, W. — patient ambulatory with assistance. Asks re: transfer. Attending advises strongly against travel. Patient has been informed. Patient has noted that he has been informed.*

The second was a request, from a third-year named Vance, E., for four hours of unsupervised access to the restricted stacks, approved by him at six o'clock that evening without reading the form, and sitting on his desk now with a tick in the box next to *supervisor present: N*.

Not N.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he opened a drawer, and took out the blue book, and turned to a page near the back where he kept a list he had never shown anybody, and at the top of it he wrote a date, and under the date he wrote a name, and then he wrote a second line:

> *14 Sept. Wei Chen. Westbrook. Permitted or not permitted — decide, and write down the reason either way, because in November I am going to be asked and I am not going to remember.*

He closed the book.

*The problem with a method,* he thought, *is that it is very good at everything except the one thing it is for.*
