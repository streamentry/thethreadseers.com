# Chapter 6: The Weight of Memory

> "The cruelest thing about arithmetic is that it is right. It was right in 1993. It has been right every day since. The only variable that ever moved was me."
>
> — Marcus Harlow, private journal, entry 2,206

*Harlow's office, East Tower. Saturday 31 August, 11:47 p.m.*

The security monitors showed him the rug for the ninth time.

That was the thing he could not get past, and he had tried, and he had a bottle of something in the bottom drawer that he had been not-drinking from for eleven years. The rug was Persian. Ben Ruiz had landed on it diagonally, left shoulder first, and the pattern under his face had been — and here was the part that would keep him awake — *a palm tree*, and Harlow had known that, had known it while he was crossing the room, and had thought, in the middle of the most dangerous ninety seconds of the last four years, *that's unfortunate.*

Not *that's a boy.* Not *that's somebody's son.* A carpet.

He shut the monitors off and sat in the dark with his hands flat on the desk, and the desk was cold, and the silver in his arms did what it always did at night, which was to be quieter than the rest of him and more honest.

Right up to the elbow now. On Friday it had been at the wrist. He had a chart for it, in his own hand, in a book that was not in the building, and the line on the chart was not a curve any more. It was a straight line with a date on it.

*Six months*, he had written in March. *Possibly five. It does not matter; I have what I need and I have four years.*

It was August. He did not adjust the figure. He had found that he could hold the number perfectly still only by not looking at it, and that he was looking at it every day, and that on the days he looked at it he was a man doing arithmetic on himself, and there was no version of that man in which the arithmetic improved.

---

He had three folders out. He had not opened the third.

The first was Ben Ruiz. Seventeen months of assessment, a signed consent form from a mother who had signed it in a hurry, a velocity report and a resonance band in the low twenties, a boy who read people the way other boys read football, a voice that went flat and young when he came up off a floor, and a single instruction in his own hand at the bottom of page four of the consent, which was *I want to be told everything, and if I am told everything I am going to ask you questions you will not like, and that is the deal, and I am seventeen and I am allowed to make it. *Resonance and a line on a chart that had gone flat on the eighteenth of August, and beside all of it, in his own handwriting, the sentence he had written in the margin and had not shown to anybody:

*This is the one I will not be able to carry.*

Because that was the honest arithmetic, and nobody ever wanted it. Eleven interventions, nine clean, two that had left marks, and the eleven was a list in a book in a drawer, and the list was not a list of triumphs. It was a list of *substitutions.* In each case a young person had been carrying a load that the vessel was not built for, and the load had found a soft place, and a soft place is not a fault in a boy; it is a fact about a boy, and it is discoverable, and the discovery is cheap.

Eleven times he had found a soft place in a person and cut the wire that was pulling on it, and eleven times the boy or the girl had come up off the floor and said *sorry* — which was the detail that undid him, every time, without fail, in front of witnesses: the apology. The load did not apologise. The boy apologised, for having been a soft place.

He had read the Authority's section nine on Sunday evening. He had read it eleven times over four years and had found, as he had known he would find, a line in it about the consent of a subject in extremis, and he had found, as he had also known he would find, that the line did not say what he needed it to say, because the line said *where practicable* and nothing in the world was practicable at Rug Two.

He had cut a boy in front of sixty-one people with no second responder and had known exactly what the file would say, and had done it anyway, and would do it again, and the reason he would do it again was the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was correct, and he had made his peace with that a long time ago in the specific sense that he had stopped pretending it was a peace and started calling it a *method*.

---

The second folder was the Council.

Eleven years. A committee that met, and a classification system with a *priority number* on it, and a number he had argued with since the spring of 2002, when a woman of thirty-three had stood up in a lecture hall in this building and asked who decided, and had been told *a committee, of which I am one*, and had believed it the way you believe anything a person in a building is saying about a building.

He had believed her.

*That is the part,* he thought, *that is the part I will have to say out loud on Thursday, and I have practised it four times and I still cannot get through it. Not: they did it to her. They did not do it to her. I was on the committee. I signed the classification schedule in 2009 that put a triage number next to her daughter's name in a drawer, because in 2009 I believed I was the man who would use it to keep her safe, and I have never once in eleven years written anything in that drawer that was not a warning.*

Lin Chen had been thirty-three. She had been, in the four months she spent in this building, the most frighteningly competent person he had ever worked with, and he had argued with her in front of eleven people about a method, and lost, and she had gone home, and six years later she had gone into a chamber on her own and had not come out of it, and he had written a report about that in the passive voice for two pages.

He still had the report. It was in the folder. He had taken it out perhaps four times in five years, and he had never once been able to get past the second paragraph, and the second paragraph said: *The subject was known to be at elevated risk and was not accompanied.*

Twelve words. He had signed it.

---

The third folder he did not open.

It had a photograph in it and it had been in the drawer since 1999 and the drawer had a lock on it, and there was a reason for the lock, and the reason was that the last four times he had opened this folder he had sat in this chair afterwards for a length of time he could not account for.

He knew what was in it. He had looked at it every night for fourteen years. He did not need to look at it again tonight, and looking at it again tonight would not make him any more able to be the man he needed to be in about nine hours, because in about nine hours he was going to have to sit across a table from a boy of sixteen and say the word *severance* out loud and mean it, and there is no way of doing that with a photograph of a seven-year-old in the front of your mind, and he had learned that in 1999 the hard way.

So: April. The garden. Aiko with a book she had not opened. Mei, seven, with cherry blossoms in her hair, because it was the second week of April and there had been a festival, and both of them had been within forty metres of him when the floor came up, and Mei had *walked towards it*, which he would be told about for the rest of his life by people who were not there.

*Just like her mother*, he thought, and then, in the dark, at his own desk, sixteen years later: *No. I am going to stop doing that. I am going to stop finding her in every frightened girl, because that is how a man ends up treating a person as a *use*, and I have been treating a person as a use since 1997, and the woman I did it to first was the best of us, and she has been gone for six years and she is not here to be angry with me about it.*

Aiko had died in the spring of 1999, of the injuries, in a bed in Kyoto, with her hand on the side of the cot where a seven-year-old had been sitting two days earlier. That was a fact he had carried for fourteen years and had never once used, not in a report, not in a justification, not in a conversation at four in the morning with himself; and the reason he had never used it was that it was not *evidence*, it was a *reason*, and reasons are what you have when the evidence has run out.

Mei was twenty-three. She was in Portland, or had been in Portland, or was in one of three other places that the last four years had given him. He knew that she was alive. He knew it because the woman who had come to him in 2002, the *recovery* team, had told him so, in the specific tone of people delivering good news they had been asked to deliver, and had then, in the same conversation, told him that the memory work on his daughter was complete and irreversible, and had not used the word *grief*, and had watched him to see whether he would use it.

Aiko had been thirty-two. Mei had been seven. Two colleagues had died at the console. That was five people in the report and he had been the one who wrote the report.

He put both hands flat on the desk.

*Right. Here is what happens in about nine hours.*

He had a card in his drawer that he had been carrying since the sixteenth of August, and it was a proposal, and it was genuinely good, and he had checked it eleven times, and he had never once written down what it would cost.

He wrote it down now, because it was half past midnight and because the woman who had taught him that a number on its own is a thing with no weight in it was six years dead and he had promised her, in a kitchen in this city, on a Sunday, in the voice he used for promises he intended to keep.

*What it will cost: a child of sixteen loses a part of herself that nobody can predict, cannot reverse, and was not consulted. She will be alive. Her father will be alive. I will be the man who did it and I will be the man who told her the price first, and those are not the same man, and the second one is the one I am going to have to be.*

He read it back.

He then wrote, underneath: *and I will not sleep tonight, and I have not slept properly since the eleventh of September five years ago, and that is a cost too, and I have been keeping it in a different column.*

---

He got up at ten past one, and put the folders away, and did not open the third.

The security desk had left two things for him, which was normal, because Ines Blackwood ran the desk better than anybody he had ever worked with and he had been systematically ruining her life for four years in order to keep it running.

The first was a request from a third-year, Elena Vance, for four hours of unsupervised access to the restricted stacks on the grounds that she was writing a comparative paper. The form was filled in correctly, which was the frightening part; Elena Vance filled in forms the way other people breathe.

The second was a medical line from Westbrook Neurological, relayed by the campus clinic, and it was flagged *family* because Wei Chen had given his number as the family contact and had not given anybody else's, and the flag had been set by a records clerk who was doing her job.

> *CHEN, W. — 31 Aug, 21:40. Re: V. (file C-2214). The outside file note of 15 Aug uses a term I do not recognise and have queried twice. I have had no reply. He has asked me this evening whether the school sends anybody to him or expects him to travel, and when I told him the school does not travel he said, and I am quoting, "Then I will go." He is not a fool. He has understood that he is a data point, and he has decided to be one at close range. Recommend somebody from that institution telephone this ward before the end of the week. I am not chasing it. I have chased it twice.*

Harlow read it four times.

*Then I will go.*

He sat in the dark in a building full of sleeping children and thought about a man of fifty-three with a failing heart and eleven years of silence, saying that sentence to a nurse on a telephone, on a Saturday night, about a Saturday's conversation in a bookshop, three hundred miles away.

He thought: *he is going to drive here. He will be in the car in the morning. He will get here in a fortnight, on a Monday, with a bag and a folder and the specific expression of a man who has read something in a woman's notebook and been frightened by it, and he will want to be useful, and I will have to decide in about ninety seconds whether to let him.*

He thought: *if I let him, he will read the machine. He is a mathematician. He is the only person in this building who is going to be able to read the machine, because the machine is not a thread and it is not a current and it does not have a *thread signature*, it has a *transfer function*, and it will be sitting in a room four hundred metres from where he is going to be standing, and nobody else in the building has ever written one down.*

He thought, with a clarity that was almost physical: *and in eleven years I have not once permitted myself to be helped, and I have built a machine on the strength of that, and the machine has been holding an open circuit on nothing since 1997 and the wall board says nine days, and there is a man with a heart condition coming here on Monday who would read it in an afternoon, and I am going to have to decide whether I am the man who stops him at the gate.*

He turned the light off.

"Harlow," he said, to the room, in the voice of a man doing a very long sum. "You have fourteen years of not asking. It is now or never and the arithmetic does not care."

Then he went home, and did not sleep, and did not open the third folder, and did not turn the pendant over, because it was a crane folded out of silver thread-paper by a seven-year-old's hands in the second week of April, and he had worn it for sixteen years, and it was the only object in his possession that was not a tool.
