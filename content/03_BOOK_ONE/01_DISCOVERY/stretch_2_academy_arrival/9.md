## THE LECTURE

*The next morning. Thursday, 5 September. Lumina Lecture Hall.*

The hall felt wrong to her now, which was a thing she could not have told anyone about a week ago. On her first day it had been a large room. It was still a large room. But she had spent one day in it, learning that the stone under the floor carried thread the way a riverbed carries a current, and that the tall keystone above the arch was threaded so densely it had a hum, and that Professor Armitage's introductory jokes landed in that hum and came out changed.

She knew the room. That was the problem. You cannot sit in a lecture and also be in the walls.

Professor Armitage was not his usual ebulligent self. He introduced the afternoon's session in the tone of a man reading a weather forecast he did not believe.

"—no lecture notes. Professor Harlow has asked us to work from first principles. His work after Kyoto is not on the reading list. It should be. It isn't, and I have raised that four times." Armitage's eyes went briefly to the back of the room and came away. "He has asked us not to take notes."

"Sir," somebody said, with the specific optimism of a first-year.

"I have asked him three times. He has asked us four times. Three days into the term, Ms. Chen, and already the two of you are the only people in this hall who have noticed that the number on the board has not moved."

A low murmur. Lyra had noticed. She had been looking at it in the station, in the queue for the hall allocation, in the refectory, on the way back from the refectory. Seventeen on the train. Seventeen yesterday. Eighteen this morning, and the model had shaved two hours off the forecast without saying why, and nobody in the year group had reacted to that at all, because they did not know that the two hours was the news.

She had a folded piece of rice paper in her sketchbook that somebody very dangerous had lost, and she had told nobody, and she had told herself four separate times that she would tell somebody today.

She had not told anybody.

Harlow came out.

She had braced for something. She had braced, since the train, for the ozone and the jasmine and the particular quality of attention that came off him like a hand laid flat on the back of her neck. What she had not braced for was that he looked ordinary. Grey suit, no jewellery except the small paper crane at his throat. He set a sheaf of notes on the lectern and squared them, and the gesture was so exactly like her father's that her chest hurt.

He did not look at her. That was the other thing she had braced for, and it did not happen either. He looked at the whole room, evenly, the way a lecturer does who has decided to be fair.

"Threads," he said, "are not a metaphor."

He let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to.

"They are a medium. They have properties. They carry, they attenuate, they interfere, they can be induced, they can be damped, and — this is the part your textbooks are coy about — they respond to intention with a measurable lag of four to nine tenths of a second. Four tenths. The time it takes to flinch."

He wrote on the board without turning around.

`AN INTENT IS NOT A CAUSE. IT IS A DELAYED CAUSE.`

"That number is the most important thing I will teach you this year. Every disaster in our institutional memory traces back to somebody who believed that wanting a thing hard enough was the same as doing it. The gap between those two is four tenths of a second, and in four tenths of a second a city can be opened."

Somewhere behind her, a student laughed, uneasily, at somebody else's joke.

"I want to be precise about what I am not saying. I am not saying measurement is cruelty. I am saying that a discipline which can count things and a discipline which is frightened of counting things are not two points of balance — they are one point and no point, and the no point is where the bodies go."

*He's talking about Kyoto.* Lyra's hand had closed around the edge of the bench. *He is standing in front of two hundred people and talking about Kyoto and not saying Kyoto.*

"You are, I am told, unsettled by the quantitative framing." His gaze came up. It found her. "So let us test it."

He did not name her. He did not need to; in a first-year lecture there is precisely one person who has filed a written objection.

"Some of you perceive texture where others perceive colour. Some perceive temperature. Some perceive sound." He said it lightly, as though he were describing a preference in a restaurant. "I am told the plurality here is unusually high. Very well. Question: does the fact that your perception is a *kind* of texture establish that the thing you are perceiving is *only* texture?"

Milo, two seats along, had his hand up. "Professor — is that the same as asking whether the medium is the message?"

"It is a prettier version of the same question, and I like Mr. Reyes for asking it, and the answer to both is no." Harlow set down his chalk. "The medium is the message in the sense that you cannot get the message out of the medium by standing further back. But the medium is not the *whole* of what is being said. A nerve does not know that it is a nerve. It knows heat."

Eli's hand went up. "Then what is the Observer Effect doing in our first-year texts? Because at minute forty of the syllabus it says—"

"Minute forty of the syllabus says that a sufficiently sensitive instrument changes the system it measures. That is *true*. It is also the most useful sentence in your year and the most dangerous, and I will spend an entire afternoon on it in three weeks and I will spend it on the difference between *a measurement being taken* and *a decision being made about a person on the basis of a measurement*." He looked around the room. "Write that down. You may write that down."

Two hundred pens.

"Final matter, and then questions."

He returned to the board. The hall leaned in.

"The failures in our records are not failures of imagination. They are failures of arithmetic. Kyoto was not a surprise. It was a *projection*, run in 1993, correctly, on incomplete data, and it said that a nexus under a populated valley, held above tolerance by a controlled draw, will fail catastrophically when the draw is interrupted and the lag is longer than the reserve." He wrote it. "Nine years later the draw was interrupted, by a blade, in a garden, for the reasons that blades in gardens are for. The projection was right. It was right in 1993. The only person who did not believe it was me."

Nobody moved.

"So when I tell you that an acceptable attrition rate of two to three percent becomes a moral imperative when the alternative is global collapse —" and here his voice did the thing Lyra had felt through three walls and a floor, the small drop into something much colder and much more sincere — "I am not being cold. I am being the third person to run the numbers, and the second of us to be believed, and I would like very much for this generation to be the first for whom being believed is not a precondition of being reasonable."

He stepped back from the board.

"Questions."

The silence lasted a beat and a half.

Eli nudged her. She had not noticed she had stopped breathing. "Your observation," he said, low, urgent. "The thing you said to Ms. Rivera. The *feel* of it. Ask him."

She put her hand up. It felt like putting a hand into a fire and then finding the fire was on her side.

"Professor Harlow. You said a measurement is not the same as a decision made about a person on the basis of a measurement. But the Animus Argenti classification is a measurement." She heard her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "It's in my file. It's on the station board's sibling — it's in the intake system, and it has a category, and the category has a *priority number* attached to it, and priority numbers are for triage." She swallowed. "Who decides which measurements get a priority number, and are they told the number exists?"

She heard her own pulse in her ears. She heard, distinctly, Milo make a small injured noise beside her — he could not hear a pulse; the fact that he had made that noise anyway told her what his face was doing.

Harlow looked at her for perhaps a second and a half longer than a lecturer needs to.

"Miss Chen." He inclined his head. Not a hawk this time. Something more careful. "That is a very good question and I am going to answer it in a way that is administratively useless to you on purpose. The answer is: a committee, of which I am one, and which meets, and which I have argued with for eleven years, and which I have never once won. That is the truthful answer. It is also, I expect, a more useful one to you than any other answer I could have given in this room."

*Eleven years.* The number landed somewhere in her chest and stayed there.

"And on the classification itself: a priority number is a *triage instrument*. It was designed, in 1999, for a collapse in the Pacific Northwest, to decide who receives a scarce stabilising agent first. It was never intended to order people. It has been used to order people. That is not the same as saying it was designed to do it, and I am aware of how much cheaper that distinction is in a courtroom than in a valley."

"Then stop using it," Eli said, before he could stop himself.

Someone laughed. It was not a nice laugh, and it was not aimed at Eli, and Harlow let it stand.

"Mr. Park has been at this school three days." He almost smiled. "On Friday that sentence will be three days old, and he will be right, and in three weeks I shall have been at this for sixteen years, and I shall still not have stopped. Perhaps that is the answer to Ms. Chen's question, and perhaps it is worth a mark."

The questions after that were ordinary, and Harlow answered them ordinarily, and Lyra sat with her hands flat on the bench and understood something she had not understood on Monday.

*He is not lying to me.* The thought arrived with a small cold shock. *He is lying to me — he has lied to me, he is lying to me right now, about the pendant, about my mother, about the file. But he is not lying about the committee. He is going to be the villain in this and he is telling me, on the record, in a lecture hall, that he lost an argument about me eleven years ago and lost it again today.*

*That is what it looks like from the inside. That is what it has always looked like from the inside.*

She was aware, distantly, of an irony. Yesterday she had sat on a lawn and let four people tell her she was not the problem. Now she had sat in a lecture hall and let a man tell her, in front of two hundred witnesses, that she was the only student in the building asking the right question.

*Both of those things are true. Neither of them cancels the other.*

Milo found her in the corridor with his hands in his hair and an expression she had never seen on him.

"What did you just do?"

"Ask a question."

"You asked a question in front of Harlow about a classification with a priority number in it." Milo put his hands down. "Lyra. That is not a question. That is a *statement* you dressed up so he couldn't object to it."

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I don't know yet." She shifted her sketchbook under her arm. It was heavier this week. "That's the honest answer. I have a thing I should show somebody and I keep not showing it, and I don't know whether that's because I'm frightened or because I'm wrong about who to show it to."

"You've been strange since yesterday."

"I've had a strange week."

"You've had a strange *life*, and you keep pretending it's a strange week." He said it without any edge, which somehow made it worse. "Eli and Zara want to know. Tonight. Common room. Bring the thing."

"All right."

"All right." He turned off down the corridor, and then turned back. "For what it's worth — the answer's yes. Ask him things. In front of everyone. That's the only lever you've got and you're the only person in that hall who can reach it."

He was gone.

Lyra stood in a corridor of a school she had been in for one day and took the sketchbook out and looked at the back of it, at the page of charcoal studies, and thought about a woman writing *extraction priority elevates to Alpha* in a careful classical hand on very expensive paper.

She still did not show it to anybody.

That afternoon, at 15:47, her Academy tablet lit up on the bench beside her and would not stop.

> **MEDICAL ALERT — HIGH PRIORITY**
> **CHEN, WEI — WESTBROOK NEUROLOGICAL INSTITUTE, ROOM 247**
> **Cardiac arrest 15:41. Resuscitated 15:47 — ROSC achieved. Stent placed. Admitted to CCU.**
> **Attending note: the arrest was not seizure. It was a ruptured vessel in a heart already failing. Nothing that happened today caused it. This was coming.**
> **Prognosis: guarded. The damage to the muscle is old. There is no bleed-related mechanism here that we can reverse.**
> **Family contact required. Immediate.**

The tablet fell out of her hands onto the flagstones, and the sound it made was very small, and went on being small.

She got down on the flagstones to pick it up, which took a while.

*Nothing that happened today caused it. This was coming.*

She had spent a day being afraid in a new building, and a man had spent a day looking at her, and a woman on a shuttle had written a word in a careful hand, and none of it — *none of it* — was what was happening to her father, and she stood in the corridor of Threadweaver Academy and discovered that the fear she had been managing was the wrong fear, and that she had been managing it very well, and that this was not a relief.
