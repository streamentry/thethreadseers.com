# Chapter 5: The Severance

> "The question is not whether we are able to cut a connection. We are able. The question is who we are willing to be afterwards."
>
> — Lin Chen, notebook, autumn 2002

## THE GATHERING

*The Iris Bookstore & Cafe, 12 Willow Creek Lane. Saturday 31 August, ten minutes to two.*

They had put out four chairs at the front and a rope across the middle of the room, and there were perhaps sixty people in it, and Lyra understood within about a minute of walking in that this was not a school and had never been dressed up as one.

It was a bookshop. There were books on the walls, being sold, with the price still on them. There was a woman at the back with a coffee urn who did not stop knitting when a woman in a grey coat stood on a chair-stool and said, into a microphone that was slightly too loud, that her name was Eleanor Weaver and that she had a great deal to say and would try to be brief.

Her threads came off her the way light comes off a window. Deep green, and under the green something colder and much steadier, and the whole of it held in a shape that Lyra could not look at directly and did not try to. She had the room the way a person has a room they have been in before.

"We are not a school," Weaver said. "We are an institution that has been teaching one thing since 1798, which is that a person who perceives connections between things is not ill, and is not imaginative, and is not a metaphor. Some of you already know that. Most of you have been told, politely, by somebody kind, that there is no such thing as a thread."

A ripple. Lyra felt her grandmother's hand arrive on the back of her neck, lightly, without looking, the way you steady a table.

"Before I go any further," Weaver said, "I am going to do the thing our doctors tell us to do at the start of a difficult appointment, and then I am going to let everyone watch me do it, because I have been told that this organisation has a habit of arriving in communities already holding an opinion."

"I don't know what you mean," said somebody at the back.

"I know exactly what you mean, Mrs. Whitlock, and it does not matter, and I am going to do it anyway." Weaver turned to the side of the room. "Dr. Osei. Would you come up."

---

Dr. Amara Osei was younger than Lyra had expected and did not look like a doctor, and it took about ninety seconds for Lyra to work out that this was a professional decision.

There was no lectern. Osei stood in the aisle with nothing in her hands, and she did not look at Weaver once.

"I am going to describe a man I saw last Tuesday in Westbrook, with his consent, and I am going to use his name because he asked me to and because there is a boy in this room who is roughly his age and I would like somebody to be frightened on his behalf for a moment."

"Dr. Osei—" somebody started.

"Fifty-three years old. Professor of applied mathematics. What he has is called sympathetic thread bleed, and it is not a rare thing, it is a *common* thing, and the reason it is common is that it is the price of loving somebody who sees." She looked at Weaver, and something went between them that Lyra could not read and did not need to. "The perception runs. It is not passive, and anybody who tells you it is passive is selling something. And the running of it makes feedback in the people it is bonded to, and the feedback is measurable, and in a man of fifty-three with a pre-existing heart condition it is not a matter of *if*."

The room had gone completely silent.

"I have written to the four families in this room who have somebody with the condition," Osei said. "I have written to them, today, before I came here, because I am not a brave woman and I would rather not be in a room with a surprise in it. Three of you are here. You already know. One of you does not, and that is the young woman by the window with the jade, and I am sorry."

Lyra felt her grandmother's hand come off her neck and then come back.

"Dr. Osei," said a man at the front, and it was her father, and Lyra discovered that she had not known in advance that he was going to speak. "Before we go any further, may I ask what your college would consider a success here?"

"Professor Chen." Weaver's voice was very slightly different. "May I say that I know your wife."

"You may not," said Lyra's father, pleasantly. "You may say that you were at the same institution as my wife at the same time, and you may say it in front of sixty people, and I would prefer that you did it now rather than at some point in the next four years when it would be more expensive."

Nobody breathed.

"That is fair," Weaver said. "I was there in 2002. I was not her friend. I was on the committee that overrode her, and I have thought about that every day for eleven years, and it is not a thing I came here today to say out loud in a bookshop, and I am not going to." She squared her notes. "Answer the man's question, Amara. He has earned it."

Osei turned round.

"Success," she said, "is a number. For a seer, a bleed can be arrested and the perception kept, at a cost in range and precision that is permanent and that nobody can predict in advance. For a relation, the bleed stops and the organ that was being strained gets to keep working. That is the entire trade. It is not a cure and it is not small and it is not reversible and the person whose perception is being cut is the only one in the room who gets no vote, which is the part I have spent eleven years failing to fix, and which is why I stood up in front of sixty people instead of sending a letter."

---

That was how it happened. Nobody planned it.

A boy in the third row, four seats from the end, stood up.

He was seventeen. He was wearing a Westbrook High hoodie and he had the loose, unhurried shoulders of somebody who did not find school difficult, and Lyra saw the threads on him before the room did: a dense warm web, gold and green, the signature of somebody who read people easily and had never once been asked to stop.

"Ben," said a girl beside him, in a whisper that carried.

"I've got it." He was already going. "Sorry. Sorry. I just—"

And then he was on the carpet on the Persian rug with his limbs arranged the way a dropped coat is arranged, and the room made a noise Lyra had never heard a room make.

She was standing before she knew she was standing, and what she was looking at was not Ben.

It was the thing on Ben.

It came off him in a colour she had no word for and had never seen: not black, not grey, but the specific dead light of a filament that has burned out and is still, structurally, present. It was not attached to anything in the room. It went from Ben, inward, down — and it was eating. That was the only description that fitted. It was laid along his other threads the way a wire is laid along a wall to hold a door shut, and where it touched, the light underneath stopped.

Zara Washington would have called it *drawing*. Lyra, who had never met Zara, would not have used that word for another eleven days, and would then never be able to think about the phenomenon without it.

"—someone get the nurse—"

"Get him *space*," said Osei, from the aisle, in a voice with a great deal of the past eleven years in it. "Everybody back. Not touching him. Do not form a circle, that is the worst thing you can do, do not form a circle—"

But the noise in the room was not a room's noise. It was a *thread* noise, and Lyra could hear it, and it was the sound of about forty people feeling something happen to somebody and having no word for it, and it came off them in long pale spikes of pure adrenaline with no colour at all.

And then a woman in a grey coat put her hand on a boy's forehead and it went quiet.

Not gradually. *Quiet.* The way a room goes quiet when somebody finds the right note.

Lyra watched Marcus Harlow do it, because she could not stop watching him do it, and she had a perfectly good view.

He moved the way water goes down a drain. There was no hurry in it anywhere. The smell arrived before anything else — ozone, and under the ozone something sweet and green and completely wrong, like jasmine kept in a room where nobody has opened a window — and it was that smell, and the hair standing up on her arms, that told her he was the man from the tape. Not a person. A *category*. A thing she had seen twice in her life in photographs on a shelf and had not, until this second, understood that it applied to a human being standing eleven feet away from her.

His hand was on Ben Ruiz's forehead. His threads were not there.

She had spent sixteen years looking at threads. She had looked at her mother's drawings until she could have drawn them from memory. She had looked at the four foot of her grandmother in a kitchen and at the two thousand feet of a high school corridor and at the six inches between a girl and a boy in an art room, and she had *seen* people, all her life, everywhere, in layers, the way you see a landscape.

Marcus Harlow had no threads.

That was not the same as having dim ones, or shielded ones, or ones she could not interpret. He had a hole where a person was supposed to be, and the edges of the hole were very tidy, and the hole did not look at her, and Lyra had to put her hand on the table.

"No," she said, and did not hear herself say it.

"Miss Chen." Weaver was beside her, and had been for some time. "Sit down."

"He's—"

"I know what he is. So do I. So, apparently, does your grandmother, and she has gone very white." Weaver's hand closed on Lyra's arm, and it was not unkind and it was absolutely not negotiable. "Dr. Ekwueme wrote to me on Friday evening and told me what you do, and told me to have somebody near you today, and I did not have anybody near you because I did not know who you were, and that is the single worst thing I have done in eleven years of doing this, and I am not going to argue about it in a bookshop. Sit down."

Lyra sat down, because her grandmother had said something in Vietnamese that she had not heard her grandmother's voice make before, and whatever it was, it was not for her.

What she did not sit down through was the last eleven seconds of it, because she was looking at the floor, and the floor is where the threads go when they are under strain, and what came up out of the Persian rug in a single line was Ben Ruiz's, coming apart.

It did not detach. That was the thing she could not stop seeing for the rest of her life, and it was the reason she was, in the end, able to say the things she said to him in the room under the mountain. It did not detach. It went along its own fault line, all at once, the way a bone goes, and the silver-black on it ignited from the far end and burned backwards, and it was over in about a second and a half, and there was no sound at all.

Ben Ruiz made a sound.

He came up off the floor in stages — shoulder, then head, then one knee — with the expression of somebody surfacing from very cold water, and he looked around at sixty people who had all gone white, and said, in a cracked voice, "Sorry. Sorry, I—"

He stopped. His hand went up to his own face, and then went round the back of his own neck, and Lyra watched him do it, four times, in nine seconds.

"What's—" Ben Ruiz's voice went flat and young. "Where's my aunt? Has anyone — where's—"

"I would like everyone to remain seated," said Weaver, into a room that had stopped listening to her some time ago, "and I would like Dr. Osei to say something about what has just happened, and I would like nobody to leave, including Mr. Stone, who has his phone out."

The room emptied itself over the following twenty minutes in the way rooms do, and the shape of the emptying was itself information: the parents went first, in pairs, and the *seers* went last, and two of them went out of the side door, and one of those two was a girl of about eighteen with her aunt's coat over one shoulder, and Lyra saw her go, and filed it, and did not know yet that she was looking at Elena Vance, or that Elena Vance had planted something in the store's back room in the four minutes while everyone was looking at the floor.

---

They were not allowed out of the back room.

"We have to be fair to you," Weaver said. "You are the guardian of record and you have asked a fair question and I have given you a bad answer, and I am not going to let you leave with it. Dr. Osei? The scans."

Osei put a tablet on the table and turned it round, and Lyra looked at it, and understood about a fifth of it, and was grateful for the other four fifths being a shape rather than a sentence.

"Your father," Osei said, to Wei Chen. "The twenty-ninth of August. This is the first time I have seen the ventricular numbers move, and I want to say three things about it and I want the order preserved."

"Please."

"One. There is no bleed-related mechanism visible in this that we can reverse, and I am aware that that is a sentence you did not want, and I have been saying it in this family for two weeks and I have said it to him as well, and he has taken it better than you are going to." Osei did not look up. "Two. What is visible is a muscle that has been dying quietly for a long time, and that has nothing to do with a gift, and that is why the arrest on the twenty-seventh of August—" she glanced at Lyra, who had not known it was the twenty-seventh, "—was not a bleed event. Three. The bleed is still running, and it is still costing him, and it is *also* not what is going to kill him in the next three months, and if anybody in this room tells you that severing your daughter's perception is going to save his life, they are telling you a thing that is arithmetically true and completely false, and I would like you to notice the shape of that sentence, because it is the shape of everything this industry says."

"So what is the shape," said Lyra.

Osei looked at her for the first time.

"The shape is that a real improvement to a real problem was available to you, and it cost something that could not be paid for in money, and the person who had to pay was you, and everybody in the room had a good reason." She put the tablet down. "That is the shape. It is always the shape. I have been in this for eleven years and I have never once seen it come out any other way."

---

"Miss Chen."

She had not seen him come into the room. That was the first thing, and she would think about it later at three in the morning for a long time.

Marcus Harlow was standing with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, and he was not a large man, and he had a face that gave nothing away and a small silver cicatrix at his left temple, and his voice, when he spoke, was so quiet that Dr. Osei had to stop to hear it.

"Professor Harlow," said Weaver, from the doorway, in a tone that made the back of Lyra's neck cold.

"Headmistress. I am not here in any capacity, and I am about to say something that will be more useful to you than anything I could say in one, and I would ask you to let me finish before you stop me, which you are fully entitled to do." He did not look at Weaver. "In 1997 a team of five of us ran a controlled extraction under a populated valley, in a garden, in April, with a child in the garden. The extraction was interrupted. Two of my colleagues died. My wife and my daughter were in the garden and they did not die, and the collapse did the other thing to them, the thing that is technically possible and that nobody in our field will discuss in front of a first-year." He set the hat down on the table. "Mr. Ruiz is going to be all right. He is going to be *entirely* all right, in the sense that the vessel is intact and the closure is clean and he will not scar, and he will not be able to perceive the connections between himself and the people he loves ever again, and he is seventeen years old and he has just found out, in front of sixty people, and he is going to be humiliated about it for the rest of his life, and he is going to be one of a great many."

He pushed the folder across the table.

It contained two sheets. The first was a child's drawing in coloured pencil: five pink petals, a stem, a careful and slightly crooked attempt at a bee. In the corner, in a seven-year-old's letters, the word *Mei*.

The second was a charcoal study of blossom branches, and the edges of the paper were scorched and blackened as though held too near a fire, and it was signed, bottom right, in a confident adult hand: *A.H.* Beneath it, in a third hand, and in ink: *For Mei — may you always remember the beauty before the burning.*

"That is my wife's," Harlow said. "That is my daughter's. They were drawn the same afternoon, four feet apart, about a hundred metres from where I was standing when the floor came up." He straightened. "Kyoto. 1997. Those are the only two facts about that day that anybody in this building is authorised to give you, and I have just given them to you in a bookshop, and I would like somebody to tell me why I did that."

Nobody did.

"Because you have just cut a boy in front of sixty people," said Lyra.

"Yes."

"And the boy was going to die."

"He was going to go under," said Harlow. "In about ninety seconds. It is not a rare thing. It looks like your friend described it and it is not a metaphor and it is not a mystery; it is a *load*, and a young man with a strong signature and a strong bond has a load like a rope, and the rope caught on something, and I was the only person in that room who had seen it happen before." He did not move. "So yes. I cut him, and he lived, and he is going to be humiliated for the rest of his life, and I have done that on eleven occasions that are on a list, and the list is in my office, and the list is the reason I would like to spend the next hour of your life talking to you, Miss Chen, and I would like to do it honestly, which nobody who has ever spoken to me about this has wanted."

---

He put his hand on the table. Not on her. Beside the folder, about nine inches from it, and he did that on purpose, and she saw him do it on purpose.

"I am going to offer you something and I am going to tell you the price before I tell you what it is, and I am going to do it here, in front of your father, in front of the headmistress, and in front of a doctor, because that is the only way this offer is any use to anybody."

"Please don't," said Lyra.

"Three to nine months," Harlow said. "That is Dr. Osei's figure and I am not going to improve on it. I want to sever the connection between your perception and his. The cost is that you will be cut, in the way that boy was cut, and the cut is clean, and it does not hurt, and you will be alive, and he will be alive, and he will get the other years." He stopped. "That is the offer. There is a second half of it and I am going to tell you the second half as well, because the only people who ever accept this from me are the ones who were told both halves."

"What is it?"

"I do not know which part of you will be missing afterwards," said Harlow. "I have three prior cases. Two of them are alive. Neither of them is recognisable to themselves. I have never been able to predict it, I have never been able to choose it, and I have never been able to do it twice the same way, and I would be lying to you if I told you the cost was your thread-sight." His hand had not moved from the table. "The cost is that somebody decides that about a child in a bookshop, and that the child does not get a say, and that the reason is that I have run the numbers and the numbers are on my side."

He looked at her father.

"Professor Chen, you are a mathematician, and you have a right to ask the obvious, so let me make it easy for you. I have not shown you a number. I have shown you two children in a garden and a boy on a carpet. If I had shown you a number you would have believed it, and that is the thing I am trying to teach you not to do."

Wei Chen had not moved for a long time.

"My wife's last note to me," he said, "was four words long. *Don't let them quantify you.* I did not know what it meant. I have had five years." He put his hands flat on the table, in a gesture Lyra had never once seen him make. "I have understood it, and I would like to say, since nobody else in this room is going to: you have just told my daughter the price of something, accurately, out loud, in front of witnesses, and you have told her that the person who cannot afford it is her. That is the whole of it. There is nothing else in the five years."

He stood up.

"We are going to go and sit in my car," he said, "and then we are going to have a conversation, and then we are going to decide about Monday, and I would be grateful if Dr. Osei would telephone me at home tonight at any hour at all, because there is going to be a question I cannot answer on my own and I would rather have it in writing."

---

They got as far as the door of the back room.

"Miss Chen."

Lyra turned round, and it was the girl of eighteen with the coat over her shoulder, standing in the aisle with the lights behind her, and Lyra understood with total clarity that this girl had been in the room for the entire conversation and had said nothing.

"You're the one who's been holding that piece of paper," the girl said. "Since the eighteenth. I'm not going to ask to see it."

Lyra's hands went cold.

"I'm not going to say how I know, either, because that's a piece of paper of my own and I'd like to keep it." The girl glanced at the back room, at Weaver, at the door. "My name's Elena Vance. I'm a third-year, and my sister came here for an open day in the spring and asked me to put a word in for her and I said no, and she has not spoken to me since March."

"...oh."

"So." Elena Vance pulled her coat straight. "Two pieces of advice, free, from somebody who is about to make a much bigger mistake than you are. One. He's telling you the truth about the price. That's the thing you should be frightened of — not that he's lying to you. If he were lying to you this would be a much easier afternoon." She put her hand on the doorframe. "Two. You're going to spend the whole of next week deciding whether to trust him, and you're going to do it alone, and it is going to feel like respect. It isn't. It's just quiet."

She was gone before Lyra could answer, and the bell over the door went twice, and it was only afterwards that Lyra realised she had never once seen the girl's threads, and had not looked.
