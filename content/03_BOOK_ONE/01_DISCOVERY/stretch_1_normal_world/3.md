# Chapter 3: Recognition

*Friday 30 August.*

The folder was on the kitchen table when Lyra came down, face down, squared at the edges, and her father was sitting at the other end of the table doing the thing he did with the newspaper, which was holding it at arm's length and moving his lips.

"You're doing the thing where you hold the pencil and don't move it," she said.

"I do not do a thing."

"You do a thing with your mouth. It goes a bit flat at the corner."

He put the paper down. "I telephoned the woman yesterday."

"You told me you had. You told me twice, and then you told me the number, and then you told me to be careful with the number, which is the part I have been thinking about for eighteen hours." Lyra put her bag on the chair and did not take her coat off. "Did she say yes?"

"She said the family was welcome. She said no one would be approached without you present. She said her name is Eleanor Weaver and she has been doing this for eleven years, and she said — " He stopped.

"Said what?"

"She said, and I am quoting, *Tell your daughter that the number on the board is a severity and not a percentage of anything.* And then she said she was sorry, and she put the phone down." Wei Chen took his glasses off. "I have been a university professor for twenty-one years, Lyra, and in twenty-one years nobody in a position of authority has ever said anything to me like that, and I do not know what to do with it."

Lyra stood in the kitchen in her coat and found that she was not sure whether she wanted to cry or to laugh, and that what she actually felt was something in between and much worse, which was *relief*, and the relief was so large that she had to put a hand on the table.

"So you're going," she said.

"I have not decided that."

"Bàba."

"I have decided that I am going to sit in a room and be extremely unhelpful," her father said, "and I have not decided anything else, and I would like the distinction observed."

---

She made him say it properly. That took forty minutes and she was faintly ashamed of how good she was at it, and he was faintly ashamed of how easily he was managed, and between them they got through the whole of it, which is the sort of thing that takes forty minutes when you are both being careful and about four seconds when you are not.

The first thing she established was the diagnosis. "Dr. Ekwueme's letter says sympathetic thread bleed. She says it means my head is doing something to yours. I want to hear you say it."

"I cannot say it. I have a mechanism, a name, and a mechanism is not the same as a fact about my own skull." He turned the folder over. "What I can say is that the two things started in the same month and I have never been able to unstart them."

"The second thing," said Lyra.

The second thing was that the letter was addressed to *Miss Lyra Chen* at the university address, and that her father had taken it out of the drawer under the phone bill he had not paid on four separate occasions between eight o'clock in the morning and half past five in the afternoon, and had read it, and had put it back, and had done the thing he did at the end of a bad day, which was to stand at the sink for eleven minutes with the tap off.

"So you knew on the twenty-second."

"I knew on the twenty-second."

"And you didn't tell me for eight days."

"No." Wei Chen did not look up. "And I would like to be permitted to say why, because I know how it is going to sound, and I would like to say it anyway."

"Go on."

"Because if I told you on the twenty-second, you would have started planning. And there is a version of you that is much better at planning than the rest of us, Lyra, and that version is the one that is frightened, and I have watched you get frightened at me for two years and I did not want to be the reason you became organised." He put his hands flat on the table, which she had never once seen him do. "That is the true reason. The other reason is that I did not want to have the conversation. I have never in my life not wanted to have a conversation, and last Tuesday I sat in a hospital and watched a woman tell my daughter that the thing about her is killing me, and I discovered that I would rather have the conversation on a Saturday than on a Friday evening, and that is a thing about me that I am not proud of and that you are entitled to.

Lyra thought about that for a moment longer than she needed to.

"Right," she said. "Okay. That's — okay. It's not fine, but I can work with it." She pulled out the third chair. "Now do the other thing."

"Which other thing."

"You told me on Thursday that you wanted three things from me before I went, and you only said one of them, and then the folder came out and I forgot." Lyra put her elbows on the table. "You've been holding something since Thursday and it's about my mother and I have watched you not say it for two days. Say it."

Wei Chen looked at his tea.

"She was at that school," he said.

The kitchen was very quiet.

"For how long?"

"Four months, in the spring of 2002. She was thirty-three and she was the best thing I have ever met and she was, for about four months, extremely happy, in a way I had not seen her be before and have not seen since." He said it carefully, the way you walk on a floor you know is bad. "She came home and she was not the same and she would not tell me what had happened, and I did not ask, because I did not want to be a man who asks his wife that at the end of a dinner party."

"What did she tell you?"

"Two things. She told me their method was wrong, and that she had argued about it and lost, and that she was going to do it her own way instead. And she told me — " He stopped, and started again, and the second time it came out of him sideways. "She told me that if anything ever happened to her, I was not to let anybody put a *method* on her. She was very specific. She said: *it will not be a monster, Wei. It will be a spreadsheet.*"

Lyra's hand went to her sternum, to bare skin. The jade was still in her grandmother's house, with everything else of her mother's, and the fact that Nai Nai had said nothing at all about any of it was somehow much worse than saying something.

"And then?" she said.

"And then, five years ago this September, she went into a chamber under that building by herself, on a night, and told nobody she was going, and the next morning she was not in the house." He said it without drama, which was how he delivered the worst things. "They searched for eleven months. I was the one who told her not to take the position. I have done the arithmetic on that every day for six years and it comes out the same every time and I would like it noted that the arithmetic is not the problem."

"That's the third thing," Lyra said quietly.

"Yes."

"What's the fourth?"

Wei Chen took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt, which never worked, and which Lyra had watched him do perhaps four hundred times in her life and had never once seen fail to be the thing he did instead of crying.

"There is not a fourth," he said. "I have three and I have said two of them and I am going to go and stand at the sink now, and you are going to sit at that table and be sixteen at me for a bit, and then we are going to have eggs."

---

She was sixteen at him for eleven minutes.

She got the third condition out of him — the one about not doing it for him — and she heard it, and she did not agree to it, and she told him so, and he let her, and something in the kitchen got easier by about four per cent. Then she got as far as the practical part and hit the wall.

"Chợ Lớn."

"Yes."

"You emigrated when you were four."

"I emigrated when I was four. My mother was born there. My grandmother was taught there, in a house on a street called Hậu Giang, by a woman who was the grandmother of the woman who taught me." Nai Nai's chain sat on the table between them, coiled. "That is a very long sentence and I have never said it out loud to anybody, and I have been waiting since Friday to see whether I could."

"And Lin knew that. Lin knew all of that, and she was taught *here*—"

"Here, in this kitchen, by a woman with a jade needle and a temper, from the age of six, because her mother and my mother were the kind of friends who leave their children with each other." He turned the chain over with one finger. "Lyra. I want to be extremely careful here, because I can feel you already arriving somewhere, and I would rather you did not arrive there at speed."

"I'm arriving somewhere."

"I know. Everyone does. The family has a great many people in it who see things, and all of them ended up in the same building, and the building is the reason your mother is not in this kitchen." He looked at her. "Chợ Lớn is not where the gold is. It is where your family is. Those are different sentences and I would like them kept apart for a while."

Lyra looked at the window for a while.

"So the map is wrong," she said.

"Your mother's map is *her mother's map*, and it was drawn in about 1996, and it says north, near Canada, and it does not say why, and it does not say when, and there is a line through 'near' in her own hand as though she had argued with herself about it." He was going through the folder now, and the folder had a photocopy in it, and he had evidently had the photocopy made himself, at a copy shop, on his lunch, which was the single most likeable thing she had learned about her father in two years. "I have looked at it for eight days. I am not a person who sees anything. I looked at it for eight days and I could not tell you whether it means anything at all, and the reason I could not tell you is that I do not know what I am looking at, and I have spent my entire professional life resenting that kind of sentence."

"Then what do you think it is?"

"I think," her father said, "that your mother went looking for something, and that she told your grandmother about it in a panic, and that the person she trusted to help her find it is the person who runs the school, and that either she found it and told them and it was suppressed, or she did not find it and it frightened her into a chamber on a night when nobody knew where she was." He squared the folder. "Those are the two available stories. In the first one she is a martyr and I am a fool who kept her home. In the second one she is a woman who was frightened and made a bad decision alone, and I am a fool who kept her home, and the difference between them is nine years old and I do not get to find out."

---

At four o'clock she went out to the gate to meet him coming home, which she had not done since she was about nine and which neither of them mentioned.

"Ba."

"Mm."

"If I get in a room on Saturday and everybody in it is very kind and very interested and slightly better informed than me about my own mother, what do I do?"

Wei Chen stopped on the pavement, and took his briefcase off his shoulder, and set it down, which he had not had to do.

"You want the tactical answer or the true one," he said.

"I can't tell."

"The true one." He shifted the briefcase to his other hand. "You are going to be very good in that room, Lyra, and I want you to know that I have thought about it and I have concluded it, and I would like you to be warned, because it is not what you are expecting. You are not going to be able to tell whether they are lying to you. Nobody can. That is not a failure of their ethics, that is what a room full of people who know things is *for*. You will not be able to tell, and you are sixteen, and you are going to sit there and feel the whole structure of it, and there is going to be a moment — it will be a short moment, it will be about four seconds long — when you will be tempted to be certain."

"Certain about what?"

"About which of them is lying." He picked the briefcase up. "And I want you to know, before we get there, that *I* have not been able to tell for five years, and that I have used every tool I have, and that a man who has been a professor for twenty-one years and has taught four hundred students how not to trust a bad argument cannot get to the bottom of this either. So you will not be able to. That is not a thing to be ashamed of at sixteen. That is the actual condition of a person who loves somebody who has gone missing, and there is no technique for it, and everybody who tells you there is, is selling."

"Then what do I *do*?"

"You write down every single thing anybody says to you that you would not be able to guess, including the kind things." He started walking. "Including especially the kind things. In a school like that the kind things are the ones that have been shaped. You will feel the shaping and you will not be able to prove it, and so you will write it down in the shape it arrived in, and in ten years you will be glad you did."

---

That night she sat on the end of her own bed with empty hands, because everything of her mother's was still in her grandmother's house, where it had always been, and she had decided — out loud, to herself, in an empty room, which she was aware was not the same as deciding — that she was done waiting to be given any of it, and that tomorrow she was going to ask.

Instead she lay on her back and held her own hands up above her face and looked at them until the room began to do the thing, which it still did, and which was that the small warm box of her bedroom stopped being a small warm box and became a room with a second room behind it.

She had been having that since she was six.

She had never told anybody. Not Rivera, not Nai Nai, not her father. Ten years of a second room, and no words.

*Nobody has ever asked me what is in the second room,* she thought, lying there with her hands empty. *Not one person, in ten years. Not my father, who notices when I do not eat. Not Nai Nai, who counts my breaths.*

The second room was there. It had been there since she was six years old, and it had never once contained anything, and on Sunday she was going to find out what was in it, and she was not going to be frightened of that, and she was absolutely frightened of that.

She lay in the dark, and did not sleep, and did not turn the light on, and did not put the paper off the door.
