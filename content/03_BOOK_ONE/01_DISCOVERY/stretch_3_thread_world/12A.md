# Chapter 12A: The Demonstration

*Wednesday 11 September. The Grand Demonstration Hall.*

## THE ABSENCE

The notice went up at nine o'clock and it was four lines long and it changed the shape of the morning entirely, because everybody in that hall had been told in August that Dr. Chen would be demonstrating, and everybody in that hall had therefore not read anything about thread ethics since the start of term, and there were two hundred and eleven first-years and about sixty of them had already prepared questions.

*Dr. M-H. Chen will present Thursday's seminar. In her absence, Professor Harlow has asked for the floor.*

Lyra read it four times and did not know why it made her feel as though somebody had taken a chair out of a row.

"You've gone quiet," said Milo.

"I've got nothing. It's a notice."

"It's a notice about a *woman*." Zara had not looked up from a page. "Two hundred people in this hall and about forty of them can read a thread signature, and every one of them is going to spend the next hour watching two men who both outrank everybody in the room, and there is going to be a *thing* about it, and you are going to be in the middle of it, and I have known you for nine days and I would like to take this moment to say that I find you extremely difficult to be in a room with."

"Chur," said Milo Reyes, who had been in the room for nine days and had already worked out that the three of them were going to be a thing whether they liked it or not.

---

## THE FLOOR

It was not a duel. That is the first thing, and Lyra wrote it down in the back of a notebook on the Thursday because she wanted to be sure of it, and because a great many people told her afterwards that it had looked like one.

Elder Adebayo Adeyemi took the floor first, unasked, and did it in a way that made the request irrelevant: he came down into the circle, and he sat down on the obsidian, cross-legged, in robes the colour of a September afternoon, and he waited.

"I teach what I know and I do not know what you teach," he said. "We will both be wrong. There is a bench at the back for anyone who becomes faint, and I am seventy-one and I have a knee and I will be using the bench, and I would like it noted that I have said so in advance, because a man of my age who does not say so is performing."

Then he made something.

He did it slowly, and he narrated it, and he got two things wrong on purpose and corrected them, and the effect of watching a competent man correct himself in public was the single most disarming thing Lyra had ever seen a person do.

*Ẹ̀mí àgbájọ.* The living gathering. He said the words and translated them and then said that the translation was a *reduction* and that the reduction was the fault of English and not of him, and that he would like somebody in the room to write down that the fault was the fault of English.

Amber and green, and they did not snap into being — they *arrived*, the way water arrives at a thing, and Lyra's eyes filled and she had to stop and do the count that Wren Zhou had taught her on a platform, and behind her four hundred threads of frightened attention went up off the floor like heat off a road.

"It's feeding on the room," Eli Park said, from two seats along, in a whisper. "It's not taking. It's *feeding*, and it's the same as what Ms. Washington does with a room when she walks in and everybody goes quiet, and it's completely different from what the professor is about to do, and I want to watch both."

A ward went up in front of Adeyemi. It was not a wall. It was a thing with roots in the floor and a great deal of patience in it, and the pattern was nothing like anything in the textbooks, and it was, Lyra thought, the first time in her life she had seen a *structure* that was obviously alive and obviously not interested in her.

"Hold that in your mind," Adeyemi said. "That is what a *relationship* looks like from the inside. It is not stronger. It is more expensive to run and it lasts longer and there is no on/off and you cannot get it in an afternoon. Hold it."

---

Marcus Harlow stepped into the circle, and the temperature in the hall went down about two degrees, and Lyra's whole body reacted before her mind had got to the word.

He was not a large man and he moved like something poured, and he did not have the ozone smell in a room of four hundred people; that was in a bookshop in August, at a boy on a carpet, and she had spent a month being frightened of a smell and now there was nothing at all, which was somehow much worse, because it meant she had been frightened of the wrong thing and had got lucky.

"Threads are not a metaphor," he said, and the textbook said that too. "They are a medium. They respond to intention with a measurable lag of four to nine tenths of a second, and every disaster in our institutional memory traces back to somebody who believed that wanting a thing hard enough was the same as doing it. I have said that sentence in this hall once. I am not going to say it again. I am going to *demonstrate* it."

Silver threads came into being in the air in front of him, and they came into being the way a nail goes into a board, and Lyra felt her stomach go cold, because she had seen that gesture forty times in her mother's notebooks in a kitchen on a street called Ellery, and in every one of them it had been described in words like *ploughed* and *driven* and *forced*, and Lyra had never once thought about why.

He built a lattice in about nine seconds. It was beautiful. It was, without any question, the most competent thing Lyra Chen had ever seen a person do with their hands, and it hung in the air over the obsidian and it was a *fortress*, and every line in it was straight, and the whole of it was beautiful in the way that a suspension bridge is beautiful.

"Efficiency," he said. "Minimal expenditure. Maximal effect. That is what you are looking at and it is what I have been trying to teach this institution to do for eleven years, and I have failed approximately two hundred times, and the reason I have failed is on that bench."

Adeyemi had not moved. His amber ward was still up, patient, rooted, entirely indifferent.

"Marcus," he said, in Yoruba-accented English, in the tone of a man who has known somebody a very long time. "That is a *fence*."

"It is a *wall*," Harlow agreed. "And a wall is what is between my daughter and a collapse."

"Between your daughter and a collapse is a *wall*," Adeyemi said, "and the wall is made of your daughter, and she is seven, and she is standing in a garden, and she has been standing in that garden for sixteen years, and you are the only person in this room who still believes she is standing in a garden, and I have watched you build eleven of these and I have watched what happens to the garden every time."

*Eleven years.*

Lyra's hand went to the jade at her throat and stopped there, because she had been about to do something else with it, and she made herself not, and did not entirely understand why, and understood four weeks later.

---

Then Adeyemi stamped his foot, and a hum went through the stone, and the amber flared, and what came out of it was a bird.

It was enormous. It had a wingspan of about nine feet and it was made of light and it was not a *symbol*, which was the thing Lyra could not stop thinking about afterwards: Harlow said the word *animistic* twice, dismissively, in the voice of a man filing a document, and the bird was not an animistic projection. It was a *structure* with a job.

It took his lattice apart in the air, in about four seconds, without touching a line of it — Lyra worked that out afterwards and could not work out how — and it came back and folded itself over Adeyemi's shoulder like a bird that has come home.

"Recycling," said Eli Park, from two seats along, in a voice that had gone somewhere else entirely. "Oh. *Oh.* He isn't making a wall. He's making a *roof*."

---

## THE WRIST

It went wrong at eleven minutes, and it went wrong because of a man's hand.

Lyra saw it happen and she could not afterwards explain to anybody why it was the hand, because a room of four hundred people saw a demonstration and a woman of sixteen saw a man in a grey suit move his cuff down about an inch too far, in the specific, practised, entirely involuntary way of a person who has been wearing something that is not theirs.

Adeyemi's eyes went to it. Lyra saw the elder's gaze arrive on that wrist and stay there, and saw the elder's face do something that four hundred people could not see, because it took about half a second, and then Adeyemi said a word, quietly, in Yoruba, and Lyra did not know it and has never been able to find it in a dictionary, and has never needed to.

Harlow froze.

Every silver thread in the air went out at once, not in a pattern and not with a bang — they *went*, the way a held breath goes, and eleven or twelve of them tore as they went, and there was a sound in the hall like a door being shut in another building.

The name came out of the old man like a stone out of a wall.

"Kyoto."

And the hall did not breathe.

---

Lyra went out into the corridor because she had to, and because four hundred people were standing up, and because the woman she wanted to speak to was going to be at the front of it and could not be got to.

There was a man against the wall with his forehead on the stone.

It was her father, and he had a stick, and he had come from Ward C on his own at eleven o'clock in the morning on the advice of a doctor who had lost that particular argument, and he had not told her, and the reason he had not told her was that he had been in Ward C for two days and had been watching a woman of eighty-one on a speakerphone teach a girl of sixteen to say a sentence in Vietnamese, and had decided that he could not interrupt that.

She stood four feet away from him and could not get a word out.

He was not weeping. That was the thing she would remember. His shoulders were shaking and his knuckles were white against the stone and he was not making any sound at all, and Lyra stood there and understood that she was looking at her father being *remade*, in a corridor, in about ninety seconds, by a word said by an old man in a hall.

"Ba—"

He put his hand up. Not to her. To the wall.

"Lyra. Go back in."

"I can't. Ba—"

"*Go back in.*" It came out of him like something breaking. "I have been sitting in Ward C for two days reading your mother's papers, all of them, the ones she left and the ones Nai Nai has had for six years, and I want to tell you what I have learned, and I cannot do it in a corridor, and I cannot do it now, and I am asking you for the only thing I have ever asked you for, which is to come back in a minute."

"Bàba—"

"Nineteen ninety-seven," her father said, to the stone. "April. A garden. A draw, and a man with a blade, and a child of seven who walked *towards* it." His hand slid down the wall about a foot. "That is what I have learned, and I have known some of it since the beginning of the year, and I did not tell you, and the reason I did not tell you is that I was afraid of a specific thing, and the specific thing was that you would go and find him."

Lyra stood in a corridor of a school she had been in for nine days and did not move.

"Bàba, I am going to find him."

"Yes," said Wei Chen. "I know. That is why I have been *reading*."

---

Behind them the doors opened, and about forty people came out into the corridor, and among them was a girl of about nineteen with silver in her hair who was taking notes at a speed that was frankly indecent, and a girl of eighteen with her aunt's coat over one shoulder who said "Ms. Chen" in a voice that was not a question, and a boy who had followed Lyra out of a hall of four hundred people without being asked and without any idea at all what he was doing.

"There is a bench," said Lyra, "in the Demonstration Hall, and it is not for the students. It is for an old man's knee, and he used it, and he said he would in advance, and he did."

Milo Reyes said, "Right," in the voice of a person who has decided to be useful before he understands the situation, which is, Lyra thought, exactly what he had done on the first day with a matrix, and she would spend the next nine days learning that it was a habit and not a fluke, and the rest of her life learning that it was the best thing about him.
