# Chapter 13: The First Disappearance

*Weave-Quake Counter: 35% instability. Node-failure projection: T+13 d, 14:00. The reference dataset was destroyed in the Maze collapse of the tenth of September; this is the Council's re-run on partial data, and nobody has said out loud that it is a guess.*

*Thursday 12 September.*

## THE QUESTION, ASKED IN A LECTURE HALL

"Professor. Can I ask you something that is not about the answer?"

Two hundred and eleven first-years, the Lumina Lecture Hall, half past two on a Thursday, and Zara Washington stood up in the middle of a row and Marcus Harlow stopped mid-sentence.

"That is a somewhat alarming way to open," said Harlow, "and yes, and I am going to enjoy it, and please be aware that I am going to enjoy it *visibly*, and that two people on the Council have been told about that in advance and have not stopped me."

"I've been in this building for eleven days." Zara did not have notes. Lyra found that she checked, twice, and that Zara Washington had come to a lecture hall of two hundred and eleven people with nothing. "And on the first day four of us held a door for about four minutes and two hundred and eleven people watched, and the number on the board in the station was seventeen, and it was seventeen on the Monday and it was seventeen on the Tuesday, and Dr. Osei has been very patient with me and she is going to stop being patient in about eleven weeks, and I would like to ask a question about the number before I run out of the thing that is keeping me from asking it."

"Ask."

"How many people know what it measures?"

And there was a silence in that hall that Lyra Chen would think about for the rest of her life, because it was not the silence of two hundred people not knowing an answer. It was the silence of two hundred people who all knew the answer and had all, independently, decided not to have it.

"Some of us," said Harlow.

"Thank you." Zara sat down.

Nobody breathed for about three seconds and then somebody at the back said, "It measures the severity of the ward network. It's a severity and not a percentage of anything. The projection is a date and not a number of hours. There are four numbers on that board and they are four different questions, and the fourth one is how full the old fear-collector under the gym is, and it is not in the student handbook, and I have looked, twice, and I would like to ask somebody about the fourth one."

"You would like to ask somebody about the fourth one," said Harlow, "and I have been waiting nineteen days for somebody to ask somebody about the fourth one, and I would like it noted that the reason I have been waiting is that I raised it at a Council meeting in 2011 and it was minuted as *noted*, and that I have discovered in the last eleven days that *noted* is a thing you can do to a question so that it never has to be answered again, and that this is a discovery I made in a lecture hall in front of two hundred people because a girl of sixteen was braver than a committee."

---

Afterwards, in the corridor, Lyra said, "That was the best thing I have ever seen anybody do."

"That was Zara. I said *noted* in 2011."

"That was the *best thing you have ever said*, you told her she was braver than a committee, in front of two hundred people."

"Mira," said Harlow — and then stopped, because he had used a name that had not been introduced, and about forty people in that corridor heard it, and one of them, a girl of eighteen with her aunt's coat over one shoulder, turned round very slowly.

"You called her Mira," said Elena Vance.

"I did."

"Her name is Zara. Zara Washington. She is a first-year and she has a *sister* in this building and it is not you." Elena Vance's voice was very level. "I don't know what you have just told this corridor. I don't know what any of it is about. But I would like to say one thing into it, and then I am going to go and do my work, and I would like it noticed that I have a *course load*."

"Noticed," said Harlow.

"Ines is my mother," said Elena Vance. "She is alive. She is in a care programme in this town and my sister has been in the same one since she was twelve, and the last person in this building who wrote that name down in a form in 2011 was you. And *Mira* is not her name, and it is not anybody's name. It is what happens when a man who has had a page in a drawer for two years tries to say out loud a name he was never supposed to have learned."

Nobody in the corridor moved.

Then she said, "Two hundred and eleven names in that hall, and there were about nine of us who could see what was happening, and every single one of us did the same thing, and I have been in this building three years and I did it too. So." She put her coat back on her shoulder. "Don't."

She went.

Harlow stood in the corridor of Threadweaver Academy with his mouth slightly open, and Lyra Chen — who had spent a month learning that a man with a silver thread in his arm was a *category* rather than a person — watched a woman of eighteen walk away from him and understood that she was braver than both of them, and that she had said so in eleven words, and that it had cost her something, and that nobody was going to thank her for it.

---

## THE ARCHIVE

They went down at four o'clock because the demonstration had emptied the building and Eli Park had worked out, in about six minutes, from a diagram and a list of reading-room hours, that the Restricted Stacks are staffed between two and four and unstaffed between four and six, and that between four and six the *access log* is still written, by a machine, and that a machine writes down the time and not the person.

"You have got eleven minutes," he said, at the bottom of the spiral stair, in the dark, with his torch off. "And I want to say the thing I am actually worried about, and it is not security, and I have written it on the back of my hand."

"Go on."

"Somebody put a piece of paper in Lyra's sketchbook on the eighteenth of August and nobody has seen it since." Eli did not look up. "I have been not-asking about that for twenty-five days. I want to say out loud, in a cellar, to two people, that I have been not-asking, and that I have a reason, and that the reason is that I have worked out that whoever put it there can put things in other people's rooms, and that if I ask Lyra to show it to me then the person who put it there finds out that a sixteen-year-old has read it, and I would like to put that on the record before I do something I am going to regret."

"Go on," said Zara.

"And I have worked out the other half of it," said Eli Park, "which is that this is *also* the reason nobody has asked Lyra to show it to us, and that it is not only me."

---

They found it in forty minutes. Not the conduit — the conduit is a *term of art* and they did not have it, and Eli had spent three weeks failing to find it by searching for it, and what he found instead was a place.

"There's a term for it," he said, into a very small torch beam. "'Rim node.' That's what the Maze log calls the east lattice. And there are four of them, and three of them are in this building, and the fourth one is not on the inventory, and *the inventory is dated 2011*."

"Where?"

"In the Maze." Eli sat down on a floor. "Lyra. Lyra, the thing you went into on Tuesday — the eighth room, the one that isn't on the list, the one Tanaka says is a *cache*—"

"You told me it was a cache."

"It is a cache. It's a cache with a *rim* on it, and the rim has a reading, and the reading on Tuesday night was thirty-four per cent, and the counter said twenty at the same time, and I have been assuming for three weeks that the difference was a *consequence*." He was talking very fast now. "It isn't. It's a *source*. Something under that building has been drawing from the ward network since Tuesday night and it has been drawing at a rate that took the counter from twenty to thirty-one in nine seconds, and it is still drawing, and the log is in a machine, and the machine is in the antechamber, and the antechamber is not locked, and there are two hundred and eleven people in this building who have just been in a lecture hall being told a great deal about the fourth number."

---

## JAMES

They came out of the Restricted Stacks at five past five into a corridor in which a boy of fourteen was sitting on the floor with his back against a wall and his school coat over his knees, doing a sum.

"Lyra," he said, without looking up. "Hi. Sorry. I'm James. Your father's cousin's son, from the branch in Leeds, which I know is a *horrible* way to say it, and I've been here since Friday because Nai Nai's branch sent me for the assessment, and I have been in the East Tower since yesterday and I have a *sheet of paper* from a man called Harlow and I don't know what it means and I'm supposed to take it to the office tomorrow, and I couldn't sleep, so I've been doing the sums on it, and there's a number on it that keeps changing and I've checked it four times."

He held out the sheet.

"James." Lyra's voice came out at a pitch she did not recognise. "Ba. How long have you been in the East Tower?"

"Since yesterday. They said the assessment room was in there. And they've been — " he frowned at the paper — "they've been coming and going. There's a *noise*, Lyra, in the floor. It isn't a machine noise, it's more like—" he tapped the paper "—it's like that. It's a number going up and down and I can *hear* the going up and down and I thought that was normal because that's what I do, and then I met Ms. Washington in the corridor on Tuesday and she looked at me like I was broken."

He put the paper down.

"Lyra, I think I am in a room where the number is me."

---

That was the whole of it, and it took four minutes, and afterwards nobody could work out why the four of them did not go to a member of staff immediately.

It was Eli's fault, and he has said so in four different documents, and the version he gives in the 2013 transcript is this:

"We had a *room*. We had a location. We had a reading that had been changing since Tuesday. We had a fourteen-year-old with a sheet of paper in a corridor. And we had a name to give to whoever we reported it to, and the name was *Armitage*, and Armitage had run Harlow's advanced seminar for six years, and I had a *diagram* in a corridor and four and a half hours of daylight and a two-hour window on a Thursday when the assessment was scheduled and a building that was emptier than it had ever been.

"And I said, out loud, to two people I trusted, *if we go to Armitage now, the only thing we will have achieved is that a professor knows we know.*"

Zara Washington said, "That is true."

"It is *not* true," said Eli Park. "It is what I said, and it is what I believed, and it is the exact mechanism that had operated on this building for seventy years, and I said it, and I said it in a cellar, on the strength of no evidence, about a man who has been nothing but decent to me, and I have never been able to work out whether I was right."

"You were right," said Lyra Chen, and has said so in print, and it did not help.

## THE ROOM

Elara Vance's room was on the third floor of the east dormitory and it was empty.

That is the phrase in the report and the phrase in the transcript and the phrase in about four hundred letters, and Lyra Chen, who has read all of them, has never found a better one.

Empty. Bed made. Desk tidy. A stack of work on the top, squared. A hairbrush on the shelf above the sink. Three nights of a term lived in a room, and not one thing *wrong* with any of it, and nothing in it at all.

The dormitory's own monitoring board said what it said: **RESIDENT, E. VANCE, LEVEL 3. OCCUPANCY: VACANT SINCE 11 SEPT 23:14.**

One night.

"It says *vacant since the eleventh*," said Milo Reyes. He had said almost nothing for two hours and he said this in a very small voice. "Lyra. It's the twelfth. She went *missing* on the eleventh and nobody — we were only *looking* for her. She's been gone since last night."

"The eleventh," said Zara Washington, from the doorway, and did not come further in.

---

The thread monitoring said something else, and it is in the transcript, and nobody in the room of four understood it at the time, and only one of them understood it in 2019.

Elara Vance's thread signature — which anybody with sight could have read off the wall of a corridor, and which eleven people in the building had read and had thought was *a lit window* — was not gone. It was there, in the same room, in the same space, at a *different depth*.

"There is something in the walls," said Zara, from the doorway. "I want everybody to be extremely careful about what I am about to say, because I have been wrong in this building twice and both times I was wrong in a direction that cost somebody something, and I would like the second one to be on the record as a thing I did.

"It isn't residue. It isn't a scar, and it isn't a *ghost*, and I am using all three of those words deliberately to say that I have considered them and rejected them.

"It's *under* her. Lyra — go and stand by the bed. Go on. Don't touch anything. Now: is there anything in the room that you can see a *depth* to?"

And Lyra Chen, sixteen years old, nine days into a term, with a jade stone at her throat and a gift she could not switch off, looked at a small room in a dormitory in a valley in September, and said:

"Yes."

And that is the first evidence in the record of anything at all being in the ground beneath the east dormitory, and it is the evidence that the *thread dimension* is not a metaphor and not a mood, and it was given by a schoolgirl who did not understand what she was looking at, and every engineer who has worked on that site since has quoted it.

---

## THE REPORT

They wrote it that night, in a common room, in about eleven minutes, and it is a bad document and it is on the record and it is *exactly* the document that a well-meaning sixteen-year-old writes.

> *To the Registrar.*
>
> *We are four first-year students. We are writing this because we are not sure who to write to and because we have been told that the registrar's intake log is the one place in this building that Security cannot reach without a countersignature, and because we have been told that by a person who we have not named and who has been extremely helpful in a corridor and who we would like to keep out of this.*
>
> *1. A resident of the east dormitory, E. Vance, has been absent since the eleventh of September. Her room is intact and the monitoring board says vacant since 11/09 23:14. We do not know what the 23:14 means and we think somebody should ask.*
>
> *2. There is something under the east dormitory. We do not know what. One of us can see a depth in it and one of us can feel a structure in it and one of us has a diagram in a book that puts a *rim* under the east dormitory and a cache on it, and a reading that has been climbing since the twelfth of September.*
>
> *3. J. Chen, fourteen, visiting, has been in the East Tower since Wednesday on a thread assessment and has a sheet of paper from a member of faculty with a number on it that he can hear, and he thinks the number is him, and we think that this is correct, and we do not know what to do about it, and he is fourteen, and he is in that building tonight, and he went to bed there tonight.*
>
> *4. We are not asking you to believe us. We are asking you to put a person on the floor below E. Vance's room and to move J. Chen, and we are asking that the response be in writing, and we are asking that the response be addressed to us and not to a member of faculty.*
>
> *5. Signed. E. Park. M. Reyes. Z. Washington. L. Chen.*

Eli Park read it four times and then said, "That is a very good document and I am not posting it."

"Give it to the registrar," said Zara.

"Give it to the registrar *tonight*, and it will be on her desk in about nine minutes, and the security log will show a first-year visitor to the registrar's office at 23:41 on the twelfth, and at some point tomorrow a person will look at the security log." Eli's hands were not entirely steady, which Lyra noticed, and about which she said nothing at that time and has since apologised for. "Lyra, I have known for eight days that there is something about that *paper*. And I have worked out in the last four minutes that whoever put it there can see what people read, and that if a first-year is logged in this building at 23:41 on a Friday, then it does not matter what is in the letter."

"Then what do we do?"

"We take the piece of paper off our own door," said Eli Park, "and we write our four sentences on it, and we hand it to Dr. Chen *in a corridor, in daylight, with a witness*, and we let the security log say that four first-years told the Registrar something in a corridor at ten in the morning, and then we do not write anything down for nine days."

"Nine days is a very long time," said Lyra Chen.

"Nine days," said Eli Park, "is how long it took a man in a good room to work out how to say no to a clipboard."

They did not wait nine days.

Eli Park was in the east corridor at ten past ten the next morning with a piece of paper and Zara Washington as a witness, and Dr. Mei-Hua Chen read it standing up, twice, and then said four words, in the corridor, in daylight, in front of a witness, and they are the four words that the rest of this is about:

"What day did you say?"

"The eleventh," said Eli Park. "Eleven forty-one at night, on a thread assessment, on a building, on an east dormitory floor, on a room."

"Thank you," said Dr. Mei-Hua Chen, and went down the corridor at a speed that made two of them follow her, and the Registrar of Threadweaver Academy was sixty-eight seconds ahead of everybody for the next eleven days, and what she did with those eleven days is in the transcript, and it is the reason there is a register of case forty-one, and it is the reason there is a *fifth board* in that building at all.
