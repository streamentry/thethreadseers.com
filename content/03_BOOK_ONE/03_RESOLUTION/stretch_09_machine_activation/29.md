## THE ARRIVAL

She had been two hours in the lift.

That was the part nobody had thought about, afterwards — not the machine, not the lattice, not the man. Two hours in a service lift, going down eleven floors at the speed of a bottle of wine, with her father beside her and Eli Park holding a case in both hands and Zara Washington standing in the corner with her arms folded and her jaw set and Milo Reyes, who had walked out of the infirmary on a stick at ten o'clock at night against medical advice that had been waived in writing, in the specific way that things in this building were waived in writing.

She had not gone because she had been summoned.

She had gone because at six o'clock that evening, in a doorway outside Ward C, a man in a dark uniform had said *come alone, and come now, and you have eleven hours*, and had then, in a second voice, entirely different, said *and bring the registrar, because there is going to be a form, and the form is the only thing down here that has ever worked.*

Blackwood had sent Mirembe Blackwood, at nineteen minutes past six, and had then sent a second message, at nineteen minutes past six, in which she had written four words: *we are past surnames.*

---

The chamber was not what Lyra had built in her head, and she was faintly ashamed of the head.

It was very large and it was very ordinary. Concrete, a cable tray, a row of lockers, a kettle somebody had plugged in. The Magnus Conduit — the thing she had put her gift through, the thing that had rewritten itself in front of two hundred witnesses sixteen days ago — occupied the middle of it the way a boiler occupies a plant room. It was not beautiful any more. It had been beautiful for about ninety seconds on the Sunday, and then it had gone back to being a piece of equipment, and had stayed that way, humming, with a light on it.

And on the other side of the floor, in a space that had been cleared and had not been cleared properly, there were ten people on the floor on mats, and nine of them were awake, and one of them was not.

Lyra stopped.

"Sit down, please," said Marcus Harlow, from the console. "All of you. It is going to take a long time and I would like to spend it with people on chairs."

Nobody sat down.

"They're the Council's," said Eli, in a voice with no air in it at all. He had the case against his chest. "Those are the *Council's* people. They are wearing Council grey and they are lying on the floor of a machine room and none of them is *shut* —" He stopped. "Why aren't they shut?"

"Because I am not the one who brought them," said Harlow, "and because it is half past twelve in the morning and in about four hours a man is going to stand up in a room and tell this Academy that a translator is carrying a valley on a circuit with no power source, and I wanted you to see them first, and I wanted to see whether you would notice."

## THE ARRANGEMENT

He had laid it out on a bench, in a row, with a clipboard, and he did it the way he did everything, and Lyra found she could not decide whether that was a comfort or the most frightening thing about him.

"Item one. Your father."

She had known it was coming. She had been ready for it for four days. It did not help.

"Sixty-eight per cent coverage at the wrist on the twenty-eighth, and it is a straight line and it is not a curve, and I have eleven weeks in it, and I have told you that and you have written it down." He looked at her, and then at her father, who was standing very still with a cane he had not been given permission to bring. "Professor Chen. You are the only person in this room who has read the transfer function on the east face of that machine. Say the number."

"Sixty-eight," said Wei Chen. "On the twenty-eighth, at about four in the afternoon, from a photograph Osei took with a telephone because we do not own an instrument. Sixty-eight per cent is a coverage figure. It is not a severity. It is the area of skin, and skin is not the question."

"What is the question."

"The question is the gradient." Wei Chen did not move. "The burn is following the lines of the conduit model, Marcus, and you know that, and you have known it for four years, and you have never once put it in a sentence, because the sentence is: *it is following a shape that exists in the architecture of the machine you built*, and if you said that out loud then somebody in this building would ask you why a man's circulatory system has adopted the geometry of a *device*, and there is no answer to that question that is not either *coincidence* or *the machine is doing it*, and you have been living in the gap between those two answers for eleven years and so have I."

Nobody said anything.

"Item two," said Harlow, and his voice did not change at all, which was how Lyra knew. "Your father has a cardiomyopathy that has been quietly getting worse for probably two years and which is at thirty-one per cent of the muscle, and a bleed at nineteen per cent of a load of four, and a heart that is now, in the plainest terms available to me, a clock. Three to nine months in September. Less in October, because he has been doing heroic things, and I want to be extremely precise: I said that to his daughter at four in the morning in a corridor on the twenty-ninth and I do not know how she took it and I have not asked, because asking would have been a way of being forgiven and I am not here for that."

Lyra felt her father's hand arrive on her shoulder, very light, and then leave, because he had understood within about a second what she needed, which was not to be touched.

"Item three," said Harlow. "You can save his life. Not extend it. *Move it.* Not onto this machine. Onto a person. If the silver channel that your daughter is currently standing in is separated from his — cleanly, once, permanently — the bleed stops, and the bleed has been one of the two things shortening his life, and it will stop being one of the two things." He set the clipboard down. "I have run that four thousand times. It is arithmetic. It is the only arithmetic in this room that everybody agrees on."

"And what it costs," said Lyra.

"Her sight," said Harlow. "All of it. I have three prior cases and two of them are alive and neither of them is recognisable to themselves, and I have told her that, in a bookshop, in front of her father, in August, and I have said it again this week in a corridor, and I am now saying it for the third time in front of a witness, and I would like the witness to note that the number is three and the number is the same."

"And in exchange," said Wei Chen.

"Nothing." Harlow's head came up. "That is the difference between the offer I made in the rain and this one. I have learned something in four days and I would like to say what it is, because you have earned it and I have not earned very much this month."

He turned round to the whole of it — the four of them, the ten people on the mats, the kettle, the lockers.

"I spent eleven years being the man who asks a question and then waits, because the waiting is the ethics. I thought that if I never *named* the thing, the thing would not have happened." He put his hand flat on the console, and the silver in his forearm moved, and he did not look at it. "On the fifteenth of September I named it. In a demonstration hall. In front of two hundred people, one of whom is standing in this room. And it happened anyway, and a boy is in a hospital bed in this building who will be a completely ordinary musician for the rest of his life, and the *naming* did not prevent it, and there was no arrangement in which it was prevented, and I have spent four days working out what that means and the answer is very short and I do not like it."

"What does it mean," said Eli Park.

"It means I was not asking. I was *offering*." Harlow looked at him. "I have made eleven offers in eleven years. I have never once been asked for anything. There is a difference between a question and a request, and I have been confusing them since 1997, and it has cost a boy his perception and a girl her childhood and a woman her life, and the last of those is not mine to pay."

## THE PROJECTION

"Then let us talk about the other thing," said Lyra. "The machine."

"The machine is starving," said Harlow. "It has been starving since the moment your silver left the upper matrix and went down. A translator with no power source is a person holding a door. It is not a machine any more. It is a *person*, and the person is fifteen, and she has been holding it for four days, and I want you to understand that she has not been told this, because nobody has been able to work out how to tell her without frightening her, and that is a failure of this building and not of her."

"Then tell me now," said Lyra.

"Tell *her* now," said Harlow, "yes, and I am not the person who does it, and that is not a rhetorical flourish, it is a practical problem, because a subject who has consented to a procedure and is then told that the procedure is the only thing standing between a valley and a rupture will consent to a *great deal*, and four wardens and a compliance officer are not a substitute for the person who asked the question."

Zara Washington said, "Then I will ask her."

Nobody had expected her to speak.

"You have all been talking about her," Zara said, "in the third person, for nine minutes, in a room she is standing in."

And Lyra discovered that this was true, that she had been an object in an argument about herself for the whole of it, and that the discovery was not humiliating; it was the most useful thing that had happened to her in four days.

"Yes," she said. "All right. Ask me."

But it was not then, because there was one more thing on the bench.

## THE RECORDING

"There is a recording," said Harlow. "It is on the console. It is a resonance capture from the fifteenth of April, 1997, made by a man called Weber, who was not on my team and who I have never been able to explain. It is the only surviving record of the garden. I have watched it — " he stopped, and recalibrated, and Lyra watched a man of fifty-four choose a word for the fourth time in nine minutes "— a number of times. I would like you to watch it, and then I would like you to notice the date on the frame, and then I would like you to say the date out loud, because I have needed somebody else to say it for six years."

"Why now?"

"Because on Thursday morning I am going to stand in a room and be asked whether I am a man who fabricates, and I have never been able to answer that question, and I have realised in the last four days that I have been carrying a piece of evidence in a drawer that would have answered it, and that I have kept it in a drawer for six years, and that the reason is that the evidence is also a *reason*, and reasons are what you keep when the evidence has run out." He squared the notes on the console, in a gesture that was so exactly like her father's that her chest hurt. "Watch it. Then say the date. Then we will do the rest of the evening, and it will be a long evening, and I am going to ask all of you the same question at the end of it, and I want you to answer it separately, and I do not want to hear four answers in unison."

---

It was ninety seconds long, and it was not what she had expected.

No collapse. No light. A garden in the last week of April, in a low cold light, shot from about waist height by somebody who was not watching the sky.

A woman with a book she had not opened.

A girl of seven with cherry blossoms in her hair, walking *towards* something out of frame, fast, with both arms out in front of her, the way you walk into a doorway you have decided is a doorway.

And, very briefly, in the extreme left of the frame, a man turning round, and the sound of a man saying a single word.

And then the picture did not end. It carried on for another forty seconds, in the same garden, in the same light, with nothing happening in it, and in the last eleven of those forty seconds a woman of about forty came into frame, sat down on a bench, and stayed there.

*Weber's own footage*, said the caption. *The event does not appear. He is the only witness. He has never given a statement.*

"Eleven forty-one," said Lyra Chen.

"Yes."

"That's the eleventh of April. The frame is stamped the fifteenth."

"Yes."

"The eleventh of April, 1997, is five years before my mother came to that building, and it is a Friday, and my mother told me once that the eleventh of April was the only day of the year her father let her skip school because of the blossom on the river, and she has never in her life told me a day of the year for any other reason."

"Yes," said Marcus Harlow.

"And in that recording," Lyra said, and her voice had gone somewhere she had never taken it, "my mother is not in it. And I am not in it. And Lin Chen's file in this building has a department number and a project code and four months in it and it is in the spring of 2002, and you have been sitting here for eleven years telling me that the last thing my mother did was go into a chamber under a mountain on the eleventh of September, five years ago, and I want to know whether you have ever once, in eleven years, written the *second* date down."

There were nine people on the floor of Sub-level 7 who had not heard that question before, and two wardens, and a compliance officer, and Marcus Harlow, and he answered it.

"No," he said. "I have never once written the second date down."

"Why not?"

"Because it does not help me," he said, "and because on the eleventh of April, 1997, in a garden in Kyoto, my daughter was alive, and on the eleventh of April, 2002, in a chamber under this mountain, a woman of thirty-two walked in on a Thursday because it was blossom week, and if I write the second date down next to the first one then I have to look at them, and I have not been able to look at them for eleven years, and I would like that in the record, and I would like it in the record in those words, because the record is the only place left where anybody can check a thing about me."

## THE QUESTION

He asked it at ten past three in the morning, and he asked it four times, and he wrote the answers down himself, on a legal pad, in a hand that had begun to shake about an hour before he started.

"Seventeen days from now, a node under a populated valley is going to fail, and eleven thousand people live over it, and the only instrument in the world that can hold a circuit open is the thing in this room, and the thing in this room has no power source, and there are four things that can be done about that, and I am going to say all four, and I have spent four days making sure I am not lying in any of them, and then I am going to leave this room and I am not going to be in it again."

He held up the pad.

"**One.** Return case forty-one to the circuit. Thirty-nine people in a lattice, thirty-seven of whom have been dead since 1941 and two of whom are not, and one of those two is fifteen years old. It is a power source. It is the only power source. It will hold the circuit indefinitely and it will hold it cheaply and it will make this building legal by Friday. It is also the thing I have spent eleven years refusing to do in a room where nobody could hear me refuse, and I have now been asked to do it by somebody with a clipboard, which is apparently the only thing that has ever worked on me."

Nobody breathed.

"**Two.** Shut the translator down. The circuit goes open, the load comes off the machine and back onto the wall, and the wall has thirty-one per cent of tolerance in it and it has seventeen days. It will not fail on the seventeenth day. It will fail somewhere in the first four hours, and it will fail without a forecast, because the model for a held circuit has never been run and everybody in this room knows it."

"**Three.** Find a person." He said it very flatly. "Not a volunteer. Not a relative. A person with a *resonance the Weave will hold*, standing in a housing, spending themselves, and a great deal of themselves, and not getting up afterwards. I have looked. There is no version of this that I can construct. I am telling you that as the man who has been trying to construct one for sixteen years, and I would like that minuted."

Lyra's father had not moved. His hand was on the back of a chair.

"**Four.**" Harlow turned the pad round. "Carry it. Split the load. It is the one that has never been on anybody's list because it requires the circuit to be *held* while it is being rebuilt, and holding it requires a person, and a person is three. So: this is not a fourth option. It is a statement about the fourth option, and the statement is that it does not exist, and it does not exist for a reason that has nothing to do with physics, and the reason is that every single one of you would rather be *finished* than be *responsible*, and I include myself, and I have been including myself since 1997, and I would like the four of you to write that down and take it with you."

He put the pen down.

"So. In the morning I am going to be asked whether I would put thirty-nine people back in a machine. I do not know what I am going to say. I have known for four days that I would not, and I have also known for four days that there is going to be a moment, at about half past nine in the morning, when a person with a gavel will describe what eleven thousand people would look like on a Tuesday, and in that moment the arithmetic will be *very* good, and it will be the only good argument in the room, and I will be the only man in the room who knows that." He looked at Lyra. "That is why I asked you to come. Not for the consent. For the witness. There is going to be a moment in the morning when a *number* is the only thing anybody has, and I have spent my life being a man who respects numbers, and I would like, for once, to be in a room with somebody who has read one in the same handwriting as I have."

---

She wrote it on the back of her hand in the dark, after the others had gone up, while her father sat on a chair with his cane across his knees and did not sleep either.

She wrote four things.

1. *He is not lying to me. He is going to be able to do it anyway, and he has told me so, in advance, in a room with witnesses, and that is not a defence and it is not a trick either. It is the first true thing anybody has given me since the fifteenth of September.*
2. *Ask about the bleed. The machine translates. It translated a cage into a conversation. Ask it one more question, about a feedback loop, and put a number on the answer.*
3. *Nine people on mats. Ask them whether they are the Council's or whether they are the lattice's. Both can be true and one of them is a person.*
4. *Four options and one sentence. "Every one of you would rather be finished than responsible." He is wrong about the four of us. He is not wrong about me.*

Then she put her hand over the writing, in case her father could see it in the dark, which he could not, and which she knew he could not, and which she did anyway.
