## THE ASK

They came to her at ten past three in the afternoon, in an anteroom, and there were five of them and a trolley and a great deal of paper.

She had been awake for about forty hours and had been in a room with a stone floor for most of it, and the anteroom had a window, and the window was the first thing since Friday that had made her cry for a reason that was not about anybody.

"Sit down, please."

"I would like to stand."

"Sit down, please," said Eleanor Weaver again, "because I have done this twice in six years and I got it wrong both times, and I am not going to get it wrong three times with a person in front of me."

Lyra sat down.

Then Mei-Hua Chen — her aunt, whom she had spoken to on the second of October at ten past nine in the morning, in a corridor, for eleven minutes, and who had spent the entire eleven minutes apologising for things that were not her fault and had then, at the end, said: *you are the first Chen in this family in four generations who was not the smartest person in the room, and it is a *relief*, and I am sorry, and I am also extremely glad* — put a single sheet of paper on the table.

"That is a form," she said. "It is called an instrument of voluntary bodily application. It is one page, front and back, and it was drafted in 1974 by a lawyer who did not believe it would ever be used, and there has been exactly one other instance in the history of this body and it is in a museum in Lyon."

"What does it say."

"It says: *the applicant applies to occupy an aperture, for a period to be determined by the instrument's officer, on terms recorded in the schedule at Annex B, and waives the protections of sections four to nineteen inclusive.*" Her aunt's voice did not change. "Sections four to nineteen are the consent sections. Signing it means you cannot be asked, later, whether you would have chosen differently."

"I know what it means."

"I am aware that you know what it means, and I want to say one more thing in my own voice, and then I will not say it again." Mei-Hua Chen put both hands flat on the table. "I have been the Registrar of this Academy for eleven years. I have signed four thousand seven hundred and eleven forms. I have never once refused one. That is not a boast, it is a *complaint that I have about myself*, and I have been the person in this building who says that the forms work, for eleven years, and the reason the forms work is that the person who *refuses* them is always somebody who cannot read."

Osei read them out. The aperture is the fault in the east lattice of the Magnus Conduit, described in the engineer's report of the first of October, and the applicant will be positioned by two persons and will remain so positioned until released by the officer or until the officer's death, whichever is first, and the engineer has given an estimate of the load-time of four hours and has said, in writing, that the load-time is not a limit on the applicant's life but a limit on the officer's ability to keep her in that position without injury, and I want that sentence read in this room exactly as it was written, because it is the only sentence in all this paperwork that anybody has been careful about."

Four hours.

"That is the whole of it," said Osei. "Four hours of holding. And then it is done, and the fault is lined, and the aperture is closed, and there is no version of this in which you come out of it."

"There is not," said Wei Chen.

"Then say it to her, Mr. Chen, because it is in your memorandum and it is not in mine, and I would like it said by the person who wrote it."

## THE REFUSAL

"No," said Lyra Chen.

She said it before anybody had finished, and she was not shouting, and she was on her feet, and nobody in that room had asked her to stand.

"Mr. Chen, I am going to say something and I would like it minuted, and I would like it minuted *before* I say it, so that nobody afterwards can say that I was persuaded.

"The fifth option is correct. I have been in a hole in that machine for four days and I know what it is like from the inside, and Eli Park is right that I can read that cage, and you are right that the arithmetic is right, and I am not going to argue with any of it, because all of it is true, and I have spent three weeks in a building learning that *true* is not the same as *right*, and a man called Harlow has been true to me four times and has cut a boy out of his own life doing it."

"Lyra—"

"Four hours is not the cost." She put her hands flat on the table, the way her father did, the way her grandmother did, the way everybody in that family did. "The cost is that it becomes a *procedure*. That is what happens. I have watched it happen to a boy in a corridor in about four minutes and then I watched it happen to me, and if I do it, then on the third of October somebody in this building will write down what we did tonight, and they will write it down *properly*, because that is what this building does, and then in a year there will be a room with a boy in it, and there will be a form, and there will be an officer, and his name will not be on it because there will be a *schedule*."

"Who was in the corridor?" said Osei, very quietly.

"You know who was in the corridor," said Lyra. "You have written his name on a board every day for three weeks."

Osei did not answer.

"Harlow says he never once asked anybody for anything," Lyra said, "and that is his whole confession, and he has been carrying it for eleven years, and he says it to me as a man who does not understand what he is looking at, and I want the Council to understand it too, because it is not about him. He is a professor. He is a *researcher*. He is the most senior thread practitioner in this Academy and he has spent eleven years doing a thing that he has never been asked to do, and he has got very good at it, and that is why the two hundred and eleven students in that hall were told they were attending a *demonstration*."

She turned round.

"So I am not doing it for the arithmetic. I am not doing it for eleven thousand people, and I want that minuted as well, because I have found out this month that I am extremely good at carrying things for other people and that it is the single most dangerous habit I own. I am doing it because the *source* is thirty-nine people in a cage and the *fifth option* puts the load on a bench with a meter and a warden's signature, and if I do not do it then the Council will do item one, and item one is a *regulation*, and I have read a regulation."

"But if you do it, it is also a regulation," said the Chair, from the doorway. "In about a year, Miss Chen. That is the honest shape of it. We will write it down, because I have sat in this chair for two days and the only thing I am certain of is that this cannot be *unwritten*. I am not going to stand here and offer you a noble version of yourself that lasts one afternoon."

Lyra looked at him for a moment.

"Yes," she said. "All right. Thank you. That is the first time in three weeks that anybody has offered me the *true* version of anything, and I would like it recorded that it came from the Chair of the Council and that it was worse than the lie and I felt better."

## THE ANSWER

There were five people in that anteroom and the Chair and four wardens, and Wei Chen was sitting on a chair by the window with his cane across his knees, and he had a piece of paper in his shirt pocket, and he had had it in his shirt pocket since half past three, and Lyra had known about it since eleven o'clock that morning and had said nothing, because it was his.

"All right," he said. "I have a number and I have had it since Sunday, and I am not going to pretend that I arrived at it this afternoon."

"Sir—"

"Let me be finished, Lyra, because you have about nine minutes before they start the procedure and I have had three weeks of watching you be the only person in a room who says the true thing, and you are sixteen, and I am not going to let you do this to me as well."

He took the paper out and did not unfold it.

"Sixty-eight per cent coverage. Straight line. Four weeks, which is eleven weeks by the skin and three by the geometry, and I have been careful about which one I am quoting and I would like the difference to be in the record, because the difference between eleven weeks and three weeks is the difference between a man dying of a machine and a man dying of a heart, and they are not the same death and I am not going to have them added up.

"The bleed stopped on the twenty-eighth. Lyra asked the machine to translate a feedback loop and it did, and it is a closed loop, and it will not start again, and I want to say clearly to this room that that is not a *cure* of anything. It closed a door. The door was never the reason I was going to die. It was a *tax* on a clock that was already running.

"So: three weeks, and I have known that since Friday, and I have had a piece of paper in my pocket since Sunday, and on Sunday morning I told a woman who was standing in a hole in a machine that I would not have said no."

"And I have not said no," he said. "I want that on the record, because I have spent twelve years not saying things out loud and I have a daughter who learned it from me, and I am not going to do it in an anteroom.

"But I am not volunteering, and the difference matters, and it is the whole of the difference. I am not putting my name down because I think it is correct, and I have heard your argument and it is correct and it is the argument of a good man. I am doing it because I have been a statistician for twenty-one years and I have never once in my life been able to look at a number and not ask *who is paying*, and in April of 2002 my wife wrote the answer to that question in a notebook in a chamber under this building and then went into that building on her own at half past one in the morning on the eleventh of September and did not come out, and I have had five years, and the *only* thing I have managed to do about it is to read her papers and get here."

"And I am not going to stand up in a Council of Traditions," said Wei Chen, "and be the second person in this family to decide on their own."

"First: I, Wei Chen, of Westbrook, apply to occupy the aperture described in the engineer's report of the first of October, for the period recorded at Annex B, which is four hours, and I understand that the officer of this instrument is Dr Amara Osei and that the engineer is Mr E. "Second: I waive sections four to nineteen."

"Third: I have a daughter in this room and I am not asking her permission and I am not asking her forgiveness and I would like both of those entered in the record as *not asked for*, because in about four hours she is going to be in a machine and she is not going to have the capacity to give me either, and I would like it noted that I have noticed the mechanism. "Fourth, and this is the one that matters. The applicant states that he was not the first person offered, and that the first person offered refused, and that he has read the refusal, and that he is making this application in the full knowledge that a girl of nineteen refused it in a demonstration hall in August because nobody asked her what the machine would be, and that if the Council ratifies this instrument then the ratification will be recorded with that refusal in the paper, in full, in her words, permanently, on a board in this building where students can read it."

## THE FAULT

She went in the way she had gone in on Sunday, which was the only way there was: through a crack, on her side, into the gap between what the machine had been built to take and what she had put in it on Saturday afternoon when she had reached up a wire in a demonstration hall in front of two hundred witnesses.

The lattice was there. Of course it was there. She had been talking to it for four days.

*Hello,* she thought. *Are you the same?*

And it was not words and it was not the way she had done it on Sunday, when she had said *I'm here, and I'm going to find out, and I'm not going to do anything to you that I can't undo*, and it had worked, and she had never worked out why.

This was: *a room, and a line, and the line going out through a hand's width of space to a place where a man is lying on his side, and a warmth at the far end of it that is not the warmth of a person and is not the cold of a machine and is somewhere in between and is the most frightening thing Lyra Chen had ever felt.*

*Hold the line,* she thought. *That's all. Don't let go. It's four hours and it is only four hours and then we put the lining in and we take you out of the box.*

And the lattice said: *we know. we have been holding things for seventy-two years. nobody has ever asked us to hold something we were not already holding.*

*They were lined, in a housing, with a meter and a warden's signature, and they were held, and they are still held, and every one of them is accounted for, by name, by pattern, by the date they went in, and by the date of their death where there is one, and thirty-seven of the dates are 1941 to 1953 and two of them are 2013, and one of the two is a girl of fifteen who is alive and is in Bellhaven and whose mother has been told.*
