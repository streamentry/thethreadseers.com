# Chapter 31: The Thing With No Number

> "A number is the only thing that works in a room like this, and every man in it knows it, and that is the trap. The trap is not lying. The trap is being right on a Tuesday."
>
> — Mila Rowntree, student advocate, aged nineteen, in a note she was not supposed to file

*Weave-Quake Counter: 68% instability. Node-failure projection: T+34 d. Rebaselined against a held circuit. The load is off the wall and onto a machine, and the model has never been run on this basis before.*

*Wednesday 2 October, 06:10. The Lumina Lecture Hall, with the Council of Traditions sitting on the floor. Nobody has slept.*

## THE ROOM

The Lumina Lecture Hall held four hundred, and there were three hundred and ten in it, and the extra ninety were on the floor.

There is no dignified way to describe what the Council of Traditions looks like when it has been convened in an emergency and has to sit on a floor. Eleanor Weaver had made one decision on Saturday about that and had written it down and had not been argued with, and the decision was that there were not enough chairs, and the reason there were not enough chairs was that the gallery was full, and the reason the gallery was full was that every member of the technical faculty had come, and the reason they had come was that somebody in an anteroom had let it out at about eleven o'clock on Tuesday night that *item four* was on the agenda.

Four of the lattice's patterns had gone between eleven and two. Nobody knew who. Nobody had been near it. A woman from the Council's own evidence office had put her hand on a wall panel in a sub-level and had wept, and had been asked to leave the building, and had not wanted to.

The hall had a stone floor and a barrel-vaulted ceiling and a great deal of silver thread in the stone, and Lyra sat on the floor at the back with her back against a pillar, because she had asked for the back and Dr. Mei-Hua Chen had granted it without comment in a way that was itself a comment.

"Order," said the Chair, who was Councilor Ibadapo Yuen and who had not expected to be Chair and had said so twice, "order. We are here on the Nexus Stabilization Protocol, amendment of 2009, and there is a procedural irregularity which I am going to state and then I am going to allow, because the alternative is that we sit here for six hours arguing about procedure while eleven thousand people find out something on Tuesday."

---

## THE SPOKEN NUMBER

"Marcus Harlow," said the Chair. "You have put a paper in front of this Council. Read the number, and then say what it is."

Harlow stood up in the third row, in a jacket he had clearly not changed, and Lyra noticed that his hands were steady, which she could see perfectly well from where she was sitting, and understood that almost all of him was not.

"Sixty-three per cent," he said. "That is the severity index of the ward network. It is not a percentage of anything. The number means: of the things this network can be asked to hold, it is currently managing about sixty-three per cent of them, and the rest are being held by a girl of sixteen who is asleep at the moment and does not know that she is asleep, or that she has been asleep for four days, or that the reason is a fault in a machine about a hand's width wide."

"Marcus—"

"Item one of the Protocol, the return of case forty-one to the circuit." He turned a card round. "Item two, the closure of the translator and the reversion of load to the wall. Item three, the appointment of a standing anchor. I am going to say something about each, and then I am going to say the thing I have come to say, and I would be obliged if the Council would let me get to it, because I have been awake for fifty-one hours and I am not going to be able to be *careful* much longer and careful is most of what I have."

"Proceed."

"Item one works," said Harlow. "It is the only item on this list that works today. It works because the lattice has been feeding that machine since 1941 and the lattice is a power source of a kind we cannot make, and thirty-nine patterns in a cage will hold a circuit for as long as the mountain stands. It is arithmetically inarguable. It requires no further modelling, no further delay, and no further *truth*." He looked at the floor. "I have refused it four times. I refused it on the fifteenth of September in a demonstration hall and I did not sleep. I refused it on Sunday night at half past four in the morning with four wardens and a compliance officer present. I refused it in a locked ward on Sub-level 2, where I had spent nine days in a good room, and I want the Council to understand that the nine days in the good room did most of the work and that a good room is the most dangerous instrument in this building."

"Does the subject understand the position of the item?" asked the Chair.

"She does. She said the word *audit* to me at seven o'clock yesterday morning in front of a lawyer, and then she told me she was refusing item one in the specific terms of a person refusing to be *relieved* of it, and I would like that on the record as the most frightening piece of moral clarity I have encountered in thirty years of this discipline, and I am not going to be able to explain why to this Council and I have stopped trying."

"Item two."

"Item two is not a decision. It is a *measurement*, and the measurement is that the wall under the Southern Nexus has thirty-one per cent of tolerance in it and has been losing it since 1997 at a rate nobody has bothered to publish. Seventeen days ago it had four hours." He said it flatly. "I have run the load transfer eleven thousand times since Sunday. It fails inside four hours, and it fails without a forecast, because there is no model for a held circuit, and every number I have given this Council about that failure is a *guess* made by a man who would like to be wrong in the safe direction."

"And item three."

Harlow did not answer for four seconds.

"Item three," he said, "is my name on a piece of paper, and I wrote it at three o'clock in the morning, and it is the fifth option, and it is not in this list, and I would like to explain why, and then I would like the Council to do something that I have never in my life seen a Council do, which is to stop and ask a person a question instead of accepting a schedule."

---

## WHAT A STATISTICIAN CAN DO

"My name on a piece of paper does not amount to very much," Wei Chen said, "and I would like the Council to understand why before it votes on it, because I have spent fifty-one hours going back over it and I have arrived somewhere I did not expect and I would rather they heard it from me in this room than from a report."

He got up with a hand on the chair. He had a stick. Nobody mentioned the stick.

"Everything I have is in a case behind me and two of my hands, and I want to be extremely clear that I am not a person with a gift and I never have been, and that the entire reason I am standing in this room rather than in a corridor in another state is that I can read a *transfer function* and that nobody else in this building, including four hundred and sixty people on this faculty, can do it in a working week."

"Professor Chen—"

"Let me do this in the order I did it." He had a piece of paper, and Lyra understood from the way he held it that he had written it out on the train, in a kitchen, on the backs of pages, exactly as he had said he would. "Sunday. A translator in a room with a power source, holding a circuit, taking a payment from nobody. I ran the source side. It cannot be done. A machine is not a resonance; it is a function, and a function does not have a floor."

"Then item three is impossible," said somebody in the middle rows.

"No," said Wei Chen. "Item three is impossible, and you said so, and you are right, and Professor Harlow has been right about that for four days and I would like the Council to hear a man agree with his own worst conclusion, because it is the only time in my life it has happened." He turned round. "But the *transmission* side is not impossible, and nobody has looked at it, because everyone in this building has spent seventy years looking at the source side and about a fortnight at the machine."

"Elaborate."

"Everything I have is a machine with a **source** and a **transmission**. The lattice is the source. Lyra Chen is the transmission. Not her mother, and not a Chen of nineteen; there is no other Chen in this, and if you have written one down anywhere then you have written down a person who does not exist. That is the whole of the thing. And a machine can be rewired at the transmission end, and what I have been doing since Sunday night, with a pen, and Dr. Chen's telephone, and a great deal of help from a boy of sixteen, is to find out whether the fault she went down can be *lined*."

"Lined with what?"

"With a *body*," said Wei Chen. "Not a person. A shape."

---

He put the paper on the floor, because there was no table, and knelt down on his bad knee to smooth it out, and a woman in the fourth row got up and knelt down and smoothed the other end of it, and did not say anything, and Wei Chen nodded at her once.

"The fault is where her hand was. It is a hand's width. It is a crack in a lattice-work core, and it has not closed in four days, and it will not close while she is in it, because it is being *used*." He tapped the paper. "A fault can be lined. A crack in a wall can be lined and then loaded and it will carry load, and the load goes through the lining rather than the crack, and nobody asks the crack any more. That is the entire civil engineering of the last two hundred years and I have watched four hundred students not learn it."

"With a body in it."

"With a *shape* in it. Not a person. Something with the geometry of a person and none of the rest." Wei Chen stood up. "The lattice has been running since 1941 and it has taught his body the geometry of a machine. That is not mysticism and it is not a metaphor; it is a measured fact that Professor Osei and I have both verified and that is the only reason anybody is talking to me. Sixty-eight per cent of his skin carries the pattern of a circuit. If that pattern is put *into* the fault, the fault carries the load without him."

Silence.

"You have just described putting a man inside a machine," said the Chair.

"I have described putting a man's *shape* into a crack," said Wei Chen, "which is a different sentence, and I want it written down in the words I have used, and I would like the Council to weigh the two sentences separately, because in four days' time somebody is going to write a report using the first one."

"And does it work."

"Twelve per cent," said Eli Park, from the floor.

*Eli Park, aged sixteen, sitting on a floor, in a building he had been in for twenty-eight days, holding a napkin.* Lyra felt her own eyes go hot and had to look at the ceiling.

"Twelve per cent of a sixteen-year-old's resonance and it holds for nineteen hours," Eli said. "I have been wrong about the fault nine times. I have written that down. The twelfth is right, and the twelfth is boring, and boring is what we wanted, because we have been living in a building for three weeks where everything that was exciting turned out to be a catastrophe."

"Can you not take more of her?" said somebody.

"No," said Eli Park.

It was the fastest and the most complete answer Lyra had ever heard in her life.

"No," he said again, louder, to the whole hall. "No, and I want that on the record, and I want the *reason* on the record, and the reason is that Ms. Chen is sixteen and has been standing in a hole for four days and has a *name* and a *cousin* in Ward C and a father standing in this hall who has not once been asked what he thinks about it, and if I say yes to that question in this room I will be doing arithmetic about a person, and I have been in this building twenty-eight days and I have watched four men do that and I would like it noted that I have decided that I am not going to be the fifth, and that it took me nineteen days and a hospital bed with a boy in it, and I would have preferred nine."

---

## THE TWO NOES

Zara Washington had not spoken for two hours, and at five past eleven she stood up in the middle of the hall and everybody turned round, because everybody in that room had been watching her not speak for two hours.

"Two people said no," she said.

"*Ch Zara.*"

"Two people said no. I have not been able to say that in this building for three weeks and I am saying it now." She was not shouting. She had never once shouted in Lyra's life. "One of them said it on the Sunday, through a circuit, in words, and she was fifteen, and she had been in a lattice for twenty-one days, and she has grown into the substrate of it, and if the channel is opened she dies on the way out, and so *no* was the only answer her body had left, and Lyra honoured it, and thirty-seven other people did not get to be honoured, and that is the sentence I have not been able to say out loud to another human being and I am saying it in a lecture hall."

Nobody moved.

"The other one could not hear the question," said Zara. "At all. In any register. Nineteen days, and I have been four feet from them twice and I have got *nothing*, and I do not know what they would have said, and I am not going to guess about a person I have never met, and I would like this Council to understand that *that* is what a person becomes when a question is asked through a cage, and that it is not a special case, and that it has been happening in this building since 1941, and that the reason nobody has ever had to answer for it is that nobody ever asked."

"Where are they?" said the Chair. "The two."

"Downstairs," said Lyra.

---

She had not wanted to bring them up. She had fought about it for twenty minutes in an anteroom and had lost twice and won once, and the reason she had won was that Eli Park had said: *they will find out anyway, and the finding out will be in a corridor, and a corridor is where everything in this building goes wrong.*

So they came up at ten past one, on a trolley, with Osei walking beside it, and a great many people in that hall found a reason to look at the ceiling at once, and Lyra stood up in the middle of the floor and said, "Her name is Elara Vance, she is fifteen, she is conscious, and she is not a case number," and then sat down, because she had nothing else and did not have to pretend to.

"I want to say the next thing myself," said Elara Vance, from the trolley, to about three hundred people, in a voice that had been unused for twenty days. "Because the woman who is in my head and got me out did not do this part, and she is not going to, because she is not — sorry, I'm not good at this. I was in a box and she asked me what I wanted and I said no because the door was on the wrong side of me. And then she went and asked the others, and she took four days, and she did it while there was a hole in a wall she could have gone down." She stopped for a second. "I want to say that the no was the bravest thing I did in my life and I want it recorded, and I also want to say that I would like somebody to have asked me in August, in a corridor, in about four seconds, what the machine was going to be, and that there is a woman on this faculty who could have done that and did not, and I am not angry with her, and I would like her to know that I am not angry with her, and I would also like the minutes to record the rest of it."

The rest of it took ninety seconds and it was the most damaging ninety seconds in the history of Threadweaver Academy, and Lyra had spent a fortnight on Sub-level 7 with a girl who could not hear, and she was the only person in the room who could not look away, and she did not.

Elara Vance named a signatory on a card, and the date, and the room she had been in.

Elara Vance said the word *volunteer* in the tone of a person who has thought about that word for a month and has not been able to get it out of her mouth cleanly.

Elara Vance said: "I want it minuted that the woman who ran the machine checked my mother's letter, and that I found out in October, and that my mother has been told nothing, because nobody in this building knew how to telephone a mother."

---

Mirembe Blackwood left the hall at half past one and did not come back for two hours.

## THE ONE THAT IS NOT A NUMBER

At a quarter to three the Council had a schedule. They had had one at eleven o'clock and they had had it at one o'clock, and the Chair had a piece of paper and the paper had four things on it, and three of them had a date.

"The Council is in recess for twenty minutes," said the Chair. "And I am going to use the twenty minutes, and I am going to use them, because I have been Chair of this body for nine hours and I have been a member of it for twenty-one years, and I have never once had to do this, and there is a young man at the back of my hall who has been right all day and has not been asked a single question, and I am going to ask him one, and if the Chair objects the Chair can be outvoted afterwards."

He looked at the back of the hall, at the pillar, at Eli Park.

"You have been sitting on that floor for five hours saying no to people who were senior to you and correct. Is there an option on that piece of paper that nobody has taken?"

Eli Park looked at the paper on the floor in front of him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said.

"Then say it, and say it slowly, and do not do me the courtesy of modesty."

"She can't hold it," Eli said, "because she is sixteen and because sixteen is not a *number*, it is a person, and this Council has spent nine hours discovering that it does not have the vocabulary for people and only has it for numbers, and I am not being clever. I have checked. We have had three days and a napkin. There is no number."

"Then why are you smiling?"

Eli Park looked up, and Lyra saw it happen from thirty feet away, because it happened to a boy's face, and she was not reading anything.

"Because the twelfth number came out boring," he said, "and I have spent a fortnight learning that boring is what we want, and about four hours ago I worked out what boring is, and it isn't a smaller number. It's a *bigger* circuit."

---

He had to be given the floor twice.

It took him eleven minutes and he had a napkin and he did not have a diagram, and the napkin was four lines long, and at the end of it Eleanor Weaver put her head in her hands for about four seconds, and Dr. Mei-Hua Chen — the Registrar, the aunt, the aunt who had been eleven years in that chair and had been frightened for six of them — said "Oh," out loud, in a small voice, in the middle of a lecture hall.

"It is not a smaller number," Eli said. "It is a bigger circuit. The transmission has been taking eighty-eight per cent of a load and the source has been taking twelve. If we line the fault — and we can, with a shape, and Ms. Chen's own model, and it is *boring* — then the transmission can take up to sixty per cent, not eighty-eight. Which means the source only has to hold forty."

"And the source is—"

"Two people," said Eli Park. "Instead of thirty-nine. Because we open the lattice. Not to take power out of it. To take it *out of the cage*, and put it in a *housing*, with a meter and a name and a warden's signature, and that is the thing that has been impossible since 1941, and it is impossible because nobody can *ask* the lattice whether it will hold a circuit without the cage around it, because asking it means speaking to it, and speaking to it means a resonance, and the only resonance in this building that the lattice has ever answered to is —"

Every head in the hall turned to the back of the hall.

"—is the one that came out of it on Sunday," Eli said, "and told us what it wanted, and then stood in a hole for four days holding the door, and who *is sixteen*, and who I have just spent four days telling this Council she cannot be, and who I am now telling this Council is the only person alive who can read that cage without an interpreter."

Nobody in that hall had ever, in the entire institutional history of the place, asked the question.

It had taken a nineteen-year-old's arithmetic, a sixteen-year-old's napkin, a fifty-three-year-old statistician, a fifteen-year-old on a trolley, and a girl on a floor with her back to a pillar, and it had taken them nineteen days, and not one of them had been senior to anybody.

*Wednesday, 2 October, 15:40. Lumina Lecture Hall.*

Eli Park had said that the source had to hold forty, and Lyra Chen had done the sum twice on the back of her hand in a room with two hundred people in it, and had got the same answer both times, and had understood about a minute later that the answer was not a number at all.

It was a queue.

And her mother had written, on a page dated the February before she was born, about a drawing of her as an infant, and had written it in her own hand, in a journal she had left in a studio in a city four hundred miles away:

*Control is not the solution. Communion is the only road. A system that requires a payment from someone who has not agreed to make it is not a system. It is a queue.*
