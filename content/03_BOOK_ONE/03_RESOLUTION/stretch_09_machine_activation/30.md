## THE QUESTION

She asked it at half past three in the morning, sitting down on the floor with her back against a machine that was holding a valley, facing nine people on mats.

"I'm Lyra Chen. Some of you were sent here and some of you were brought, and I'm going to assume it doesn't matter, because from where I'm sitting it doesn't."

"Steward," said a woman of about sixty, on the second mat, in the voice of somebody who had been asked a question on an official form. "We're the Council's observers. There's nine of us and there's two doctors and a minute-taker. We've got twenty-one days."

"Observers of what?"

"Of the instrument," said the woman, and there was something in her face that Lyra had begun to recognise, which was the look of a competent person who has been given a job they were not told the terms of. "We've been down here since the fifteenth. We log intake, output and the twenty-one conditions. That's it. Nobody told us what we were observing."

"Then here's the question, and I'd like it answered out loud, one at a time, and I would like it written down." Lyra put her hands on her knees. "Do you want to be here?"

Nobody spoke for about four seconds.

"Yes," said the woman, "because if we go up, there is nobody in this room, and if there is nobody in this room then on Thursday there is a vote with no witnesses in it."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," she said. "It is what I answered, and I answered it because you are asking a room of people who are the only adults within four hundred metres, and I would like you to understand that I have noticed you are sixteen and that I have noticed that you have done the thing where the real question is underneath the question, and that I am not going to help you with it, because you did not come down here to find out what nine people want. You came down here to find out what *thirty-nine* want."

Lyra looked at her for a long moment.

"Yes," she said.

"Yes," said the woman, and something in the room changed temperature, and Lyra had the very clear sensation of a machine with a new instruction.

---

The lattice was not in the room.

That was the first thing Lyra had not understood and the first thing she understood, about four hours in, and the understanding came to her sideways, in a corridor, at half past four in the morning, because she went out for air and found that the air on Sub-level 7 did not have any in it.

The lattice was underneath Sub-level 7. It had been underneath the site since 1941. The housing had been grown shut, and the housing was what had come away from a service panel like dried coral in a room four days ago, and what was inside it had been put in a case and taken out of the building at twenty past ten that Sunday night in a sealed box with an inventory number on it and a girl's signature on the form.

Which meant that what was running the translator had no source, and had had no source for four days, and was nonetheless still running.

"It's you," Zara said. She was standing at the far end of the room with her back to a locker and her hands at her sides and had been like that for forty minutes. "Lyra. It's *you*. It has been you since Sunday night. I have been sitting four feet away from you for four days."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I worked it out on the sixteenth." Lyra said it to the floor. "Four days. I put it together on the Wednesday, in a common room, with Eli, and I have not said it out loud to anybody because the moment I say it out loud somebody puts it in a file, and I have had four files this month and I would like to have one conversation that is not in one."

"So say it now," said Milo, from the chair, "and say it to me, because you are not saying it to me, you are saying it to the wall, and the wall does not need it."

Lyra looked up.

"Sixty-eight per cent coverage," she said. "Straight line. Four weeks. It isn't his skin, it's the *shape* — it's following the geometry of the model. He said it out loud to my father at midnight in a room with nine witnesses and he did not say it to *me*, and it was not an accident, and I have been not-angry about it for four days, which I would like recorded as the most impressive thing I have ever done." She put her hand flat on the floor. "He has had sixty-eight per cent of his own blood in a shape that only exists inside a machine, for four months, because he has been standing next to a prototype core and working on it. It is not *thread-burn* in the sense that the textbooks mean. It is second-hand geometry. And it means that if you remove him from this building, it stops, and if you keep him in this building, he has four weeks, and if you put him in *that*—"

She turned and looked at the translator.

"—then it finishes tonight, and it finishes because he is the only thing in the world whose body has already agreed to the shape."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That," said Marcus Harlow, from the console, in a voice with nothing in it, "is the fifth option."

Milo turned round. "What?"

"That is a fifth option and I did not list it, because I have spent eleven years not being allowed to think about him." Harlow had not moved. He had not moved in four hours, and he had a legal pad in front of him with four answers written on it in a hand that had stopped shaking at about two o'clock. "A person whose body has already adopted the geometry. Who does not have to be *compatible*. Who does not have to be found. Who is a statistician, and is fifty-three per cent dead, and has a *model* in his own hands, and who walked down here on a cane at ten o'clock at night because he was not going to be upstairs."

"That is a man," said Wei Chen.

"That is a man," said Harlow. "I have had him in my building for eleven days and I have not once, for one hour, allowed myself to consider it, and I would like the record to show the mechanism of the omission, because it is the most interesting thing about me and nobody is going to ask." He put the pen down. "It is this: he is the father. And I have spent sixteen years finding daughters."

## THE ARGUMENT

They argued for two hours and eleven minutes and it was not an argument between the five of them so much as an argument conducted by all four of them in four different directions at once, and Lyra — sitting on the floor of a machine room with her back against a boiler that was holding a valley — thought, at about half past five in the morning, *this is what it is supposed to be like, and I have never once had it before, and I have been calling it being rescued.*

Eli wanted the model. He had the case and he had a pen and by six o'clock he had a thing on a napkin that made four of the Council's observers stand up.

"Four options and none of them require a *person* except three, and two of those three are a cage and a death, and the third is your father, and you did not list him because of a psychological mechanism that you have now described, and that is fine, that is human, I am not interested in the human. Look at the *shape*." He had it flat on the floor between them. "A translator with no source is a person holding a door. The reason she is holding it is that the circuit was built to *expect* a source and has a shape where a source goes. A person's resonance is a source. A *machine's* resonance is not a source, because it was never designed to be one — but a machine's geometry is exactly what her body has spent four months learning."

"You are describing a prosthetic," said Wei Chen, from the chair.

"I am describing a *fittings problem*," said Eli, "and you know exactly what I mean, and I watched you work it out in about two seconds, and I would like you to say it, because I have a bad feeling and you have not had one and I have known you for two days."

Wei Chen was quiet for a moment. The kettle had boiled some time ago and had not been switched off.

"The fault," he said. "The thing she went down in. On Sunday." He looked at his daughter. "You went down a *crack* in the core. Not through the matrix. Through a fracture that followed your hand. And the fracture is still there — I can see it from here, it is a fault line in the lattice work, it is about a hand's width, and it has not closed, because she has been *standing in it* for four days, and holding a fault open is not the same as holding a door, and the difference is that a fault can be *lined*."

Silence.

"Lined," said Zara.

"With a body. With a *shape*." Wei Chen put his cane down flat on the floor so that he had both hands free, which was a thing Lyra had never seen him do. "Not a person. A *shape*. Something that has the right geometry and does not mind being geometry, and does not get frightened, and does not have to be a person at all, and you could get it out of this building in a lift in an hour, and it would be a piece of equipment, and I would like everybody in this room to notice that I have just said the word *equipment* about my own body and that nobody has yet worked out that I am not joking."

---

Zara had been the quietest person in the room for four hours, which was the single most frightening thing Lyra had ever seen, because Zara Washington was never quiet; she was the noise in the room, she was the one who put her hand on your arm and asked permission.

On the Friday, at eleven o'clock at night, four days ago, she had knocked on a door and said: *there is something under this floor and it is the worst thing I have ever come near and I would like you to be in the room while I go and look at it, and I would like it minuted that I have asked you and not assumed you.*

"There is a third thing nobody is looking at," Zara said.

They all looked at her.

"Nine people on mats, and four wardens, and a translator, and a lattice in a case on its way out of the building." She was not looking at any of them. "And I have been sitting in this room for four hours with my hands at my sides, and I have not been able to look at any of them, because when I did in September — on the first day, on a lawn, in a corridor — I *had* to, and I did it for four seconds, and then I apologised, and the apology cost somebody something, and I have been thinking about it for a month." She looked up. "And I have not been able to touch anything in this room because there is something in this building that is a *person*, that is not one of the nine, and that is not the machine, and that has been about six feet from me since I came down at half past twelve, and I want somebody to tell me whether I am mad."

"Where," said Lyra.

"There," said Zara, and pointed, without looking, at a corner of the floor about two feet from the base of the translator, where there was a mats' edge, and a rolled blanket, and a girl of fifteen.

## THE FIFTEEN-YEAR-OLD

She had been on the tenth mat, folded under a blanket, and Lyra had walked past her four times.

The observers had not seen her. Zara had not explained why. Nobody had.

Her name was Elara Vance and she was fifteen and she had been in a lattice since the eleventh of September, which was twenty days, and she had grown into the substrate of it the way a hand grows into a glove, and she could not be moved without killing her, and she had said *no* to a question asked through a circuit by a girl of sixteen at four hours in the morning on the twenty-ninth, and the *no* had been the only answer available to her, and Lyra had honoured it.

She was conscious. That was the thing nobody had been ready for. She was conscious and she had her eyes open and she was looking at the ceiling of a machine room, and when four people knelt down beside her she did not scream, and she did not say anything at all, and what she did instead was hold up a hand with the palm out, in a gesture that four people in that room recognised and that the nine observers did not.

*Ask. But not yet. I know you have questions and I have had four days to work out which ones matter.*

"Elara," said Lyra.

The hand came down about a centimetre.

"Vance," said Zara, very quietly. "First-year. She was in the Clinic on the eleventh. I taught her breathing in March."

"Zara."

"I know." Zara's voice was entirely level. "I have known since the twelfth of September and I have not said it, and I have been carrying it for nineteen days, and I would like it minuted that I have been carrying it alone and that I have done it *well*, and that I would like it struck out of the record as an achievement, because it is not one."

---

Nobody asked her anything for an hour.

That was Lyra's decision and it cost her two separate arguments, and she won the first one against Eli, who wanted to know whether she was in pain, and she lost the second one to Wei Chen, who pointed out, without any particular weight, that a person who has been in a substrate for twenty days may be in a great deal of pain and that the ethical question was not whether to ask.

So they asked one question. Lyra asked it, and she asked it badly, and Elara Vance answered it by moving one finger, which she had done on Sunday, and which the circuit had carried as a *no*.

"Did you say no to me because you meant it, or because there was no other answer?"

Three seconds. The finger went up and came down.

It meant it.

"Right," said Lyra, and her own voice cracked in the middle of the word and she did not stop and did not apologise, and it was the first time in a month that she had not apologised for anything in front of any of them, and Zara Washington put a hand flat on the floor next to her, and did not touch her, and did not say she was sorry.

## THE CHANNEL

At ten to seven in the morning, on the floor of Sub-level 7, with four wardens and nine Council observers and a kettle that had been boiled hours ago, Elijah Park found the second thing.

"There's a route into the building that isn't a door," he said, from the cable tray, with a torch in his teeth. "And it isn't a wire either. Lyra, come and look at this, and everybody else stay exactly where you are, because I have got a piece of paper and a torch and I have about nine minutes before somebody in a uniform comes down here and asks me why I am three metres up a wall."

It was a plate on the wall behind the lockers, about the size of a hardback book, painted the same colour as the concrete, and it came off in one piece because it was not fixed to the wall, it was sitting about four millimetres proud of it on a layer of something that had been poured and had not set.

"It's a panel," Eli said. "It's *recently* poured. That is not eighty years old. That is about six weeks old."

"How can you tell?"

"Because it hasn't gone yellow." He turned round. "And because there's a hinge on the far side and a thumb catch, and I have been reading documents all my life, and that is a *hatch*."

Lyra put her hand flat on it. And she could feel it, which was the new thing, which was the thing she had spent a fortnight learning to be suspicious of: a room's worth of attention, pressed against a door, and behind the door nothing at all, and underneath the nothing all the way down, sixty feet, a *warmth*.

"There's something in the wall," she said. "And it is not a machine, and it is not people. It is — " and she stopped, and worked, and did not like what she arrived at, and said it anyway, because she had promised in a corridor in the rain to say the real number out loud. "It is a *collection*. It is a room full of objects and every single one of them has been handled by somebody who cared about it."

Zara had gone white.

"That's the Sanctuary," she said.

"That is not a word I know," said Mila Rowntree, from the back, in the voice of a nineteen-year-old who was supposed to be the advocate and who had been sitting silently for four hours.

## THE REFUSAL

They got it open at ten past seven.

It was not a room. It was a cabinet, and it had been built into a cast in a wall in 1974 by somebody who had known what they were doing and had no intention of it being found, and it was eleven inches deep and four feet wide and it had shelves.

It was full of things. That was the whole of Lyra's first impression and it stayed the whole of her first impression for the rest of her life: a concrete box in a wall under a machine, with four feet of shelf, and every square inch of it taken.

A ball of red silk, wound and re-wound into a shape like a closed fist.

A comb of horn with the teeth gone, in a child's size.

A jar with a cloth over it and something inside it that Lyra could not identify and did not want to.

Forty-one small objects, and about nine documents, and every single one of the objects had a person's hand on it, and Lyra Chen stood in front of a shelf under a mountain and cried for the first time since the fifteenth of September, with no particular sound at all, and Eli Park watched her do it and did not say one word, which was the single most useful thing anybody did for her that month.

"Protocol Kheredu," said Zara Washington, from somewhere behind her, and it was the first time Lyra had ever heard her sound like that. "It's a real list. It's seven items and the first three are gone, and it's been real since nineteen ninety-one, and there is somebody in this building who has been the only person keeping it for two years, and I have known them for four years, and I did not know."

"Who."

"Uncle Tobias," said Elena Vance from beside the cabinet.

Nobody had been watching the door.

That was the part that mattered afterwards, and it was the part that made the record, and it was this: at seven fourteen in the morning, on Tuesday the first of October, in the machine room of Sub-level 7, a security officer came down the stairs — and did not come in — and stood in the doorway for about nine seconds, and then went back up, and did not report, for eleven hours, that the machine room had contained a person who was not on any list.

Her name was Mirembe Blackwood. She was the Security Chief of Threadweaver Academy. She had signed off the transfer that had put Marcus Harlow in a locked ward sixteen days before, which was a thing she had done in order to keep him off the Council's list, which was a thing she had done because of a form, which was a thing that had been her entire life for four years.

And on Tuesday the first of October at seven fourteen in the morning, standing in a doorway, having heard the name Vance spoken aloud in a room she had ordered sealed, she had understood in about two seconds that the Sanctuary was open, and that a Council advocate was in the room, and that the advocate was nineteen years old and had been appointed on Friday by a registrar who could not sign for anything, and that if she reported it then the object would be sealed and the list would be impounded and the four of the seven would be entered as evidence by an institution, and everything she had done for four years would have been done *by* the institution, and nothing in it would ever have been returned.

So she went up the stairs, and she shut the door, and she stood on the other side of it in the dark for about a minute and a half.

And then she went to her office, and she took out a form, and she wrote, on the reverse of a request for the return of property belonging to a student who had never collected it, the following:

> *Kheredu-Seven: four located, one located and in the room, two outstanding. Do not enter. Do not inventory. I will hold the door until the advocate is appointed properly and the thing is done in a room with witnesses and a form, or I will hold it until I am relieved, whichever is later. Relieve me or do not. Either is a decision and I will live with it.*
>
> *M.B.*

She sent it to one person, who was not on the Council, and who had been, for sixteen days, in a locked ward on Sub-level 2, and who was not permitted to send anything and would therefore have to do something much worse, which was to have it memorised by a nineteen-year-old advocate and said out loud on the record.
