*Monday 14 October.*

## THE ROOM

The room was on the ground floor of the old observatory and it had a window, and the window was the reason they had put it there, and everybody who came into it looked at the window for a while and then had to be made to stop.

There were nine chairs in a circle and a table with water on it and a whiteboard, and the whiteboard had already been written on and erased and written on again, and the second set of writing was still visible underneath the first if you stood at an angle, which nobody had thought about, and which was the single most accurate piece of criticism anybody made of the Council in the following four years.

Lyra sat in the ninth chair. She had asked for the ninth chair and had been given the ninth chair and had been given it without a word, and she noticed, and she wrote on the back of her hand in pencil and rubbed out again four days later because she was not going to be a person with a ritual.

The Inquiry into the Conduit sat at eleven o'clock on a Monday morning in October, and it sat on nine weekdays and it did not sit on the weekend, and it had a name and a chair and a clerk and no legal counsel, because the Chair of the Council had decided in a corridor on the Saturday that a thing with a *lawyer* in it would be a trial, and that this was not a trial, and that he was the wrong man for it and had said so.

He was the wrong man for it. He sat in the tenth chair, which had been added on the Monday morning, and did not speak for two hours, and got the last word at four o'clock by standing up and saying "I have nothing" and sitting down again, and it was the single most difficult thing anybody watched him do that year.

---

Dr Amara Osei had a board. Not a whiteboard — a board, a thing on a wall, with a thing she had drawn on it, and she had drawn it before she knew whether she was allowed to.

Three colours. She had used three colours for a week and everybody in the room understood what they were, and nobody said so, because Osei had told them on the first morning, in the flattest voice anybody had ever heard in that building: *the colours are not a ranking. There is no ranking. The colours are: still here; altered; gone. I have used the word gone about nine people and I have put them in the fourth colour and I would like that recorded, because the fourth colour is not for people who are worse off. It is for people whom I have stopped being able to predict, and I would like somebody in this room to understand that those are not the same and that my colleagues in eleven years will say that they are.*

"Twenty-three names," she said. "Of one hundred and ninety-eight. That is the honest number and I would like it said first, before the speeches.

"Fourteen have recovered to a stable perceptive state. Not to *their* perceptive state. To one they do not have, that they did not have before, and which in nine cases will not be stable, and in three cases I cannot model at all, and I have a note about each and I will read the note if anybody asks and I will not volunteer them because you do not need my terror in this room, you need my arithmetic.

"Three have total loss. Total. Not partial, not reduced: a room they can see and nothing in it.

"Two have a cochlear bleed and will heal, and I am including them because otherwise the only thing anybody in this room will talk about is thread loss and I have watched what a building does when it has only one noun.

"Four are in the second colour and I do not know what they are. I have a girl who reads emotion now, properly, in a two-mile radius, and she cannot turn it off, and she is sixteen, and she has asked me for something to stop it and I have got nothing, and I have been telling her since the fifteenth of September that I have got nothing, and I would like it recorded that the honest answer and the kind answer are the same answer in this case and that is a coincidence I do not expect to be granted twice.

"And then there is the fourth colour." Osei put her thumb on the board. "Three people. Ms. Sandoval has not opened her eyes since the tenth of September. There is a boy in a ward in another state who was taken off this campus in a car on the sixteenth with a signature from this institution and a label that said *specialist programme* and whose family has only been told, in a letter, that he is receiving specialist care. And there is a girl of fifteen who went into a lattice on the eleventh of September on a card she signed herself and who came out of it on the second of October into a room with a meter and a warden's name on it, and who is alive, and who is in Bellhaven, and whose mother has been told on the telephone by a Registrar and not by the man who did it."

"And you want the last one minuted," said the Chair.

"I want the last one minuted *first*," said Osei, "before anybody in this room says anything about the fourth colour, and I want it minuted with a time on it, and I want it read out to Ms. Chen's aunt in front of the girl at some point, because I have read the file and it took four minutes and a girl of fifteen to produce a change in this Academy's behaviour that the entire institutional apparatus of the Council failed to produce in eleven years, and I would like the record to say *four minutes*, so that the next person in this building who is frightened has a figure."

## THE LIST

They did the list on the first day and it took five hours and it was the worst afternoon of Lyra Chen's life, and she had spent a month having worse afternoons, and she noticed the absurdity of that at about four o'clock and it made her laugh, once, out loud, in a room of nine chairs, and everybody looked at her and she apologised and then did not apologise, and that was the first time the room noticed anything about her other than that she was sixteen and had lost a gift.

"Read them," said the Chair. "Not the outcomes. The *names*. Out loud. Because the first thing this building does with a name is put it in a column."

They read them out loud.

Three of them were sitting in the room.

"Twenty-three," said the Chair, when they had finished. "Read again tomorrow, and the day after, until the twenty-fourth of October, and then read it to the Council on the twenty-ninth, and then read it every term for as long as this Academy exists, in a room with a window, at eleven o'clock in the morning. I would like it entered that I do not know whether that is a punishment or a service and that I have decided to do it anyway because it is the only thing in this building I can think of that cannot be quietly dropped."

## THE ADMISSION

He came at two o'clock on the Thursday.

He came with two wardens and a lawyer the Council had appointed for him against his instruction, because he had said, on the first morning, that he did not want one, and the Chair had overruled him in a corridor in eleven seconds, and had minuted the overrule, which was the second thing in eleven days that had gone into a minute that nobody expected.

The lawyer was there for about twenty minutes and did not use it.

"She should have stayed in Bellhaven," said the lawyer, on the way out, to nobody. "There is no reason on earth for her to be in that building."

"There is no reason on earth for any of them to have been in that building," said the Chair. "That is rather the point of the room."

---

It is not a long speech. It is four sentences and it took about ninety seconds and it is in the transcript in full, and it is on the fifth board in this building, in a Registrar's hand, because somebody in that room had the sense to ask for it to be written where students could read it and was told that this was not what the fifth board was for and was then overruled in a corridor in about four seconds.

"I built it.

"I did not know about the housing, and that is a defence, and I am not going to offer it, and I want it in the transcript that I declined to offer it, because a man who declines to offer his own defence to an inquiry has understood something about inquiries.

"I signed a card on the eleventh of September without reading it, and there is a drawer in this building with my name on it, and the card says *volunteer* in my hand, and it does not say *she was fifteen*, and every one of those four things was true and available to me, and I did not write them down because if I had written them down I would have had to hand the drawer in.

"And I do not know what the lattice is and I have had four weeks in a good room to work out whether I want to know, and I have arrived at yes, and I would like to be asked, and I would like to be asked by somebody who is not going to be kind to me about the answer."

He sat down.

"Mr. Harlow," said the Chair. "You have four minutes. Is that a condition?"

"That is a request."

"It is a condition, and I am granting it, and I would like the transcript to show that I granted it in four seconds, which is faster than I granted it to his lawyer on Monday, and that I would like everybody in this room to draw whatever conclusion they like about that."

---

They asked him. Not Lyra — the Chair asked him, and it was the Chair's question and the Chair's voice, and the transcript records that Lyra Chen was asked twice whether she wished to put a question and declined both times and gave a reason each time, and the reasons were *it is not mine* and *I am the reason, and I have been the reason for four weeks, and if I stand up in this room and ask a man a question then this room becomes about me*, and the Clerk wrote both reasons down in full.

He answered for two hours and eleven minutes.

He told them about 1941 and about a man who had built a cage and put his dying brother in it and died in a bed eleven months later and had sealed the housing and left a note saying that he had done a great and necessary thing and that he was sorry.

He told them about April, and a garden, and a seven-year-old walking towards something, and he said it in the flat voice of a man reading a report, and the transcript records that four people in that room stopped breathing and that one of them was the Chair.

He told them about Aiko, and that she had died of it in 1999, and said the word *fourteen years* and did not use it as an excuse, and was asked about it, and said: *no, and I would like that in the transcript too, because I have had fourteen years and I have used it as a reason approximately nine thousand times, and the last time I used it was on the twenty-seventh of September, in a demonstration hall, to two hundred people, and it is a very good reason and it is a *queue*, and I have been taught that word by a girl of nineteen and I would like the record to show that I did not have it before the second of October.*

He told them about his daughter, who was twenty-three, and about a restraining order signed in 2011, and about eleven letters in a drawer in a room on Sub-level 2, of which he had sent one.

"May I read the letter," said the Chair.

"No," said Harlow. "You may not. It is eleven lines and it is addressed to a person who has not written to me since 1999 and has no obligation to, ever, and if it is read into a record in this building then it becomes a document about me, and it is not a document about me."

"That is the first refusal anybody has made in this room that I have believed on the first hearing," said the Chair, "and I am going to observe that it is a refusal to *publish*, and that it is the only good-faith version of this entire business that I have seen, and that I have been waiting four days to see somebody make one."

---

They broke at half past four and Lyra went out into the corridor and sat down on the floor, which she had done so often in October that she had a favourite.

Zara Washington found her.

"He is not a monster," said Zara, sitting down on the floor beside her, which was a thing that a girl who had been raised in a house where you did not sit on floors had worked on for a month.

"I know."

"That is not what I was going to say. I was going to say: *he is not the thing, and I have known that since the fifteenth of September, and I said it out loud in a hospital bed on the twenty-eighth and Eli nearly took my head off, and I have had three weeks to find out whether I was right and I was right and it is the most useless thing I have ever been right about.*"

"He has eleven months," said Lyra.

"He has eleven months and a letter and a room with a panel in it showing a valley in the wrong weather, and yes." Zara put her hands on her knees. "Lyra. I want to say a thing and I want you to know I have thought about it for three weeks and I have not said it to a single person, including the one I live with.

"The reason I have not been able to say it is Milo."

"Milo."

"He has been *magnificent* about all of this." Zara's voice did not go up. "He has been in a chair with a stick and he has done a hundred and forty hours of corridor work and he has organised a boy of fifteen's entire legal representation, and he has done it in a way that has made everybody in this building look at him the way you look at a *saint*, and I have watched eleven adults do it, and I have watched one boy of sixteen *let* them."

"Milo—"

"And on the fourth evening of the Inquiry he came back to our corridor and he said, in the voice he has been using since the twenty-eighth of September, *it's fine, I'm good, don't* — " she stopped and started again. "And I got angry, Lyra. I got so angry that I had to go and stand in a stairwell. Because he is doing the thing you did, except he is better at it, and I do not know how to tell him that I have *noticed*, and I have been the person closest to him for three weeks and I have not managed it, and if you do not manage it then nobody will, and I have been carrying that for six days and I have got a fifth board to think about and I am *telling* you because you are the only person in this building who will understand what I am actually saying."

Lyra sat on the floor of a corridor in an observatory and put her hand, quite deliberately, on the back of Zara Washington's hand, and asked, and was answered.

"That is exactly the right thing to do," she said. "Zara, that is exactly the right thing to do, and it is going to be extremely hard, and I am going to help you do it, and we are going to do it on Thursday, and we are going to do it in front of the other two, and Eli is going to be *appalling* about it, and it is going to be the single best afternoon of my entire life."
