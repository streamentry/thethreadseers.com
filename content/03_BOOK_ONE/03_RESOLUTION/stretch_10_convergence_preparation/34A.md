*Wednesday 6 November.*

## WHAT A BUILDING LOOKS LIKE AFTER

Nobody had told Lyra what a day like this was like, and she had spent a month assuming there would be something to look at.

There was a great deal to look at. There was a queue at the registrar's desk from seven in the morning. There were two hundred and eleven people at the Nexus Concord at nine o'clock in the evening. There was a queue outside the room where the register of case forty-one was open to the public, and it was a queue of people who had come to read thirty-nine names.

"What are you all doing here?" asked Lyra Chen, to a man of about sixty in a car park, on the Thursday, genuinely, because she could not work it out.

"Reading the names," he said.

"Have you got somebody in there?"

"No." He looked slightly embarrassed, in the way of a man who has arrived at a building with a purpose and has been told it is not the purpose. "My wife's aunt. 1949. She was — nobody ever told anybody."

That was nine times on the first day, and Lyra counted, because she had not yet worked out that she was allowed to stop counting, and a man from a village four hundred miles away told her on the second day that she was the first person he had spoken to about it in sixty-four years and that he had got in his car and driven two hours for it, and that he was going to have to tell his wife, and that his wife did not know either.

The register is public. It is a bound book on a table in a room with a window, and there is a clerk, and anybody may read it, and there is a *second* book beside it in which a visitor may write — not names, not grievances, but the *details* that were lost: a date somebody remembered wrongly, a village nobody had written down, the name of a plant, a song.

"Eleven hundred and six entries by December," said the Registrar's deputy, in a report nobody asked for. "Nine hundred of them are wrong. That is not a criticism of the visitors. That is the finding. *The Academy's records for 1941 to 1953 are not merely incomplete, they are wrong in nine hundred places, and every one of the nine hundred is a place, and the record was written by people who had never been to the places.*"

## THE REPAIRS

There were nine, and they were all dull, and Eleanor Weaver gave them to a reporter in a corridor in about four minutes and then spent four months being disliked for them.

One: the schedules. Published. Weekly, in the same place, with the same format, whether or not there is anything to say, and a schedule that says *nothing this week* is a schedule.

Two: the Act. The Authority for the Care of Persons of Perceived Thread, section nine — the section that has been in force since 1974 and that has, in forty years, been observed eleven times out of eleven opportunities, in a building, in a demonstration hall, in front of sixty-one people, with no second responder and a rug — was amended in a fortnight. It is a short amendment. It requires a named second responder at any outreach; it requires the second responder to be able to say *no* to the first; and it requires the word *no* to be recorded whether or not it is subsequently reversed, which is the entire of it and which four hundred people had argued for in eleven years and which was got in a fortnight by a girl with a folder and a woman with a telephone.

Three: the Council. Yuen removed from the Council, and from the 2009 working group, and from every committee touching human subjects, and appointed, at his own request and over the objection of four members, to a body that reviews *appeals*, which has no powers whatever and meets in a room with a bad chair. He asked for it and the Chair said no and Yuen asked again in writing and the Chair, who is that man, granted it, and minuted that he had refused once first, so that the record showed that the request had not been a formality.

Four: the injury fund. Not named for anybody. Forty-one thousand two hundred and eleven accounts, opened in nine days, from an institution that has a *five-year renewable charter* and has had one for sixty years, and every renewal of which was signed without discussion. It is not a fund for the twenty-three. It is a fund for *whoever is next*, and Dr. Osei's single most quoted sentence in the next decade is in a footnote on page nine of a report and reads:

> *I have been a physician here for eleven years and I have treated four hundred and eleven people for a condition caused by the fact that somebody in this building was near them with a number. The fund does not treat anybody. It buys time. Time is what we did not have on the twenty-seventh of September and I would like somebody to remember, when they are tempted to make it a good press release, that the thing that was missing was not money.*

Five: the Sanctuary. Opened. Formally, publicly, in the room with the window, on the eleventh of October, by the Registrar, with a Security Chief two hundred miles away by video link, at her own insistence and on the Registrar's condition, that she had to be talked into using. Four of the seven items in Protocol Kheredu-Seven have gone back. Three are disputed and are going to a hearing in a country Lyra has never been to, and the Registrar has been made a party to it by a lawyer from that country who has read four pages of the register and then written a letter saying, *you have kept a hundred names and no stories, and we will give you the stories, and some of them will be difficult, and you will have to sit in a room with people who are not going to forgive you for seventy years, and you have to go anyway.*

Four hundred and nine people came to the opening. Two hundred and eleven to the Concord. And a man from a village four hundred miles away came twice, and left four entries in the second book, and the third of them was the name of his wife's aunt, which was not in the first book, and which is now in every copy.

Six: the Maze. The archway. Rebuilt, and it is the only thing in this book that anybody has ever got wrong twice, and it went wrong again in a way that is worth recording because the record is the point: the 2014 archway, built under Weaver's own instruction, put the Chinese knots back exactly as they had been in 1954, because that was what the 1954 drawings showed, and a first-year from the third week's course went and looked at it, and then went and looked at the left-hand post, and then found a woman of ninety in a care home in the next valley who had made the 1941 version out of silk and had kept it in a tin.

She had kept it in a tin for seventy-two years. It was four inches of silk and it took eleven months to make and it was *wrong on purpose*, in three places, and the third wrongness was the loose knot on the left-hand post, and when they asked her about it she said:

"My husband did that. In nineteen forty-one, with a knife, while he was making the other one, because he said the second one had come out too perfect and he was frightened of it. And then he died, and I kept the wrong one, and I have thought about that for seventy-two years, and I would like somebody to write down that a mistake made by a frightened man in one hour survived two wars and an institution and a demolition order and it is the only thing I have ever made that anybody looked at for seventy years."

The left-hand post of the archway of Threadweaver Academy is loose. It is deliberately, provably, documented loose. It is on the maintenance schedule. It has been on the maintenance schedule since 2014 and it is the single most visited object in the building.

Seven: the transcript. The entire Inquiry record, all nine days, published in full after the final sitting, including the four hundred and eleven pages that nobody had wanted. Four thousand copies went out in the first week, to every family on the board, and to every academy in the Council's list, and to eleven countries on request, and it has been required reading in four teacher-training courses and it is out of print and reprinted twice and it is, according to a survey in 2019, the second-most-cited document in the history of the Council after the 1974 Act, and it is a document about a machine, and a rug, and a card nobody read.

Eight, nine, ten, eleven: there were eleven, and by the end of the year there were nineteen, and by 2016 there were forty, and the Chair of the Council, who was then a different man, put the number on a wall in the vestibule of the Lumina Hall with no other explanation, and it is the only number in that building that is not a severity, and it is the one that visitors photograph.

## THE CONCORD

*Wednesday 6 November, nine o'clock. The Great Loom Chamber.*

They did it in the evening, which nobody expected, and in the Loom, which everybody did.

"There is a thing this institution is *for*," said Eleanor Weaver, "and it is not on a plaque anywhere in this building, and I have been Headmistress for six years and I have used the phrase *academic standards* four hundred times and I have never once used this one, and I am going to use it now and then I am going to hand the floor to somebody who has been in this valley for nine days and has not been to a staff meeting.

"The Loom is a nexus anchor. It holds this valley's share of the network. It has been holding it since nineteen forty-one and before that since the eighteen-seventies, and it has been holding it in the same way for a hundred and forty years, which is to say: alone, and at a cost, and with a lid on it.

"Two months ago something in this building did the arithmetic of a hundred and forty years in about ninety seconds, in a demonstration hall, and got it wrong by fourteen per cent, and there were four hundred people in the room. We are not going to be able to unlearn that. The only thing available is to hold it *differently*, and to be honest that we do not know what differently means yet, and to ask."

---

The first person to speak was an old woman in a wheelchair who had been at the back and had asked for no microphone, and who was introduced only as *Bà Sáu*, and who was eighty-four, and who had come from a house on a street in a city four thousand miles away, and who had not been asked to come and had come anyway, at enormous cost, in a wheelchair, through an airport, with a woman of nineteen who had held the door for her.

"There is no loom in my tradition," she said, in English, slowly, and the room understood every word of it and about half of the meaning, and there was a granddaughter beside her who translated the other half and would not be asked to translate it again.

"There are currents. There have always been currents, under this street and every street my family has lived on, and the oldest of them is under a market in a place you have never heard of, and it is not a *loom*. A loom is a machine that makes something. What is under the market is a *belly*. It is not making anything. It is carrying, and it has been carrying since before anybody here had a word for the carrying.

"Your Loom has been carrying alone for a hundred and forty years because somebody, in nineteen forty-one, made a lid. And your Loom is not *ill*. It is *load-bearing*, and there has never been enough of it in this room to say so out loud, and that is my whole contribution, and I am going to sit down now."

They did it in eleven parts and it took four hours and ten minutes, and Lyra was the last, and she was sixteen, and the Chair had to explain to the room who she was, and did it badly and apologised for it afterwards.

She did not describe a technique. There is no technique. She put her hand on a stone that had been part of the archway since 1781 and that was, as of 2014, on a maintenance schedule for being deliberately loose, and she said four things.

"One. I cannot see threads any more. I want that said first, every time, in every room, for the rest of my life, and I want it said by somebody else as well, and I would like it on the Concord record that the person who is doing this cannot do the thing she learned to do when she was six, and that she is doing it anyway, and that this is not inspiring and should not be reported as inspiring.

"Two. The current under this building is carrying about sixty per cent of a load and the other forty is on a bench with a warder's name on it, and eleven of us know that and nine hundred and ninety-nine other people in this valley do not, and we are going to keep that on a board, and that is the whole of the institutional part of this evening.

"Three. I am not going to say anything about communion. Everybody in this room has been using the word for two months and it has been used in a great many ways and about half of them have been ways of not saying a number. My mother wrote it in a notebook and then wrote, four lines later, *a system that requires a payment from someone who has not agreed is not a system, it is a queue.* Both sentences are hers and they are four lines apart and the four lines are the argument, and I would like the argument entered and the word left where it is, on a wall, being useful, meaning five different things to five different people including me.

"Four. There is a woman of eighty-four who came four thousand miles and has just told us that our central organ is a *belly* and not a *loom*, and that she is right, and that I have spent a month being taught that by a fifteen-year-old, and that I am not going to credit myself for it."

---

The Loom settled at about eleven o'clock. There is no other word and everybody who was there used it.

Osei, afterwards, to a reporter who was standing on the steps and who was clearly going to print the word *miracle*: "It went down nine per cent and it stayed down for four hours and then it went up one, which is within tolerance, and which is what I would have predicted, and which I predicted at four o'clock that afternoon in a corridor, and which nobody wrote down at the time, and which I have been slightly bitter about for a month and have now stopped being bitter about, because a miracle is not a miracle, it is a *measurement we did not make in time*, and if this building learns one thing from me in the next forty years I would like it to be that sentence, and I would like it on a board, and Eli Park can have the job."

## THE FOUR OF THEM

They did it in an anteroom, at half past midnight, because four teenagers who had not been in a room together without a crisis since September found that they did not know how to have a conversation any more and had to be told.

"Thirty per cent of our year group did not come back from August," Eli Park said. "That is a fact. It is a fact that will be on a form somewhere, and I have been avoiding it for four days, and I would like to say it and then I would like to talk about something else for a while."

Nobody argued with him about it. Zara put her hand flat on the table in a way that was the whole of her entire personality.

"Alexandria," said Zara Washington. "My mother's mother. I am not going to study *empathic resonance* — she has a word for it that has no English in it and I refuse to write it down in a Latin alphabet. I want to learn the thing that she learned, which is a set of household practices for *attending* to a person, and they have a name, and the name is not a discipline, and every programme in this building that I have been offered in the last three weeks is a discipline." She looked at the table. "And I want a word for what I can do. Because I can read the emotional weather of a two-mile radius and I cannot switch it off and Dr. Osei has got nothing, and I am going to spend my twenties learning to live inside a thing that is going to be there when I am forty, and I would like somebody to have a *name* for it that is not *complication*."

"Lyon," said Eli Park. "A formal place, a real supervisor, a two-year contract, and access to the Lyon lattice, which is the fourth one in the world, and which is in a car park, and which is the only one of the four that is still — " he stopped. "Which is still *in a car park*. Which is a thing I did not know was true until October and which I think about four times a day."

"And?"

"And when I get there I want to write it up," said Eli Park. "Not the machine. The *question*. Eleven people in this room have been asked to describe what they want in the next four years and nine of them said a place and two of them said a *method*, and I have noticed that the two who said a method are the two who have been given the least. I would like somebody to write up the noticing."

---

Milo Reyes did not say anything for a while.

"I am going to be an ordinary musician," he said. "I have made my peace with it and I worked it out on the fourteenth of October in about eleven minutes, which everybody thinks was a long time and was actually the fastest decision I have ever made, and I want it recorded that it was fast, because people keep giving me a *look* about it, as though I had chewed on a piano, and I have not, and I am not going to."

"That is not a decision," said Eli Park. "That is a *fact about you*. I am not being cruel. I am saying that those are different sentences and that since the first week of term I have watched about forty people write the second one down when they meant the first, including two adults who sit on a Council."

"Then here is the first sentence," said Milo Reyes. "I am going to be an ordinary musician, *and* I can still feel a room. Not hear it. Feel it. Which is not what it was and is not nothing, and it costs me about a headache every four hours and a stick, and I have been putting it in a log, and I have got fifty-two days of it, and I have got a *pattern*, and the pattern is that I am only good at it for about eleven minutes at a time and then I go stupid.

"And I want somebody to tell me what happens in the twelfth minute. Because *somebody* is going to write that down one day, and it is going to be in a drawer, and there is going to be a number on it." He looked round. "That is why I am telling you *now*, while I am sixteen, and while there is no instrument at all. Put it in the log. Put it on the board. There is no girl in a hole in this one. There is just a boy on a stick with a notebook and eleven minutes and a question, and I would like it entered that I raised it *myself*, in advance, and that nobody made me."

They wrote it down. All four of them, in four hands, on the back of a form, and it went into the record of the Concord at ten past one in the morning, under item nine, and there is a copy in a file in a building in this valley and there is a copy in a car park in Lyon and there is a copy on a piano in Alexandria and there is a copy in a corridor in a high school in Westbrook where two people who were sixteen in September now teach.

---

Lyra Chen did not say her own thing at half past midnight.

She said it on the fourth of November, in the room with the window, in eleven words, into a recorder, with the Clerk present, and she gave the recording to the Clerk and not to the four of them, and Eli Park did not know about it for six weeks and was extremely angry for about a minute and a half and then was not.

"Do you want me to read it back?" said Mila Rowntree, who had been the Clerk to that Inquiry for nine days and the student advocate for five weeks, and who had by then learned considerably more about minutes than about threads.

"No," said Lyra Chen. "That's the point. Nobody reads it back. That's what you write a minute for."
